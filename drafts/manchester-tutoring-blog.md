# What Manchester parents get wrong about tutoring (and what actually works)

If you live anywhere south of the Mancunian Way and your child is in Year 5, you've probably already heard the word grammar. Maybe at the school gate. Maybe at a dinner party where someone mentioned Altrincham. Maybe in a WhatsApp group that's quietly turned into a 7am revision schedule. The Trafford grammar effect doesn't stay in Trafford. It moves across the postcode line and shapes how families in Didsbury, Chorlton, Whalley Range and Levenshulme think about their kids' education, even when those kids aren't sitting the Trafford test.

That pressure is real. But most families spend it badly.

## The Trafford effect that no-one explains

Trafford is one of eleven local authorities in England that still operates a fully selective grammar-school system at age 11. Altrincham Grammar School for Boys, Altrincham Grammar School for Girls, Sale Grammar and Loreto Grammar are all rated Outstanding by Ofsted. They publish their entry threshold each year and it doesn't move much. The competition isn't a rumour. It's mathematical.

Two things follow from that.

First, the Year 5 reasoning preparation that gets a child through the Trafford 11+ is not the same skill the school day teaches. State primary curricula don't include verbal reasoning, non-verbal reasoning or the kind of timed numerical thinking the test rewards. Families who realise this in October of Year 5 still have time. Families who realise it the week before half term in Year 6 don't.

Second, the grammar question echoes outwards. Even if you've decided your child won't sit the Trafford test, the comprehensive your child does go to is shaped by the fact that a slice of the most academically prepared 10-year-olds across South Manchester are being filtered out into Trafford. That changes the classroom you're left with, the peer group your child works alongside, and the pace at which content gets delivered.

## And then there's the city itself

Across Manchester city as a whole, Progress 8 sits below the national average. That isn't because Manchester children are less capable. It's because secondary classes are large, teacher time is finite, and the gap between a student who quietly slipped two months ago and a student who's keeping up is invisible until November mocks make it impossible to miss.

Most of the GCSE students who come to us in Manchester are not failing. They're somewhere between a 4 and a 6 and they're putting in the work. The problem is that the work isn't landing. They've revised. They've done past papers. The grade hasn't moved.

That's almost never a content problem. It's a where-the-marks-are problem.

## What good tutoring actually does

Good tutoring works for a smaller, more boring reason than most parents expect. For one hour a week, the entire session is built around what your child does and doesn't understand. Not what the class is on. Not what the textbook says next. What your child, in particular, is still getting wrong on the 4-mark questions in Paper 2.

A good Manchester GCSE tutor will spend the first half of the first session not teaching. They'll be reading. They'll look at the last mock paper. They'll look at the homework book. They'll ask the student to explain how they approach a 6-mark question, in their own words. And they'll write down where the marks are leaking.

Usually it's one of three patterns.

There's the foundation-gap pattern: a fractions concept missed in Year 7, an algebra method that never got drilled, a Literature text introduced when the class had already moved past the previous one. The student has been compounding around a gap they don't know is there.

There's the exam-technique pattern: the student knows the material, but can't write it under timed conditions in a way the mark scheme rewards. They lose marks they should have. This is more common at A-Level than GCSE, but it shows up at both.

And there's the confidence-collapse pattern: the student has decided, at 14, that they're not a Maths person, or not a writer, or not someone who does Sciences. They've stopped putting their hand up. They've stopped attempting questions they think they'll get wrong. The grade is a symptom. The belief is the cause.

A tutor fixes each of those patterns differently. But the diagnostic comes first. That's what most school classrooms can't do at the moment of greatest need, because the moment of greatest need is rarely the moment a teacher of 32 can stop.

## What about online?

Manchester is geographically large. Salford, Wythenshawe, Cheadle Hulme, Prestwich, the corridors out towards Stockport and Bury. Finding a specialist tutor in your child's exact subject, exam board and year group within a 15-minute drive is a real constraint. Online tuition removes it.

Sessions run on a shared interactive whiteboard. The student and the tutor work through problems together in real time. For Maths and the Sciences in particular, this is closer to a one-to-one classroom than a video call. Most students take three sessions to be comfortable. By session four, the fact that the tutor is online is the least interesting thing about the arrangement.

Online also makes consistency easier. No travel means sessions can be at 5pm on a Wednesday or 8am on a Saturday. The number of cancelled sessions drops sharply, and consistency is the single biggest predictor of whether the grades will move.

## When parents come to us, and what we wish they'd done sooner

The most common time families in Manchester get in touch is November or January of Year 11, after the first or second set of mocks. The grades have come back. The conditional sixth-form offer is on the line. The student is anxious. The parent is anxious. And the timeline is now four to six months.

It works at that point. We've taken Year 11 students from a predicted 5 in Maths to a 7 by August. We've taken A-Level students from a C in Psychology to an A. But four months is short, and shorter is harder.

The single best thing any Manchester family can do is start earlier. October of Year 10. The summer before Year 11. Year 12, the moment A-Level content stops feeling like a continuation of GCSE. That's when the gap is small and a tutor can close it without the clock pressing.

The second-best thing is to ask harder questions of whoever you hire. Not what subject they teach, but what mark schemes they know cold. Not what university they went to, but which Manchester exam-board specifications they've worked with. Not their hourly rate, but what their students actually went on to score. A good tutor will answer these questions without hesitation.
