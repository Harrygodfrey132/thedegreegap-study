faqs:
  - q: "Which part of GCSE English do Rochdale teenagers struggle with most?"
    a: "Most often it's the Literature essay, more than any single text. Students learn plenty of quotations, then write about each one separately, so the essay explains lines without ever making an argument. The unseen poetry comes a close second, because there's nothing to revise in advance. On the Language papers, the writing task loses marks when nobody plans before starting. A tutor usually picks one of these to start on, based on your child's last mock."
  - q: "Does it matter whether my child's school uses AQA, Edexcel or OCR for English?"
    a: "Yes, mostly in how the papers are put together. Each board groups the Shakespeare play, the 19th-century novel, the modern text and the poetry into its papers in its own way, and the poetry anthologies differ too. The skills overlap a lot, but practising with the wrong board's questions wastes time your child doesn't have. We confirm the board and set texts on the call and match a tutor who's taught them."
  - q: "My child reads all the time. Why is their predicted English grade so low?"
    a: "Usually because English is marked on the quality of written answers under time, and that's hard to see from the outside. A child who reads widely and talks well about books can still write essays that describe instead of argue, or run out of time before the last question. The tutor reads your child's recent papers against the mark scheme and shows you where the marks went, which is often a more hopeful picture than the grade suggests."
  - q: "Will my child's English tutor be a teacher or a university student?"
    a: "Either, depending on the match. Some tutors on our platform are qualified teachers; others are graduates or undergraduates, many from Russell Group universities, and all of them have tutored for at least two years. For a Year 11 studying An Inspector Calls, someone who sat the same paper a few years ago can be a great fit. What we test hardest is whether they can explain a mark scheme in plain words."
  - q: "My daughter insists she's fine at English and won't hear of a tutor. Should we push?"
    a: "Gently, maybe. Teenagers often turn a tutor down because they picture another school lesson, and English can feel personal, since it's their own writing being judged. A free 15-minute video call lets her meet the tutor first, with no commitment. If she tries a couple of lessons and still doesn't want to carry on, you stop, and there's nothing to cancel."
