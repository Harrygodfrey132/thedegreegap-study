  - q: "Is there one part of GCSE English that Hull teenagers tend to dread?"
    a: "The question we're asked about most is the unseen poetry. Your child has never seen the poem, has a short time to read it, and then has to say what the poet is doing rather than what the poem is about. Close behind it comes comparison, where two texts need discussing together rather than one after the other. Both get easier with a method: read twice, find two techniques, link each to the question. A tutor practises that until it's automatic."
  - q: "What will my child actually do in an hour of English tutoring?"
    a: "Write, mostly. The hour usually opens with five minutes on whatever was set last week, then one task done against the clock, maybe a Language question or a single Literature paragraph. Together they compare it with what examiners reward, and before the end your child redrafts the weakest sentence. It feels slower than rereading notes, but redrafting is where most of the progress happens."
  - q: "My child is dyslexic. Can an English tutor still help them write stronger answers?"
    a: "Yes. Mention it when we speak and the shortlist will lean towards tutors with experience of dyslexia. Expect short planning frames, colour to keep ideas apart, and a lot of talking an answer through before a word is written, so the argument is settled before spelling becomes the worry. If your child gets extra time or a reader in exams, timed practice is set up the same way."
  - q: "My child won't even open the set texts. Where do we start?"
    a: "With the shortest way in. Once your child can talk about the text, reading the rest feels far less like a mountain. They'll meet the tutor on a free video call first, so the first lesson isn't with a stranger."
