faqs:
  - q: "What grade do Liverpool sixth forms ask for before A-Level English?"
    a: "Most publish a minimum for the subject itself rather than an overall score, and it sits above the general entry bar. St Edward's College, Liverpool Blue Coat and Calderstones each set and publish their own requirements, and they vary by course and change year to year, so read the current page for the school your child is applying to. The practical point is that the English grade is judged on its own, which is why a student with strong Maths and a middling English can still be turned down for the English A-Level."
  - q: "Why is my child struggling with GCSE English when they read all the time?"
    a: "Reading widely and writing to a mark scheme are different skills, and the second one is rarely taught explicitly. Confident readers often lose marks because they write about what happens rather than how the writer built it, or because they never learned to plan an answer in the first four minutes. In a first session we usually find the student knows more about the text than their marks suggest. The gap is in getting it onto the page in the shape the examiner is looking for."
  - q: "How much English work should my child do between sessions?"
    a: "About 60 to 90 minutes, and for English that is usually one full essay or two timed exam questions rather than more reading. Writing under the clock is the thing that has to be practised, because knowing a text does not automatically mean being able to write about it in 45 minutes. Past that 90 minutes the returns drop off sharply. One properly marked essay a week beats three unmarked ones."
  - q: "Can a GCSE English tutor help a child with dyslexia or processing differences?"
    a: "Yes, and we ask about learning differences when we match so the tutor is chosen with that in mind. Many tutors in our network work regularly with neurodivergent students, and English responds well to explicit structure: sentence stems, a fixed planning method, a paragraph shape the student can lean on when the blank page is the hardest part. Lessonspace helps here too, because a plan can be built visually and saved for the next session. Mention it on the consultation call."
  - q: "What if my child doesn't want a tutor?"
    a: "It's the worry we hear most, and it's usually about the idea of a tutor rather than the reality of one. Students resist before they meet someone, then settle within two or three sessions because it isn't school-shaped: one adult, full attention, nobody else in the room to hear you get it wrong. Your child meets the tutor in a free 15-minute video call before anything is booked. If they genuinely don't want to carry on after a session or two, pause it. That's their call and we won't push."
