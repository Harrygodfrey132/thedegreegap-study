faq_1_q: "The college application goes in before Christmas of Year 11. So when should tutoring start?"
faq_1_a: "Year 10, if you have the choice. The form itself isn't the hard part. What matters is the grade the school's written down by the autumn of Year 11. Every conversation about courses comes back to that number, and it's built from Year 10 work and the first mocks. A tutor who starts in Year 10 has two terms to close a gap before it ends up on a form. Starting in Year 11 still helps, and plenty of families do, but the work narrows to exam technique and past papers. Either way, the tutor's first job is a marked paper, not a new topic."
faq_2_q: "My daughter's school is on a different exam board from her cousin's. Does that change who you suggest?"
faq_2_a: "Yes, and it's the first thing we check. Schools across Stockport use different boards, and each board words its questions in its own way and tests the practicals differently. A tutor who knows your daughter's course is useful from the first ten minutes. One who doesn't will spend the first few lessons learning it, on your time. So on the call we'll ask for the board, and the tier too (Higher or Foundation). If you're not sure, it's usually on the school's website or the front of a recent mock paper."
faq_3_q: "Do the winter mocks matter more in Stockport, with the college application already in?"
faq_3_a: "They matter, just not in the way most parents fear. A college place depends on the real results in August, so a poor mock doesn't lose it. What a mock does is show where the marks are going before the real exam does. The tutor goes through the marked paper with your child question by question, sorting the questions they couldn't do from the ones they could do but dropped marks on anyway, then plans the spring around the bigger pile. A mock that goes badly in December and gets read properly is worth more than one that goes fine and gets filed away."
faq_4_q: "He's fallen a long way behind. Is GCSE tutoring still worth it this late?"
faq_4_a: "Yes, but the plan looks different from one for a child who's two marks off a grade. The tutor goes back to where the understanding stopped, often Year 8 or 9 rather than this term, and rebuilds from there instead of racing through the current topic. That sounds slower. It's usually quicker, because he's seen the material once already and it comes back fast once the missing step is in place. A 4 in Maths and English opens most college courses in the borough. Nobody can promise it, but a focused term of weekly lessons gives him a real chance of getting there."
