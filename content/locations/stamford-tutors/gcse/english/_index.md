faqs:
  - q: "My child can spot every technique in an extract. Why isn't that enough for the higher marks?"
    a: "Because spotting is only the start. Plenty of children name a technique in an extract or a poem and stop there, without saying what it does to the reader, and that's where the higher marks sit. Comparison questions catch people out too, when two texts get written about one after the other instead of side by side. Then there's the time limit. A Stamford tutor works on all of this with timed GCSE English questions from your child's own board, so the explaining gets quicker as well as deeper."
  - q: "The school's predicted my child a 4 in English. Is that fixed?"
    a: "No. A predicted grade is the school's best estimate from the work it's seen so far, and the work can change. In English, a lot of marks come down to habits, like explaining rather than describing, or planning before writing, and habits respond to practice. We'd never promise a particular grade. What a tutor can do is find which habits cost marks in the mock and work on those, using papers from your child's own board. If a sixth form place hangs on a 4 or a 5 in English Language, it's worth starting sooner rather than later."
  - q: "How much English work should my child do between lessons?"
    a: "Less than you might fear. An hour or so a week is plenty for most children, perhaps one timed answer and a few quotations to learn. More than that tends to bring less back, especially in Year 11 when every subject wants a slice of the evening. The tutor sets it at the end of each lesson and goes through it at the start of the next, so nothing your child writes goes unread."
  - q: "My child is dyslexic and dreads the long English answers. What would a tutor do differently?"
    a: "Quite a lot, starting with the pace. When we speak, we'll want to know what your child finds hardest, whether that's reading an unseen extract quickly or getting ideas into order, and suggest Stamford GCSE English tutors who've worked with dyslexic teenagers before. In lessons, a long answer gets planned as a handful of short points first, so the writing has a frame to hang on. If extra time or other exam arrangements aren't in place yet, it's worth asking the school, since it's the school that applies for them. And because every lesson is recorded, nothing has to be caught first time."
  - q: "Can we do English tutoring on a tight budget?"
    a: "Yes, with a bit of planning. One lesson a week is enough for most children, and some families start in Year 10 with a short run of lessons on one weak area, like the Language writing task, then stop. Others save a few extra lessons for the weeks before the mocks. You pay lesson by lesson from £37 an hour, with no contract, so you're never paying for lessons you don't use."
