    a: "Progress 8 across Brighton and Hove has historically sat below the national average. That is an area-level measure, not a judgement on any school or any child, and what it tends to mean in practice is that a number of capable students arrive in Year 10 without having been taught the mechanics of an examinable essay. That is a teaching gap rather than an ability gap, and it is the kind that one-to-one closes quickly, because it is a small number of specific techniques rather than a huge body of missing knowledge."
  - q: "Why is my child struggling with GCSE English?"
    a: "Most often because nobody ever taught them to plan. English is the subject where a student can work hard for years without being shown the actual shape of a high-scoring paragraph, and reading more does not supply it. The other common cause is quote recall in a closed-book exam, where a student who understands a play completely cannot get at the evidence under pressure. Both look like weak analysis on a mark sheet and neither is. The first session is spent working out which one is in front of us."
  - q: "Can an English tutor help a child with dyslexia or processing differences?"
    a: "Yes, and we ask about learning differences when matching rather than waiting for a parent to bring it up. Several tutors work regularly with neurodivergent students. Online helps more than families expect. The whiteboard keeps working visible instead of wiping it away, explanations can be chunked at the student's own pace, and because every session is recorded nothing has to be held in memory first time round. Mention it on the consultation call and it shapes who we suggest."
  - q: "What happens if the tutor is not the right fit?"

This comes up in English more than in most subjects, because the work asks a teenager to share writing they feel exposed by, and a tutor who is right for one fifteen-year-old can be wrong for the next.
