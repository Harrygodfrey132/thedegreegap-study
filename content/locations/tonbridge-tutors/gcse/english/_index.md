faqs:
  - q: "Where do Tonbridge teenagers usually lose marks in GCSE English?"
    a: "The parts you can't simply revise. Unseen poetry and the Language reading questions use texts your child hasn't seen before, so they need a method rather than notes. Literature essays are written from memory, against a clock that often runs out before the last one's finished. The Language writing task, which comes last, tends to get whatever time is left. And at Tonbridge sixth forms like Judd and Hillview, it's the Language grade that gets checked, so these are worth getting right."
  - q: "Does it matter which exam board my child's school uses for GCSE English?"
    a: "Yes, a lot. AQA, Edexcel and OCR set different papers, and for Literature each school picks its own novel, play and set of poems. Tonbridge schools use a mix of boards, so if you've got two children at different schools, they may well be on different ones. A tutor who's taught your child's exact texts can start on real essay questions in the first lesson. We'll check the board and the books on the call. If you're not sure of the board, your child's mock papers will usually say."
  - q: "My child talks well about books. Why is their predicted English grade lower?"
    a: "More often than not, it's the timed conditions rather than understanding. Talking about a book in class is one thing. Writing about it in silence, against the clock, is another. That's when essays drift into retelling the story, or stop before the best point. Mocks carry a lot of weight in predicted grades, so that's what shows up. A tutor reads a recent mock essay, finds where the marks slipped, and practises that one habit until it holds with a timer running."
  - q: "Are the English tutors qualified teachers?"
    a: "Many of them are, including some English teachers. Every tutor on our platform is a graduate, and some sat these exams themselves not long ago. For a child who needs patience and structure, an experienced teacher is often the best fit. For one who wants to push further, someone who knows the latest mark schemes inside out can be great. Each of them has had an interview with Harry or Joe."
  - q: "What if my child refuses to have an English tutor?"
    a: "It's very common, and English is often the subject teenagers feel touchiest about, because it means showing someone their writing. That usually eases once they've met the tutor and found it's nothing like a lesson at school. So your child meets the tutor on a free video call first, and can turn down a particular person without turning down help altogether. If it still isn't working for them after a couple of lessons, there's no contract, so you can stop there."
