faqs:
  - q: "What do Nuneaton teenagers tend to struggle with most in GCSE English?"
    a: "The Literature essays, more often than not, because they mix memory, argument and speed in one go. Your child has to recall quotations from a play or novel studied months ago, build a point about them and finish inside the time. The 19th-century novel trips up the most, whether that's A Christmas Carol or, on Edexcel, George Eliot's Silas Marner, because the language feels distant. A tutor works on short, timed answers first, then full essays."
  - q: "Does it matter whether my child's school does AQA or Edexcel for GCSE English?"
    a: "Yes, more than parents expect. The Literature set texts differ, so a tutor needs to know the exact novel, play and poetry anthology your child studies. The Language papers are built differently too, with different kinds of extract and different writing tasks. The skills overlap, but practice papers from the wrong board can mislead. We check the board on the call and match to it."
  - q: "Is it an English tutor my child needs, or just more revision?"
    a: "Look at a recent marked essay. If the ideas are good but the marks are low, with comments like 'develop this' or 'more analysis', it's usually technique, and a tutor fixes that quickest. If whole questions are blank or unfinished, it's timing or confidence, which also suits one-to-one work. If your child is already scoring well on most questions, focused practice at home may be enough, and we'll say so on the call."
  - q: "My son is dyslexic and dreads writing essays. Can an English tutor help?"
    a: "Yes. Mention it on the call, and the tutors we suggest will be ones who've worked with dyslexic teenagers before. Lessons tend to break an essay into small pieces: a plan of four or five words, one paragraph at a time, a short list of quotations rather than a long one. The shared whiteboard helps, because plans can be drawn as boxes and arrows instead of lines of text. And you can ask the school about access arrangements for the exams."
  - q: "My daughter says she doesn't need a tutor for English. Should we push it?"
    a: "Gently, maybe. Lots of teenagers say no because they imagine an extra school lesson, and a one-to-one hour feels quite different once they're in it. She can meet the tutor on a free 15-minute video call first, with no commitment. If she tries a few lessons and still doesn't want to carry on, you stop, with nothing to cancel. Often the first essay that comes back with a better mark changes her mind more than anything we could say."
