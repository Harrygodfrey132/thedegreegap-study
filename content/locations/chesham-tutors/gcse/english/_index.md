faqs:
  - q: "What is the hardest part of GCSE English for Chesham students?"
    a: "Two things account for most of the lost marks locally. The first is the long evaluation question, where the student is asked how far they agree with a statement about the text and answers by describing what happens instead of arguing with it. The second is the poetry comparison, which needs both poems held in the same paragraph rather than one after the other. Neither is a reading problem. Both are answer shapes, and shapes can be taught."
  - q: "Why has the English prediction come back below what we expected?"
    a: "Predictions come from mock papers plus teacher judgement, and the gap parents usually notice is between what a child can discuss at home and what they can produce in 45 minutes with no notes. A student who talks brilliantly about a novel and then writes three descriptive paragraphs will be predicted on the writing. The first job for a tutor is reading the actual mock script, because the marks lost there tell you which of the two problems it is. Chesham Grammar, Chiltern Hills and The Amersham School all run their English mocks on their own timetables, so ask when your child's was sat as well as what it scored."
  - q: "What happens in a typical GCSE English session?"
    a: "An hour usually runs in three parts. The first fifteen minutes go on last week's writing, marked against the real criteria with the reasons said out loud. The middle half hour rebuilds one technique, an opening that takes a position, embedding a quotation, or holding two poems together. The last fifteen minutes are a timed question on that technique alone, marked there and then. Students see their own script improve inside the session, which does more for belief than being told it will."
  - q: "What has English tutoring actually moved for other families?"
    a: "We quote real ones rather than averages. One parent of a Year 10 who had gone quiet on the subject describes moving from a grade 5 to a 6/7 across six months with the same tutor. Another describes a daughter passing English and Maths at 4 and 5 after arriving well short of both. Movement tends to come in bands rather than points, and the honest range is one band a term where the work between lessons happens."
  - q: "What if my child flatly does not want a tutor?"
    a: "Very common, and worth handling gently rather than winning. Most resistance is about being watched while getting something wrong, which is sharper in English than in Maths because the work feels personal. The free 15-minute meeting exists partly for this: your child meets the tutor with nothing at stake and no writing involved. Most engage within two or three sessions once they notice it is one adult paying attention rather than a class of thirty. If they still hate it after two, stop."
