faqs:
  - q: "What do Windsor teenagers usually find hardest in GCSE English?"
    a: "In our experience, it's usually the comparison and the unseen poem. Both ask your child to work with a text they've never seen, under time, and to say how two writers differ rather than what each one says. Set texts feel safer because they can be revised. A tutor practises the unseen skills little and often, with short timed extracts, until comparing becomes a habit rather than a scramble at the end of the paper."
  - q: "My child's school changed English exam boards. Does AQA or Edexcel make a difference?"
    a: "It does, mainly to how the papers are built. Both boards test reading and writing, but they build their papers differently. AQA's second Language paper, for instance, asks how two writers from different times handle a similar topic, and Edexcel arranges its texts its own way. The Literature set texts and poetry anthologies differ too. So we match your child with a tutor who knows their board, and if a sibling sat the other one, their old notes may only help in parts."
  - q: "How much English work should my child do between lessons each week?"
    a: "For most teenagers, an hour a week is plenty. That might be one timed paragraph, ten minutes a day learning quotations, and some reading of their own choice, anything from a newspaper to a novel. The tutor sets it and marks it the following week. Much more than 90 minutes tends to turn into copying out notes, which feels busy but rarely moves a grade."
  - q: "My child is dyslexic. Can an English tutor still help?"
    a: "Yes. Mention it on the call, and the tutors we suggest will be ones with experience of teaching dyslexic teenagers. Many use fixed planning frames, so your child knows exactly what goes in each paragraph and has less to juggle while writing. Lessonspace helps too, because plans sit on a shared screen and every lesson can be replayed. If your child has extra time in exams, the tutor can practise with that timing so it feels normal on the day."
  - q: "My teenager says English tutoring is a waste of time. Should we push them?"
    a: "Gently, and not for long. A lot of teenagers picture an extra English lesson with someone reading over their shoulder, and that picture usually changes once they've met the tutor. That's why your child gets a free 15-minute video call first, with nothing booked. If they've tried two or three lessons and still don't want it, stop. It only works when they're willing to give it a go."
