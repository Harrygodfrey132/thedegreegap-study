faqs:
  - q: "Which part of GCSE English trips up Ampthill teenagers most?"
    a: "Usually the Literature essays more than the reading. Plenty of students know their texts well and still write about the story instead of the writer's choices, or can't recall a quotation when it counts. Comparing unseen poems is the other big worry. On the Language side, the longer writing question tends to get rushed at the end of the paper. A tutor works out which of these is costing your child most and starts there."
  - q: "My child says English is 'just opinions'. Why are they losing marks?"
    a: "Because the marks go to opinions that are backed up and explained, and that part is a skill nobody quite teaches as one. Your child might spot a good idea about a character and then move straight on, without the quotation or the comment on the language that earns the higher marks. A tutor shows them what a full point looks like, then practises it until it becomes the normal way they write a paragraph."
  - q: "Can the same tutor cover English Language and English Literature?"
    a: "Yes, and most of our English tutors teach both. The two GCSEs ask for different things: Language is about reading unseen extracts and writing your own piece, while Literature is about set texts you've studied. But the habits overlap, like close reading and planning before writing. One tutor who sees both can move the time to whichever paper needs it that month."
  - q: "Writing is slow and tiring for my child because of dyslexia. Is English tutoring still worth it?"
    a: "Yes. Tell us about it on the call, and the tutors we suggest will be people who've taught dyslexic teenagers before. One-to-one, the tutor can break an essay into small steps, use plans and sentence starters, and practise at a pace that suits your child. It's also worth checking with school whether your child gets extra time or other exam arrangements, because practising under those conditions makes the real thing feel familiar."
  - q: "My child flatly refuses to have an English tutor. What now?"
    a: "Don't push it on day one. Most teenagers say no because they imagine an extra English lesson, and it feels different once they've met the person. That's why your child gets a free 15-minute video call with the tutor before anything is agreed. Let them ask questions and have a say in the choice. If they try a couple of lessons and still hate it, you stop, with nothing to cancel."
