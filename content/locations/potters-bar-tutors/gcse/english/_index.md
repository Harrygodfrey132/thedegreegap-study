faqs:
  - q: "Could English hold my child back from an apprenticeship at 16?"
    a: "It can, which surprises some parents. Many apprenticeships ask for a pass in GCSE English, and a teenager who starts one without it may have to keep working on English alongside the job. The application counts too, since a form and an interview are often where an employer first sees how your child writes and explains themselves. A tutor works on the reading and writing the GCSE needs, so English isn't the thing standing in the way."
  - q: "Why is my child struggling with English when other subjects are fine?"
    a: "Often because English rewards a skill that's hard to pick up in a busy classroom: turning an idea into a paragraph. Your child can understand a poem perfectly well in class and still only manage two lines about it in a test. Sometimes it goes back further, to essay planning in Year 7 or 8 that went by too fast in a big class, so every essay since has been written on the fly. Working out which it is, from your child's own writing, is where the tutor starts."
  - q: "How much English work should happen between lessons?"
    a: "Not loads. An hour or so a week is plenty for most children: perhaps one timed paragraph on a set text and one short piece of writing, which the tutor looks at in the next lesson. Small, regular pieces beat a big push the weekend before a mock. Reading helps too, and it doesn't have to be classics. A long magazine article or a decent novel gets your child used to sentences that do more than one job."
  - q: "My child is dyslexic. Can an English tutor still help?"
    a: "Yes. Plenty of tutors on our platform are used to working with dyslexia, and we'll ask about it on the call so we can choose well. One-to-one, the tutor can break an essay into small, clear steps, use colour and layout on the whiteboard, and let your child talk an idea through before writing it down. Nobody's rushing them. Every lesson's recorded too, which means an explanation can be replayed as often as your child needs. It's also worth asking the school about exam access arrangements."
  - q: "And if the English Language result comes back below a 4?"
    a: "Then there's a second chance sooner than you might think. English Language is one of only two GCSEs, along with Maths, that can be resat in November, as well as the following summer. A teenager without a 4 also keeps studying English after 16, whether that's at a sixth form or at a college within reach of Potters Bar, such as Barnet and Southgate or Oaklands. A tutor can start straight after results day, on the longer reading answers and the writing task, where there's often the most to gain."
