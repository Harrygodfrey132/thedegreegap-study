faqs:
  - q: "How does GCSE English attainment around Colchester compare with the national average?"
    a: "Essex sits close to the national average overall, with a wide spread underneath it, and Colchester holds most of that spread in one town. That county-level figure tells an individual family very little. The more useful comparison is between your child's current grade and the grade their next step publishes, because that gap is the one that can be worked on. English also moves more than most subjects once technique is addressed, which makes the starting point less predictive than it first appears."
  - q: "How late into Year 11 is it still worth starting English?"
    a: "Right through to the spring, though what a plan can realistically cover narrows as the year goes on. The spring mocks are what sixth-form predicted grades are built from, so work done before them counts twice. English in particular needs runway, because changing how a student plans and structures an essay takes a few weeks to bed in and then needs repetition under timed conditions. Starting in April or May is still worth doing, but the plan narrows to the highest-yield techniques rather than a rebuild."
  - q: "Why has the school predicted a lower English grade than we thought?"
    a: "Predicted grades come from mock performance and teacher judgement, and in English the gap parents notice is usually between how well a child talks about a text and how well they write about it under time pressure. Those are genuinely different skills, and only the second one is assessed. A tutor's first job is to read recent mock scripts and find where the gap sits, whether that is planning, evidence, timing, or an argument that arrives too late in the essay to be rewarded."
  - q: "How do you match an English tutor for a dyslexic or ADHD student?"
    a: "We ask about it directly on the consultation call and treat it as a matching criterion rather than a footnote. Plenty of tutors on the platform work regularly with neurodivergent students, and several specialise in it. English is often where processing and organisation differences show up most sharply, because the subject asks for sustained written output under time pressure. Lessonspace helps here, since explanations can be chunked visually, structures kept on screen as scaffolds, and notes revisited after the session rather than held in memory."
  - q: "What if my child does not want a tutor?"
    a: "It is the most common worry parents raise and it is worth handling gently rather than pushing through. Most students resist the idea before they meet anyone, then settle within two or three sessions, because one-to-one is not school-shaped: one adult, full attention, nobody else in the room to compare themselves to. The free 15-minute video meeting exists partly for this, so your child meets the tutor before committing. If they still do not want to continue after a session or two, pause it. That is their call."
