faqs:
  - q: "Which bit of GCSE English trips up Tamworth teenagers most?"
    a: "The Language extracts your child hasn't seen before, and the analysis paragraph in Literature. Unseen extracts take away the safety net of revision. A child who's learned lots of quotes can still stall when the material's new, and what helps is a reading method they can repeat, not more content. In Literature, the marks sit in the lines after the quotation, where your child needs to say what the writer's choice does, not just what it is. Both are technique. That's why they often move faster than parents expect, once someone names them."
  - q: "How will you know what's going wrong with my child's English?"
    a: "By reading your child's own writing, not the school report. The first lesson is for finding the problem, before anything gets planned. Your child writes or brings an essay, and the tutor marks it line by line against the mark scheme while they watch. Ideas that never get developed. Quotes dropped in without comment. An argument that changes direction halfway through. A conclusion that repeats the introduction. Those are four different problems with four different fixes, and one grade hides all of them. From the second lesson, the plan's built around whichever costs most."
  - q: "How much English homework will my child get between lessons?"
    a: "Roughly one short piece of writing a week. It doesn't have to be a full essay. A single strong paragraph, marked carefully, does more good than several long essays nobody looks at. Your child learns from seeing how the mark scheme treats their own sentences. Literature usually adds a little quote learning, a few minutes most days instead of one long Sunday session. The tutor sets it at the end of each lesson, so it follows straight on from what they've just done."
