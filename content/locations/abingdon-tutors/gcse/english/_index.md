faqs:
  - q: "Which bits of GCSE English trip Abingdon teenagers up?"
    a: "Most often it's the gap between saying what a writer means and showing how they do it. Your child spots that a character's angry and quotes the line, then stops, when the marks are for the word choice that shows the anger. In Literature, it's having a few short quotations ready for each set text, because the exam is closed book. If your child fancies A-Level English Language or Literature, both offered at Larkmead, that close reading is exactly what the A-Level builds on. The tutor starts with whichever of these is costing your child most."
  - q: "Does the exam board make much difference in English?"
    a: "Mostly through Literature. Each school chooses its own set texts, so two friends at different Abingdon schools might not share a single book, and the board decides how questions on those texts are asked and marked. If the tutor already knows your child's play or novel, lessons can skip the plot summary and go straight to essay practice, which helps most in Year 11, when time is short."
  - q: "Is it a tutor my child needs, or just more practice at home?"
    a: "A recent marked essay usually tells you. Good ideas that stop halfway or run out of time often just need practice against a timer, and you can do that at home. Essays that summarise the story instead of making a point about it suggest a gap in method, and that's the part a tutor can teach. We're happy to look at a piece of work with you on the call. Sometimes practice at home is all it takes, and we'll tell you if we think so."
  - q: "My son has ADHD and won't write more than a line. Is a tutor worth trying?"
    a: "Often, yes, and it's something parents raise with us a lot. Tell us on the call what's helped before and what hasn't, and we'll suggest tutors who are used to working with ADHD. A tutor might start by letting your son say his answer out loud, then help him write down the first sentence of what he just said. Short bursts tend to work better than one long slog, and there's nobody else there to feel slow in front of. Every lesson's recorded, so he can go back to a plan or a model paragraph when he's ready."
  - q: "Am I allowed to watch the first lesson?"
    a: "Yes, most tutors are happy for you to sit in on the first one, just to see how they explain things and how your child takes to it. After that, most parents leave them to it, because teenagers tend to say more about their writing without an audience. You'll still know what's going on. The tutor sends a short note after every lesson on what they worked on and what comes next, and the recording's there if your child wants to go back over something."
