faq_1_q: "The winter mock is a few weeks away. Can a tutor change it in time?"
faq_1_a: "Often, yes, if the marks are going on technique rather than knowledge. A few weeks is usually enough to work on how your child reads a six-mark question and how they use the time in a paper. Those two cost more marks than most parents expect. A gap from Year 9 is different. A few weeks won't close it, but the mock will show you exactly where it is, and that's what the spring term is for. The first lesson usually makes it clear which of the two you're looking at."
faq_2_q: "My son's school already runs revision sessions. What does a tutor add?"
faq_2_a: "Someone to sit with his own wrong answers, which is hard to manage in a room of thirty. School revision goes through the topics in order, and that's useful. A tutor starts from the last paper he sat instead, finds the two or three kinds of question that keep going wrong, and stays on them until they hold. Then the school sessions start making more sense to him, because he's not getting stuck in the same place every week. The two work side by side. Nobody's replacing school."
faq_3_q: "Not every Slough school uses the same exam board. Does that change who you'd suggest?"
faq_3_a: "Yes, and we check it before anything else. Two children taking the same subject at different Slough schools can be on different boards, and each board words its questions and marks the longer answers in its own style. So on the call we'll confirm your child's board, and whether they're sitting the higher or foundation papers, then only suggest tutors who know that course. A tutor who already knows it is useful from the first ten minutes. One who doesn't spends your money catching up."
faq_4_q: "My daughter's a long way behind in Maths. Where does a tutor even start?"
faq_4_a: "Further back than the topic her class is on, and that's deliberate. When a Year 10 is a long way behind in Maths, it usually comes down to a method from Year 7 or 8 that never settled, with everything since stacked on top of it. The tutor finds that spot on a paper in the first lesson, goes back to it without making a fuss, and builds forward from there. It's slower than revision to begin with. But it's the version that's still there after the next test."
