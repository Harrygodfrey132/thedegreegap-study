faqs:
  - q: "What's the hardest part of GCSE Science for children in Slough?"
    a: "Usually it isn't a topic but a type of question: the ones that turn Science into Maths. Moles and concentration in Chemistry, rearranging equations and converting units in Physics, magnification and percentage change in Biology. Children who revise well still drop these marks, because a revision guide teaches the fact and the paper wants the working. Close behind are the six-mark answers and the questions on required practicals, where your child has to describe the method the way it was done, not the way they half remember it. Recall is rarely what's holding them back."
  - q: "Should my child be on foundation or higher tier for Science?"
    a: "It depends on where your child is now, not where anyone hopes they'll be, and in Combined Science it's one decision for all six papers. Higher goes up to a 9-9, with little safety net at the bottom. Foundation stops at a 5-5 and puts far more of the marks within reach. A child sitting around a 5 in Year 10 is often right on higher, with focused work on the calculation questions that decide it. A marked paper in Year 10 turns it into a decision rather than a guess. And the school should be part of that conversation."
  - q: "Can a Science tutor work with my child's dyslexia, ADHD or slower processing?"
    a: "Yes, and Science suits it well, because a lot of lost marks come from how much there is to hold in your head, not from the understanding. Tutors who often work with neurodivergent children use the whiteboard to break a six-mark answer into named steps. They keep one layout for every calculation, and turn each set experiment into a routine your child can rehearse. Every lesson has a replay, so nothing depends on notes scribbled under pressure. Tell us on the call what school already has in place, including exam arrangements like extra time, and we'll match on that first."
  - q: "Is Science tutoring worth the money?"
    a: "If there's a clear gap, it usually is. One weak science, or one tier decision, is often holding down a grade that covers all three. If your child is already on target across all six papers, the gain is smaller, and a short block on one topic might be enough. The call is there to give you a straight answer, even if that answer is 'not yet'."

One adult, one screen and no class watching is a different thing, and a child who's been quietly lost in Physics since Year 9 usually feels the relief.
