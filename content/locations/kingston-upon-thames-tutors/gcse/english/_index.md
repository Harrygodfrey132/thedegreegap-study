faqs:
  - q: "Where do Kingston upon Thames children lose most marks in GCSE English?"
    a: "Answering the question on the paper rather than the one they revised for. Lots of Kingston children reach Year 11 well read, and the marks still go missing in the same three places. The Literature extract question, where the answer drifts to the whole text and leaves the lines in front of them. Unseen poetry, where nothing's been prepared. And the writing tasks at the end of the Language papers, done in a rush because the earlier answers ran long. All three are technique. That's good news, because technique moves faster than reading ever could."
  - q: "What's the best point in Year 11 to bring in an English tutor?"
    a: "September to November is best, before the autumn mock. Around Kingston upon Thames that mock carries extra weight. Tiffin's sixth-form applications from other schools close at the end of the autumn term, and Kingston Grammar makes its conditional offers before Christmas, both on predicted grades. English has a second reason to start early. Answering the question asked is a habit, and habits take a run of marked essays, not a fortnight. Starting after Christmas still helps, and plenty of families do. The work just narrows to timing and technique."
  - q: "My child's only in Year 10. Isn't that too early for an English tutor?"
    a: "No, and for English it's often the better year. Year 10 is usually when the set texts are first taught, so the tutor can build the habit of planning against the question while your child's meeting the books in class. That beats unpicking a year of wandering essays later. Lessons are calmer too, with more close reading of the passage and less exam pressure. A child who learns in Year 10 to pick what matters, rather than pour everything onto the page, starts Year 11 with the hardest part done."
  - q: "How do you pick the English tutors on your platform?"
    a: "Carefully, and around 3% of applicants are taken on. There's a written application first, then a subject test on the current courses and mark schemes, not the ones they sat themselves. Then Harry or Joe interviews them, and the real question is whether they can explain something clearly to someone who isn't getting it yet. References come after that. For English we also look at how they mark, because honest, careful marking is most of what your child gets from the hour."
  - q: "Can I watch my child's English lessons, or is that a bad idea?"
    a: "Not a bad idea at all, and most tutors are happy for you to join the first one. After that most parents step back. A fifteen-year-old will say 'I don't know what the question means' to a tutor far more easily without a parent there, and that's where the hour starts to help. You won't be kept in the dark, though. Every lesson on Lessonspace has a replay, and the tutor sends a short note afterwards on what was covered and what's been set. You can ask to see the marked essays too."
