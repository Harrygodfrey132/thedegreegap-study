faqs:
  - q: "What is the hardest part of GCSE English for Kingston upon Thames students?"
    a: "It's answering the question set rather than the one they revised for. In a borough where a lot of students arrive at Year 11 well read, the marks go missing in the same three places: the Literature extract question, where a student writes about the whole text instead of the lines in front of them; unseen poetry, where there's no prepared material to lean on; and the writing tasks on the Language papers, where the last answer gets written in a rush because the first ones ran long. All three are technique, which is why they move faster than reading ever could."
  - q: "When is the best time to start GCSE English tutoring in Year 11?"
    a: "September to November, ahead of the autumn mock. Around Kingston upon Thames that mock carries more than usual, because Tiffin's external sixth-form applications close at the end of the autumn term and Kingston Grammar makes its conditional offers before Christmas, both against predicted grades. In English there's a second reason to move early: answering the question asked is a habit, and habits take a run of marked essays rather than a fortnight. A start after Christmas still helps, and plenty do, but the work narrows to timing and technique."
  - q: "My child is in Year 10. Is it too early to start GCSE English tutoring?"
    a: "No, and for English it's the better year. Year 10 is when the set texts get taught for the first time, so a tutor can build the habit of planning against the question while the class is still meeting the material, instead of unpicking a year of essays that wandered. The sessions are calmer too: more reading of the passage in front of them, less exam pressure. A Year 10 who learns to select rather than empty everything they know onto the page tends to arrive in Year 11 with the hardest part already done."

  - q: "Can I sit in on my child's GCSE English tutoring session?"
    a: "Yes, and most tutors welcome it for the first one. After that most parents step back, because a fifteen-year-old admits 'I don't know what the question means' to a tutor far more readily without a parent in the room, and that admission is the whole point of the hour. You still see everything: the session on Lessonspace is recorded, the marked essays come back to you as well as to your child, and the tutor sends a short note after each lesson on what was covered and what's set.
