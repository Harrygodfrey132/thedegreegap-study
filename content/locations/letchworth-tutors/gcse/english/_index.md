faqs:
  - q: "What's the hardest part of GCSE English for Letchworth teenagers?"
    a: "For a lot of teenagers, it's the reading questions on an extract they've never seen. There's no set text to revise for those, so your child needs a routine for reading it. Literature is almost the opposite: the exam's closed book, so key quotations have to be learned well enough to use under pressure. Timing catches out many good readers too, with the writing task squeezed into the last few minutes. The details change with the exam board, so we'll check which one your child's on before suggesting tutors."
  - q: "My child's good at Maths. Why is English such a struggle?"
    a: "Mostly because the method behind a good answer hasn't clicked yet. Maths has clear steps and a right answer at the end. English marks go to things that feel vaguer, like how well an idea's explained or how closely a quote is picked apart. A child who likes rules can find that unsettling, and may decide they're 'just not an English person'. Often it goes back to essay planning that never quite stuck. The tutor's first job is finding where it went wrong, then giving your child a routine they can repeat on any question."
  - q: "How do I know if my child needs an English tutor or just more revision?"
    a: "Look at a recent essay or mock paper, not just the grade. If the ideas are good but answers run out of time or never reach a point, that's technique, which is usually the easier fix. If the page mostly retells the story, or the quotes are missing, there's a bigger gap in how to read and plan, and that's hard to close alone. Revision on its own helps most with Literature quotes and context. For the Language papers, practice works far better with someone marking it and explaining why."
  - q: "Can an English tutor help if my child has dyslexia or ADHD?"
    a: "Yes. Plenty of the tutors on the platform have worked with dyslexic children and children with ADHD, and we'll ask about it on the call so we can suggest the right people. In English, that might mean planning on a simple grid on the whiteboard, or writing in short timed bursts instead of one long stretch. One-to-one, the pace is your child's, not the class's. And because every lesson's recorded, a method can be replayed as often as it takes."
  - q: "My child says they don't want an English tutor. What then?"
    a: "You're not the first Letchworth parent to hear that, and English tends to get the firmest 'no', because nobody likes handing their writing over to be judged. Meeting the tutor usually does more than any amount of persuading. The free 15-minute video meeting lets your child see who it is and ask a question or two, with nothing booked. If that tutor isn't right, we'll suggest another. And with no contract, if your child's still unhappy after a few lessons, you can stop."
