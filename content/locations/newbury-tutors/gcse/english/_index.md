faqs:
  - q: "What grade does my child need in GCSE English for A-Level English at a Newbury sixth form?"
    a: "It varies, so check the current lists. St Bart's asks for a 5 in English Literature before A-Level English Literature, and Kennet's list asks for a 6 in Literature or Language. Park House and Trinity publish their own. Beyond that, English Language counts for nearly everything: St Bart's wants a 4 in it, or a resit alongside A-Levels. If your child is on a 4 now, reading and timed writing are usually where the quickest progress is."
  - q: "What actually happens in a GCSE English lesson with a tutor?"
    a: "Most lessons run for an hour. The first few minutes go on last week's practice, a paragraph or a timed answer, marked together so your child sees where the marks went. The middle part is whatever is costing most, perhaps reading a Sign of the Four extract closely or planning a piece of descriptive writing. Then a short exam question against the clock, with the mark scheme beside it, so the habit sticks."
  - q: "My child reads all the time. Why are their GCSE English marks so low?"
    a: "Reading for pleasure and reading for the exam are different skills, and plenty of keen readers find that out in Year 10. The exam wants your child to pick out a few words, say what they do and link them to the writer's purpose, all in a set time. It wants a plan before an essay, too. Often nobody has shown them how to do that quickly. The tutor's first job is finding which step is missing."
  - q: "Can a GCSE English tutor help a child in Newbury with dyslexia or ADHD?"
    a: "Yes. Mention it when we speak, and the tutors we put forward will be ones with experience of dyslexia or ADHD. On Lessonspace, extracts and essay plans sit on a shared whiteboard in small chunks, and every lesson can be replayed, which helps with learning quotations. Tutors often teach a fixed shape for each type of answer, so less has to be held in mind at once. If your child has extra time in exams, the tutor can practise with that too."
  - q: "My teenager says they don't need help with English. What should we do?"
    a: "Don't push hard, but don't drop it either. English feels personal, so a lot of teenagers bristle at the idea of help with it. That's why your child meets the tutor on a free 15-minute video call before anything is booked, and the first lesson starts with what they already do well. If after two or three lessons they still don't want it, stop. It has to be their hour."
