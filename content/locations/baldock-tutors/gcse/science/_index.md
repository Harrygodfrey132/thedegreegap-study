angle_body_1: "The longest questions on a Science paper carry six marks each, sometimes nine, and they are the most reliably misread part of the exam. A student writes three accurate statements, stops, and scores two. The reason is that the mark scheme is not counting facts, it is looking for a chain: this happens, which causes this, which is why that. Statements sitting side by side without the links between them are worth less than a student expects. The command word makes it worse, because describe, explain and evaluate ask for genuinely different things and are often treated as if they mean the same."
angle_body_2: "This is good news, in a way, because it is a writing habit rather than a knowledge gap, and writing habits change faster than knowledge does. The first hour looks at extended answers from a recent paper and marks them in front of your child against the real scheme, which is usually the moment it clicks. faqs:
  - q: "What is the hardest part of GCSE Science for students around Baldock?"
    a: "The extended answers, more than any particular topic. Six and nine-mark questions appear across all three sciences and they reward a chain of reasoning rather than a list of correct facts, which is not how most students are taught to revise. Beyond that, three topic areas come up repeatedly: rearranging equations in Physics, moles and concentration calculations in Chemistry, and the genetics questions in Biology where the working has to be set out the way the scheme expects. All four are method rather than knowledge, which means they respond quickly."
  - q: "What is the difference between AQA, Edexcel and OCR GCSE Science, and does it matter for tutoring?"
    a: "Yes, and in Science it matters more than in other subjects. The content overlaps while the examining does not: the boards assess required practicals differently, mark the six-mark answers to different structures, and print different amounts of equation support in the paper. A student who learned a practical under one board's method can meet the same practical framed another way and lose marks on material they know perfectly well. We confirm the board on the consultation call, and where a student has moved schools we look for a tutor who has taught both."
  - q: "Should my child take foundation or higher tier GCSE Science?"
    a: "It comes down to where your child sits in Year 10 rather than where anyone would like them to end up. Higher tier reaches the top grades but scores very little below its floor, while foundation caps lower and makes the marks available far more reachable. A student close to the boundary is often right on higher with focused work on the specific topics that decide it. A student well below usually does better on foundation, where questions are winnable and confidence builds. A Year 10 diagnostic makes that a decision rather than a guess, and school should be part of it."
