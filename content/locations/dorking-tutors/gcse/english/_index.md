faqs:
  - q: "What English grade does my child need for A-Levels at the Ashcombe or Reigate College?"
    a: "More than many families expect, even for subjects that aren't English. The Ashcombe's sixth form guide says entry to A-Level courses usually needs a grade 4 in English Language as well as Maths. Reigate College asks for a 5 in English Language for courses such as Biology, Psychology, Economics and Business. Requirements can change from year to year, so check the current prospectus. If your child is close to a boundary in Year 10, a term of steady work on Language Paper 1 can be well spent."
  - q: "English Language and English Literature: does my child need help with both?"
    a: "Often, but not always equally. Literature rewards knowing the set texts well and having quotations ready, which is mostly steady practice. Language is about reading unseen extracts quickly and writing well under time pressure, which takes a different kind of practice. Plenty of children are comfortable in one and shaky in the other. A tutor looks at recent marks for both papers and splits the lessons to match, rather than giving them equal time out of habit."
  - q: "How much should my child do between English lessons?"
    a: "Around an hour or so a week is plenty for most. Ten minutes a day on quotations, a few lines at a time, beats a long session on Sunday. Then one practice paragraph or a short plan, which the tutor reads before the next lesson. More than that tends to bring diminishing returns, especially in Year 11 when every subject wants time. If your child has a mock coming, the tutor may ask for one timed answer instead."
  - q: "My child is dyslexic and finds long essays exhausting. Can a GCSE English tutor help?"
    a: "Yes. Mention it on the call and we'll only put forward tutors with experience of teaching dyslexic students. On Lessonspace a tutor can break an essay into small visual steps on the whiteboard, and short plans often help more than long drafts. They'll also make sure practice matches any exam arrangements your child has, such as extra time or a reader, so it feels familiar on the day."
  - q: "My child says they're 'just bad at English'. Will a tutor help, or make it worse?"
    a: "Usually help, though it's worth taking the worry seriously. Children often say this because English marking feels vague, so they can't see what they'd do differently. A tutor makes it concrete: this quotation, this point, this is where the mark comes from. Your child meets the tutor on a free 15-minute video call first, so there's no stranger at lesson one. And if after a couple of lessons they really don't want to carry on, you can stop with no fuss."
