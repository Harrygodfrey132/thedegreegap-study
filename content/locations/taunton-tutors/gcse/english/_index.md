faqs:
  - q: "Where do Taunton teenagers drop the most marks in GCSE English?"
    a: "The extended writing, more than anything. Half the marks on each AQA Language paper sit in that last question, and a child without a plan loses them steadily, not all at once. In Literature, it's the essays written from memory, where quotations get remembered but not explained, and the unseen poem. A tutor usually finds one of these is the main leak and starts there, so the first few lessons feel focused rather than general."
  - q: "My child's school uses AQA for English. Does the exam board really matter?"
    a: "Yes, more than people expect. The reading and writing skills are shared, yet each board builds its papers its own way. AQA, for instance, sets two Language papers of 1 hour 45 minutes, each half reading and half writing, and other boards split the texts and the marks their own way. Literature set texts vary from school to school as well. That's why we ask about the board and the texts on the call, and match a tutor who knows both."
  - q: "If I sat in on a GCSE English lesson, what would I see?"
    a: "Usually it opens with last week's piece of writing, marked, and a chat about the one thing to change. Then the tutor teaches a skill, like building a paragraph around one quotation or varying sentence openings, using a short extract on the shared screen. The last stretch is timed practice on a real exam question. Your child leaves with one task for the week, never a mountain."
  - q: "My son is dyslexic and hates writing. Can an English tutor still help?"
    a: "Yes. When you tell us on the call, we match a tutor who's worked with dyslexic students before. They'll break the writing into small steps, plan with your son on the whiteboard before anything gets drafted, and focus on what earns marks rather than on neatness. Access arrangements like extra time are set by his school, but the tutor can help him practise using them well."
  - q: "My teenager insists English is fine and refuses a tutor. Do we push?"
    a: "Gently, maybe. Lots of teenagers say no because they picture extra school, and it feels different once they've met the tutor. That's why your child has a free 15-minute video call with them before anything's booked. And if a couple of lessons in they still can't stand it, you simply stop: there's nothing to cancel. English is often the subject children think they can't improve, which is usually not true."
