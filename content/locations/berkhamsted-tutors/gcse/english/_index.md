  - q: "My child's always been a big reader. So why does GCSE English feel this hard?"
    a: "The exam rewards a skill that reading on its own doesn't build. It takes practice to plan an argument quickly, pick quotations that can be reused and write analysis instead of description, and a busy class rarely leaves time for that one-to-one. Strong readers can be the most frustrated, because they understand the book and still lose marks on structure and time. In the first lesson the tutor looks at a recent essay to see which of those is the problem, and starts there."
  - q: "Does the tutor set homework? How much?"
    a: "Yes, usually about an hour a week, occasionally up to ninety minutes. Normally that's one timed answer, or a plan and a paragraph, on whatever the lesson covered, plus a few quotations to learn. Much more than that doesn't tend to help, because in English the marking and feedback count for more than the amount written. The tutor goes through it at the start of the next lesson, so homework and teaching stay joined up."

    a: "Yes, and we ask about it on every first call. In English the useful changes are practical ones. Essay plans built from a fixed structure. Quotations learnt in small sets, spaced out over the week. Reading aloud where it helps, and timed writing built up bit by bit. The whiteboard keeps everything visible and saved, and there's a replay of every lesson. Tell us what support your child already has, including exam access arrangements, and we'll match a tutor with relevant experience."

After that most parents step back, because teenagers tend to speak up more freely without an audience, and talking an essay through out loud is a big part of English. You'll still get a short note after every lesson on what was covered and what's been set."
