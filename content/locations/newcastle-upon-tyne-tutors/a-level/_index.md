faq_1_q: "Strong GCSEs, then a shock on the first Year 12 assessment. Is that normal?"
faq_1_a: "Normal enough that we hear it most weeks in the autumn. GCSE rewards recognising a question type and running the method. A-Level asks the student to choose the method with nothing announcing it, to show why, and to do far more of the work alone between lessons. A student who cruised through Year 11 has often never built the habits that needs, and the first assessment finds that out. It is not a talent problem. The tutor reads the script, finds the habit that stopped working and rebuilds it inside the current topic, so the fix and the syllabus move together."
faq_2_q: "When do Newcastle sixth forms write the UCAS predicted grades, and can tutoring still move them?"
faq_2_a: "Predictions are built from Year 12 exams and the first assessments of Year 13, and they go on the UCAS form in the autumn of Year 13, earlier still for medicine and for Oxford and Cambridge. So the work that changes a prediction happens in Year 12 and over the summer after it. A tutor cannot write the prediction, the sixth form does that, but the sessions can change the performance the prediction rests on. Where the prediction is already set, the work turns to the grade the offer will need in August, which is the one that matters in the end."
faq_3_q: "How does online A-Level tutoring work when the subject is all diagrams and working?"
faq_3_a: "It runs on Lessonspace, a shared whiteboard where the tutor and the student draw and write on the same screen. A Mechanics diagram, an organic mechanism or an essay plan is built in front of your child rather than described, and the whole session is recorded, so the explanation can be replayed before a mock. Nobody drives across Newcastle upon Tyne on a school night. The bigger point is that the match is made on the specification your child's sixth form teaches, not on who lives nearby, and at A-Level that decides more than it does at GCSE."
faq_4_q: "The August grade was not enough for the offer. Is a retake year worth it?"
faq_4_a: "Sometimes, and it deserves an honest conversation rather than a reflex. A retake works when the gap is exam technique or one strand of the subject, because those can be fixed in a focused year. It works less well when the grade reflects a subject the student has stopped wanting to do. The plan is different from first time round: fewer hours on content, more on the questions that went wrong, with the mark scheme open. Universities publish their own view of resits, and some courses treat them differently, so check the course before deciding. We will say plainly if we think it is the wrong call."
