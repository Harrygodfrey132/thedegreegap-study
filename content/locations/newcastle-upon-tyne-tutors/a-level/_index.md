faq_1_q: "Strong GCSEs, then a shock on the first Year 12 assessment. Is that normal?"
faq_1_a: "Completely normal, and we hear it most weeks in the autumn. GCSE mostly rewards spotting the type of question and running the method. At A-Level, nothing tells your child which method to pick. They have to show why it works, too, and far more of the learning happens alone between lessons. If they cruised through Year 11, they may never have needed those habits, and the first assessment is where that shows. It isn't a talent problem. The tutor reads the paper, finds the habit that's stopped working, and rebuilds it inside the topic they're on now, so the fix keeps pace with the course."
faq_2_q: "When do Newcastle sixth forms decide predicted grades, and is it too late to help?"
faq_2_a: "Rarely too late, but the timing matters. Predictions are built from the Year 12 exams and the first assessments of Year 13. They go on the UCAS form in the autumn of Year 13, and earlier in the autumn for Medicine, Oxford and Cambridge. So the work that shifts a prediction happens in Year 12 and the summer after it. A tutor can't write the prediction. That's the sixth form's job. But lessons can change the work it's based on. If the prediction's already set, the focus moves to the grade your child's offer needs in August, which is the one that counts in the end."
faq_3_q: "Can online lessons really work for a subject that's all diagrams and working?"
faq_3_a: "Yes, they really can. Lessons happen on the platform Lessonspace, a shared whiteboard where your child and the tutor both draw and write on the same screen. A Mechanics diagram, a Chemistry mechanism or an essay plan gets built in front of your child rather than described. Every lesson is recorded, so they can replay the explanation before a mock. Nobody drives across Newcastle on a school night, either. We also match on the exact course your child's sixth form teaches, not on who lives nearby, which matters even more at A-Level than at GCSE."
faq_4_q: "The August grade wasn't enough for the offer. Is a retake year worth it?"
faq_4_a: "Sometimes, and it's worth a proper conversation before deciding. A retake tends to work when the gap was exam technique or one part of the subject, because a focused year can fix those. It works less well if your child has simply stopped wanting to do the subject. The plan's different from first time round: fewer hours on content, more on the questions that went wrong, with the mark scheme open. Universities each have their own view of resits, and some courses treat them differently, so check the course first. If we think it's the wrong call, we'll tell you plainly."
