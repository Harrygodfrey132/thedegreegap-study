faqs:
  - q: "What do Peterborough teenagers find hardest about GCSE English?"
    a: "Timing, more often than the content. The Language papers and the Literature papers both ask for several developed answers in a fixed time, and the mock we see most often has a strong first answer followed by two that were clearly rushed. Next comes writing an argument rather than a description, which is a habit more than a gap in knowledge. Schools across the city teach different set texts, so the book itself matters less than these two things. They turn up on nearly every paper, whatever your child studied."
  - q: "What homework will the tutor give my child?"
    a: "Twenty to forty minutes between lessons, and what it is matters far more than how long it takes. One planned paragraph written against the clock beats an hour of rereading, because the marks go on how an argument's built, not on how much of the text your child can recall. If timing's the issue, short timed bursts two or three times a week do more than one long sitting. Most tutors set the task at the end of the lesson, aimed straight at what they've just worked on, and mark it before the next one."
  - q: "Would a tutor help, or does my child just need to revise more?"
    a: "Get out a recent marked essay and ignore the grade for a minute. Is the writing organised, with sensible points, but still not scoring? Then it's technique, and a tutor's the quickest fix, because it's about how answers are built rather than what your child knows. If the writing trails off halfway, or the essay describes the text instead of answering the question, a tutor can help there too. But if your child's already writing well-structured arguments and just needs more practice, extra past papers marked by their teacher may be all it takes."
  - q: "Can you help if my child has dyslexia, ADHD or processing difficulties?"
    a: "Yes. A number of tutors on our platform work with children who learn differently, and in English the support is mostly practical. Planning frames on the whiteboard, one paragraph shape used every time, and long answers chopped into named parts, so your child is never staring at an empty page. Where recall's hard, the tutor swaps long quotations for short, flexible ones, which are easier to remember and often score better. Tell us on the call what the school already has in place, including exam access arrangements, and we'll match on it."
  - q: "What if my child and the tutor don't get on?"
    a: "We'll find someone else, free of charge, and you won't need to give a reason. Most mismatches show up on the free video call, before any money changes hands, and that's partly what it's for. If it's the first paid lesson that doesn't feel right, a quick message is enough. Only around 3% of tutors who apply join the platform, but there's still plenty of choice, so the second match can be someone who works quite differently. That matters in English, because your child has to feel comfortable writing in front of them."
