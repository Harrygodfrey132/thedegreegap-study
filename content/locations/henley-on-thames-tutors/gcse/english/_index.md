faqs:
  - q: "What's the hardest part of GCSE English Language for a child in Henley-on-Thames?"
    a: "More often than not, the question about how a writer creates an effect. Your child has to pick out a few words, say what the writer is doing with them, and explain why it works on a reader, all in a few minutes. Plenty of strong readers end up describing the extract instead. The writing task is the other one, mostly because time is short by the end of the paper, so a quick plan matters as much as good sentences."
  - q: "My child reads all the time. Why are they struggling with English?"
    a: "Because reading widely and writing analysis against the clock are different skills. A child who devours novels often knows exactly what a passage is doing but hasn't been shown how to prove it in a paragraph. Others lose marks on structure, writing one long answer instead of several points that build. The tutor's first job is spotting which it is, usually from one timed answer in the first lesson. Then the practice goes on that one thing, a little every week."
  - q: "What happens in an online GCSE English lesson from Henley-on-Thames?"
    a: "Usually an hour, in three rough parts. First, a look at whatever your child wrote since last time, with the tutor marking it on the shared whiteboard. Then a new technique, like comparing two writers' viewpoints or planning a story opening, practised on a short extract. The last part is a timed question, so the new habit gets tried under some pressure. Every lesson is recorded, and you'll get a note afterwards on what was covered and what's next."
  - q: "Does a dyslexia or ADHD diagnosis change how English tutoring works?"
    a: "It shapes it, yes. English is often where dyslexia shows most, because so much of the paper is reading and writing at speed. If your child has extra time or a reader in exams, tell us, so the tutor can practise under the same conditions. Planning in bullet points on the whiteboard before writing, and colour-coding the parts of an answer, both help a lot of children. We'll suggest tutors who've worked with dyslexia or ADHD before."
  - q: "What if my child flatly refuses to show a tutor their writing?"
    a: "Then start with reading rather than writing. A tutor can spend the first lesson or two talking through an extract with your child, with nothing to hand in. Most teenagers relax once they can see the tutor's on their side. Your child can also look at the profiles with you and choose who to meet on the free video call. If a few weeks in it still isn't working, you just stop, as nothing's signed."
