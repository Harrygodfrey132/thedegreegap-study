  - q: "Why is my bright child finding GCSE English so hard?"
    a: "Usually because the essay has its own rules, and learning them takes practice with close feedback, which is hard to get with a whole class waiting. The texts get covered thoroughly. But knowing a book and scoring on a question about it are two different skills, and that gap is where most marks go. The classic sign is a child who talks brilliantly about a text, then writes a description of it. The other is timing: one strong paragraph, then two rushed ones as the clock runs down. Both are fixable, and faster than a reading problem would be."
  - q: "How much work will my child have between English lessons?"
    a: "Twenty to forty minutes is plenty. What they do in that time matters far more than how long it takes. One planned paragraph written to time beats an hour of rereading notes, because English marks go on how an argument's built, not on how much of the text your child can recall. If timing's the problem, short timed bursts two or three times a week do more than one long sitting. Most tutors set the task at the end of the lesson, aimed at what they've just worked on, and mark it before the next one."
  - q: "My child has dyslexia or ADHD. Can an English tutor still help?"
    a: "Yes. Several tutors on our platform regularly work with children who have dyslexia or ADHD, and in English the help is mostly about structure. Your child gets a planning frame that keeps the argument on track, and a set paragraph shape so they aren't inventing one from scratch each time. Long answers get broken into named parts, which is far less daunting than a blank page. If your child has extra time in exams, the tutor will work on how to use it, because that's often where the real gains are. Tell us on the call what school has in place and we'll match with that in mind."
