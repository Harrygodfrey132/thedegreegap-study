faq_1_q: "My son is joining a new sixth form in September. Should he start tutoring straight away?"
faq_1_a: "Give him three or four weeks to settle, then look hard at the first piece of assessed work. Students who move schools at sixteen are adjusting to new teachers, a new timetable and sometimes a different exam board from the one they sat at GCSE, and the first term covers ground quickly. If that first assessment shows a gap, starting in October means it gets closed before the November and January assessments that start shaping his prediction."
faq_2_q: "How much does the Year 12 work count towards what universities see?"
faq_2_a: "More than most families realise. Predicted grades are usually written in the autumn of Year 13 and lean heavily on Year 12 assessments and the end-of-year exams. Those predictions go on the UCAS application, and offers get made on them months before the real papers are sat. So a Year 12 that goes well is not just good practice. It is most of the evidence a school has when it writes the number."
faq_3_q: "She did well at GCSE. Why does A-Level feel so much harder?"
faq_3_a: "Because the questions stop pointing at the method. At GCSE a well-prepared student learns to recognise question types and apply the matching routine. A-Level papers mix topics inside one question, reward the reasoning written on the page more than the final line, and give very little away in the wording. Strong GCSE students often feel this first because recognition served them well. A tutor works on the choosing and the written reasoning rather than piling on more content."
