faq_1_q: "My son's joining a new sixth form in September. Should he start tutoring straight away?"
faq_1_a: "Not on day one. Give him three or four weeks to settle, then have a proper look at the first piece of marked work. At sixteen he's getting used to new teachers, a new timetable and sometimes a different exam board from GCSE. And the first term covers a lot of ground. If that first assessment shows a gap, starting in October leaves time to close it before the November and January assessments, which begin to shape your son's predicted grade."
faq_2_q: "Does Year 12 actually count for university?"
faq_2_a: "Yes, more than most families expect. Predicted grades are usually written in the autumn of Year 13, and they lean heavily on Year 12 assessments and the end-of-year exams. Those predictions go on your child's UCAS form, and universities make offers on them months before the real exams. So Year 12 is where most of the evidence comes from when the school sits down to write the number."
faq_3_q: "She did well at GCSE. Why does A-Level feel so much harder?"
faq_3_a: "Mostly because the questions stop pointing your daughter towards the method. At GCSE, a well-prepared teenager learns to recognise each kind of question and the routine that answers it. A-Level papers mix topics inside one question. They reward the reasoning written on the page more than the final line, and the wording gives very little away. Children who did well at GCSE often feel it first, because recognising the question used to be enough. So the tutor works on choosing a method and writing the reasoning down. More content isn't the answer."
