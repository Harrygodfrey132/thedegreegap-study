faqs:
  - q: "What is the hardest part of GCSE English for Bishop's Stortford students?"
    a: "Unseen poetry and the timing of the Literature paper cause the most trouble. Unseen poems can't be revised, only practised, so students without a clear method freeze. Literature essays demand accurate quotation from memory and an argument about a theme, not a retold plot, and many students run out of time on the second question. In Language, the final writing task suffers because it comes last. Each of these is a technique problem rather than a reading problem, which is why timed practice moves it quickly."
  - q: "Why is my child struggling with GCSE English when they read a lot?"
    a: "Because the exam rewards a skill that reading alone doesn't build: writing a planned, timed argument about a text. Many keen readers understand the books well and still write essays that summarise instead of analyse, or run out of time before reaching the best point. Others never learnt to plan, so every essay is written as it comes. A tutor's first job is finding which of these it is, using a recent essay or mock, and then practising that one habit."
  - q: "How much GCSE English homework should there be between sessions?"
    a: "Around an hour a week is usually enough, and more rarely helps. Typically that is one timed piece, such as an essay paragraph, a poetry comparison or a Language question, which the tutor marks before the next session, plus a few minutes a day on quotations. Being marked closely is worth more in English than volume. A student writing one essay a week that gets proper feedback tends to improve faster than one writing three that nobody reads."
  - q: "Can a tutor support a child with dyslexia or ADHD in English?"
    a: "Yes, and English is often where the support matters most, because the subject leans heavily on reading speed and sustained writing. Tutors break essays into small named stages, use visual plans on the whiteboard and build timing up gradually rather than all at once. Each Lessonspace session is recorded, so your child can revisit an explanation instead of relying on notes. Tell us about any diagnosis or exam access arrangements on the first call, and we match with that in mind."
  - q: "Can I sit in on the first English session?"
    a: "Yes, and most tutors are happy for a parent to join the first lesson online. It gives you a sense of how the hour is structured and how your child responds. Most parents step back after that, because teenagers tend to open up and ask more questions without an audience. You still hear what happened: after every session you get a short written note of what was covered, how it went and what was set for the week."
