faqs:
  - q: "What trips up Bishop's Stortford teenagers most in GCSE English?"
    a: "Unseen poetry and running out of time on the Literature paper, most years. Your child can't revise a poem they've never seen, only practise a way of reading one, and without that it's easy to freeze. The Literature essays want quotations from memory and an argument about a theme, and lots of teenagers spend so long on the first question that the second gets squeezed. Then in Language the final writing task gets the tired end of the exam. None of that's a reading problem. It's technique, so timed practice tends to shift it quite fast."
  - q: "My child reads loads. Why are they struggling with GCSE English?"
    a: "Reading builds understanding, but the exam marks something else: a planned argument, written against the clock. So a keen reader can know the book inside out and still hand in an essay that retells it rather than picking it apart, or save their best idea for the last five minutes. Some children also dive straight in without a plan, and the essay wanders. The tutor looks at a recent mock or essay to see which of these is going on, then works on that one habit until it sticks."
  - q: "How much will the tutor set between lessons?"
    a: "About an hour a week is usually plenty, and more rarely helps. That's normally one timed piece, like an essay paragraph, a poetry comparison or a Language question, which the tutor marks before the next lesson, plus a few minutes a day on quotations. In English, close marking is worth more than volume. One essay a week with proper feedback tends to do more than three that nobody reads carefully."
  - q: "Can a tutor help with English if my child has dyslexia or ADHD?"
    a: "Yes. English asks for a lot of reading at speed and long stretches of writing, so it's often the subject where support helps most. Tutors split an essay into small, named stages, sketch plans visually on the whiteboard and build timing up slowly. Every lesson in Lessonspace has a replay, so your child can rewatch an explanation rather than depend on notes. Let us know about any diagnosis or exam access arrangements when we first speak, and we'll match with that in mind."
  - q: "Can I sit in on my child's first English lesson?"
    a: "Yes, and most tutors are happy for a parent to join the first lesson online. You'll get a feel for how the hour runs and how your child takes to it. Most parents step back after that, because teenagers tend to open up and ask more questions without an audience. You won't be left guessing, though. After every lesson you get a short written note on what was covered, how it went and what's been set for the week."
