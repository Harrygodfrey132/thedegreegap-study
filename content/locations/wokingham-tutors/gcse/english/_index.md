faqs:
  - q: "Which part of GCSE English trips up Wokingham students most?"
    a: "Remembering quotations for the closed-book Literature exam, more than anything else. With AQA, which The Holt and Bohunt Wokingham have both published, your child needs quotations from four texts and fifteen poems with no book on the desk. Second comes timed writing in Language, where the plan, the writing and the checking all have to fit into about 45 minutes. Both improve quickly with the right kind of practice."
  - q: "How many English lessons before we'd notice a difference?"
    a: "Quotation recall often improves within three or four weeks, because it's mostly regular testing. Writing takes longer, often eight to twelve weekly lessons before the marks move up a band, since your child is changing habits as well as learning techniques. The first lesson works as a diagnostic, so afterwards the tutor can give you a rough idea of the timescale for your child."
  - q: "My child is only in Year 10. Is that too early for English tutoring?"
    a: "Not at all, and English suits an early start. Quotations learnt slowly through Year 10 stick far better than a list crammed in the spring of Year 11. It's also when many schools finish one or two of the Literature texts, so there's time to go back over them while they're fresh. At this stage, lessons feel less like exam prep and more like reading and talking about the books."
  - q: "My child is dyslexic. Can a GCSE English tutor still help?"
    a: "Yes. Mention it when we talk, and the tutors we put forward will have experience of teaching dyslexic students. Lessonspace helps, because essay plans and quotation lists sit on a shared screen your child can look back at, and every lesson can be replayed. Many tutors break an essay into fixed steps, so there's less to hold in mind at once. If your child gets extra time in exams, the tutor practises to that timing."
  - q: "My teenager insists they don't need help with English. What should we do?"
    a: "It's common with English, because most teenagers feel they can already read and write. A free 15-minute video call with the tutor usually takes the edge off, since your child sees it's a conversation, not a lesson in front of a class. Agree to try two or three lessons. If they still really don't want to carry on, stop, because it only works if your child is willing to use the hour."
