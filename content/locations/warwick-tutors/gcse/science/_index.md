And that spread matters far more to you than a county figure. Two children at neighbouring schools can be on different Science courses, with different amounts of lab time behind them. The comparison worth making is your child's latest paper against the one they'll actually sit. That's what the first lesson does, and unlike an average, it gives you something to work on this term."
  - q: "How do I find out which parts of Science my child is dropping marks on?"
    a: "Get the last marked paper out and look at which kind of question lost the marks, rather than at the total. If it's the short recall questions, there are gaps in the content. If it's graphs and data, it's usually a method your child hasn't had enough practice at yet. If it's the long six-mark answers, the knowledge is often there but the chain of reasoning isn't getting onto the page. Most children lean clearly one way. It takes five minutes, and the tutor does the same thing in more detail in the first lesson."
  - q: "How much Science work should my child do in the week between lessons?"
    a: "About twenty to thirty minutes, and it should be exam questions, not rereading. Answering cold and then marking against the scheme builds exactly what the paper tests. Rereading notes just builds the feeling of knowing it, and that doesn't carry into the exam. If data questions are the gap, two or three short graph questions a week do more than one long sit-down. Most tutors set the work at the end of the lesson so it follows on from what they've just covered, then mark it at the start of the next one."
