faqs:
  - q: "Which bits of GCSE Science trip up Redhill teenagers most?"
    a: "The ones where Maths and Science meet, more often than not. In Chemistry it's quantitative work, moles and concentrations on the Higher papers, and balancing equations. In Physics it's electricity and the equations that have to be rearranged before they can be used. In Biology it's inheritance, where a Punnett square has to turn into a probability. And across all three, the required practical questions. A marked mock usually shows within one lesson which of these is costing your child most."
  - q: "Will my child need three tutors for three sciences?"
    a: "Rarely. For Combined Science, plenty of tutors on our platform teach all three at GCSE, and one tutor keeps things simple for your child. For triple Science, where the papers go further, we sometimes suggest a separate tutor for the science that's furthest behind, often Physics or Chemistry, and leave the rest to school. Harry or Joe will say which they'd suggest for your child when you speak."
  - q: "What does a GCSE Science lesson with a tutor actually look like?"
    a: "An hour, in three rough parts. The first ten or fifteen minutes go on last week's homework, marked, with any slips talked through. The middle half hour is new ground: a practical drawn out and explained, or a type of calculation practised until it's routine. The last part is real exam questions on that topic, timed, then marked against the mark scheme so your child sees exactly where marks are won."
  - q: "How do you pick your Science tutors?"
    a: "Harry or Joe interview every one, and only about 3% of applicants make it onto the platform. Before that there's a written application, a check on subject knowledge and references. The interview is the hard part. We ask tutors to explain something like a titration or a series circuit to someone meeting it for the first time, because knowing Science and teaching it to a fifteen-year-old aren't the same thing."
  - q: "My child has decided they're 'not a science person'. Is tutoring worth a try?"
    a: "Usually, yes, and it's one of the most common things parents tell us. That feeling tends to start with one topic that didn't make sense, often in Year 9 Chemistry, and grows from there. A tutor goes back to that point and lets your child get a few things right. Your child meets the tutor on a free video call first, and if after a couple of lessons it really isn't working, you can stop."
