faqs:
  - q: "Which English questions cost Reigate children the most marks?"
    a: "Comparison, more than anything else. On AQA, Language Paper 2 asks your child to compare two non-fiction sources from different centuries, and Literature asks them to compare poems. In both, lots of children write two separate mini-essays and lose the marks for linking them. Unseen poetry comes next, because there's no text to revise. Then there's timing on the final writing task, which arrives when concentration has already dipped. All three get better quickly once they're practised head-on."
  - q: "My child reads all the time. So why is GCSE English such a struggle?"
    a: "Because reading well and writing an exam answer about reading are different skills. Keen readers often retell rather than analyse, or spot something clever and forget to explain what it does. Others have plenty to say but no plan, so the best point turns up in the last paragraph. In the first lesson the tutor looks at a marked answer to see which it is. Each needs a different fix, and none of them means your child lacks ability."
  - q: "How much English homework will there be between lessons?"
    a: "An hour or so. Normally it's a single past-paper question written to time on that week's topic, and a handful of Literature quotations to learn. More than that rarely helps, since English gets better through feedback on each piece, not through sheer volume. The tutor marks it before the next lesson, so you'll find each lesson opens with what went well, what didn't and the one thing to change next time."
  - q: "How do you choose the English tutors?"
    a: "Harry or Joe interview every one of them, and only around 3% of the people who apply get onto the platform. Each sends a written application, has their subject knowledge checked, then has that interview, and we take up references. The interview's where most fall away, because it tests whether they can explain something like embedding a quotation to a teenager who can't do it yet. Plenty of people write good essays. Far fewer can teach it."
  - q: "Can I sit in on my child's English lesson?"
    a: "Yes, and some parents like to for the first one. As lessons are online, you can sit next to your child or join from another room. Most parents step back after that, because teenagers tend to talk more openly about their writing without an audience. You'll still get a note after each lesson saying what was covered and what was set, and there's a replay of every lesson if you'd like to see how it went."
