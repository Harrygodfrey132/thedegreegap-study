faqs:
  - q: "What is the hardest part of GCSE English for Reigate students?"
    a: "Comparison, more than anything. On AQA, Language Paper 2 asks students to compare two non-fiction sources from different centuries, and Literature asks them to compare poems, and in both cases many students write two separate mini-essays and lose the marks for linking. Unseen poetry is the next casualty, because there is no text to revise. Then timing on the final writing task, done when concentration has already dropped. All three improve quickly once they are practised directly rather than hoped for."
  - q: "Why is my child struggling with GCSE English when they read a lot?"
    a: "Because reading well and writing an exam answer about reading are different skills. Keen readers often summarise or retell rather than analyse, or they notice something clever and forget to explain its effect. Others have plenty to say but no plan, so the best point arrives in the last paragraph. The first session looks at a marked answer to see which of these it is. Each needs a different fix, and none of them is a sign of weak ability."
  - q: "How much GCSE English homework should there be between lessons?"
    a: "About an hour, sometimes a little more. Usually that means one timed answer to a past-paper question on whatever the lesson covered, plus a short quotation-learning task for Literature. More than that rarely helps, because English improves through feedback on each piece rather than volume. The tutor marks the answer before the next lesson, so the session opens with what went right, what didn't, and the one change to make next time."
  - q: "How do you vet your GCSE English tutors?"
    a: "Around 3% of applicants make it onto the platform. Each one sends a written application, has their subject knowledge checked and then sits a structured interview with Harry or Joe, followed by references. The interview is where most people fall away, because it tests whether they can explain a technique like embedding a quotation clearly to someone who doesn't yet do it. Strong essay-writers are common. Tutors who can teach it are much rarer."
  - q: "Can I sit in on the GCSE English lesson?"
    a: "Yes, and some parents like to for the first one. Because lessons run online, you can sit alongside your child or join from another room. Most parents step back after that, since teenagers usually talk more openly about their writing without an audience. You still get a note after each lesson with what was covered and what was set, and every session is recorded if you want to see how it went."
