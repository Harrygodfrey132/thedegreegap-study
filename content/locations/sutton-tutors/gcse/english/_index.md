A borough-wide average says nothing about one teenager on one paper, and in English the spread inside a single class is wide. What helps more is putting your child's last marked answer next to the grade bands for the paper they're sitting. That's what a first lesson does. And unlike a borough figure, it gives you something to act on."
  - q: "My child reads all the time. Why are they struggling with GCSE English?"
    a: "Because reading builds the raw material, not the technique, and that's why keen readers can still write thin answers. The change usually comes around Year 8, well before GCSE. Essays stop being marked for what you know and start being marked for the argument you build. In a full class there's rarely time to go through that shift one-to-one with every child. So a teenager who hasn't got the hang of planning writes everything down in the order it occurred to them. The first week works out whether that's what's going on, or something else."
  - q: "Why is my child's predicted English grade lower than we thought it'd be?"
    a: "Usually it's the gap between what your child understands when they talk about a text and what gets onto the page under exam timing. Predictions come from mocks and teacher judgement. Handwriting speed, planning time, an opening paragraph that circles before it argues: each of those costs marks that knowledge can't win back. Ask school for the marked script, not just the number. It shows where the answer stopped scoring, and that makes the next conversation a useful one."
