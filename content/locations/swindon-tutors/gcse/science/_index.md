  - q: "What actually happens in a Science lesson?"
    a: "It's an hour, in three parts. First, about fifteen minutes on last week's questions, marked properly so any misunderstandings come out instead of staying hidden. Then half an hour of teaching, on whatever topic or skill the first lesson pointed to. The method gets written out on the shared whiteboard, not just talked through. The last fifteen minutes are fresh exam questions on that topic, marked against the real mark scheme while your child watches. Next week's questions get set at the end, so they follow straight on."
  - q: "How much work should my child do between lessons, and what kind?"
    a: "About twenty to thirty minutes, and it's best as exam questions, not reading. Answering a question with the notes shut, then marking it against the mark scheme, practises exactly what the paper tests. Rereading builds the feeling of knowing, which isn't the same thing and doesn't carry over into the exam hall. If the practicals (the set experiments the exam asks about) are costing marks, little and often works best. A few short bursts a week on variables, equipment and sources of error do more than one long sitting. Most tutors set the work at the end of the lesson, so your child isn't left guessing what to revise."
  - q: "What if my child has dyslexia or ADHD? Can Science tutoring still work?"
    a: "Yes. In Science the things that help tend to be small and specific. Setting out every calculation the same way, so nothing gets lost halfway through. Learning each practical as a numbered list of steps and variables, not a paragraph. Drawing a process as a diagram on the whiteboard instead of a block of text. Several tutors on our platform already work this way. It helps most if you tell us on the call what already works at school, including any access arrangements. Then the tutor can build on it from the first lesson."
  - q: "Can I sit in on the lessons?"
    a: "Of course. Lots of parents do for the first one, just to see how it works. After that we'd usually suggest stepping back, because most teenagers relax more without an audience. You'll still know what's going on, though. Every lesson has a replay, so you can watch any part of it later. And the tutor sends a short note on what was covered and what's been set for the week. If you'd like a proper chat about progress, just ask. You don't have to wait for a set time."
