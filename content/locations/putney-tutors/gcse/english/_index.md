faqs:
  - q: "What do Putney teenagers usually find hardest about GCSE English?"
    a: "Usually the writing, even for keen readers. The reading questions reward close attention, which plenty of children already have. The writing tasks reward planning and control against the clock, which most haven't practised much. In Literature, the toughest moment tends to be the unseen poem or passage, because there's no text to revise in advance. Putney High's Cambridge course has an unseen paper of its own, and AQA's unseen poetry closes its second paper. A tutor works on both through short timed practice."
  - q: "Why does my child do well in other subjects but struggle in English?"
    a: "Because English marks how a point is made as well as the point itself. In Maths or Science a right answer earns its marks. In English, two answers making the same point can land a band apart, depending on how far the idea is developed and supported. Children who like right answers can find that unsettling. What helps is a structure they trust, for planning and for opening a paragraph, practised until it holds under time."
  - q: "Does my child need a tutor, or would more reading be enough?"
    a: "Reading helps, but on its own it rarely moves an English grade. Look at a recent marked essay. If the comments are about ideas, more reading and talking about books may do the job. If they say 'develop this' or mention structure, or the piece ends unfinished, it's a technique gap, and that's where a tutor is quickest. If your child is already scoring well across mocks, a few targeted lessons before the exams may be plenty."
  - q: "My child is dyslexic. Can an English tutor still help?"
    a: "Yes. Tell us on the call and we'll suggest tutors who have worked with dyslexic students. Lessons can use short, clear steps, planning grids on the shared whiteboard and talking an answer through before writing it, which takes the pressure off spelling while ideas take shape. Every lesson is recorded, so your child can go back over an explanation instead of relying on notes. If they have extra time in exams, the tutor can practise with it."
  - q: "My teenager says English tutoring would be pointless. How do we get past that?"
    a: "It's a very common reaction, and it usually softens once they've met someone. Your child can talk to the tutor on a free 15-minute video call first, with no obligation. One person, full attention and nobody else's work to compare with feels nothing like a classroom. Give it two or three lessons. If your child still doesn't want to carry on after that, we stop, and it's their call."
