faqs:
  - q: "Year 12 or Year 13, when is the right time to start A-Level Maths tutoring?"
    a: "Year 12, and the gap between the two is wider than most families expect. Predicted grades come out of Year 12 performance and reach universities early in Year 13, so work landing then counts for the application as well as the exam. Starting in Year 13 still improves the final grade and plenty of families do it, but the offers have usually been made against a number set earlier."
  - q: "Why is my child's Maths predicted grade lower than I expected?"
    a: "Predictions rest on mock performance and teacher judgement, and at A-Level the common gap is between following a method and choosing one unprompted. A student can be fluent in lessons and lost in a paper where nothing announces the topic. Reading the actual script rather than the grade is the only way to see which it is, and it is also what makes a conversation with school productive rather than adversarial."
  - q: "What if my child does not want a tutor?"
    a: "At seventeen it deserves proper weight, because they are close to running their own work and being overruled rarely helps. Most resistance comes before they have met anybody and is about what accepting help seems to say. The free 15-minute video call lets your child form their own view and turn down a particular tutor without turning down the idea. If they still do not want it, pause."
  - q: "Can an A-Level Maths tutor help a child with dyslexia, ADHD or processing differences?"
    a: "Yes, and a number of tutors do this regularly. The practical help is about reducing load: a consistent layout so multi-step working does not get lost, long questions broken into named stages, and one method worked until automatic. Timed practice is introduced gradually where exam conditions are the difficulty. Tell us on the call what school already has in place, including exam access arrangements."
  - q: "What is the hardest part of A-Level Maths for York students?"
    a: "The step at the start of Year 12, where a question stops announcing which method it wants and the student has to choose. Strong GCSE performers often find this hardest, because pattern-matching carried them a long way and suddenly has nothing to match. After that the losses concentrate in whichever strand gets neglected, usually Mechanics or Statistics depending on temperament."
