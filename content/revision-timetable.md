## How to make a revision timetable that works

A revision timetable has one job. It decides in advance what gets revised and when, so nothing is left until the week before the exam. The planner above does the sums for you, and these are the rules it follows. They're worth knowing if you'd rather draw your own.

**Start from your first exam and work backwards.** The summer 2027 exams are due to start on Monday 10 May 2027. The number of weeks between today and your first paper is your budget, and every subject has to fit inside it.

**List your subjects, then the topics inside them.** A subject is too big to revise in one go. Use the [topic checklist](#rt-downloads) to break each one into topics, and rate every topic red, amber or green.

**Give weaker subjects more time, but not all of it.** The planner gives a subject that needs the most work three sessions for every one a confident subject gets. Confident subjects still need regular practice, or they quietly slip.

**Keep sessions short.** Thirty to 45 minutes, then a break of five to ten minutes. A two-hour session mostly adds tired minutes at the end.

**Mix subjects across the day.** Two or three different subjects in an evening feels harder than one subject for three hours. It works better, though, because each switch makes you pull the knowledge back out again.

**Test yourself rather than re-reading.** Flashcards, practice questions and past papers do far more than re-reading notes or highlighting. A large review of study techniques by Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing and spaced practice as the two most useful methods, and rated highlighting and re-reading as low.

**Plan your rest.** Keep one evening a week free and stop at the same time each night. A plan you can keep is worth more than an ambitious one you drop after a fortnight.

**Check it every Sunday.** Move anything you missed, rate your topics again and adjust next week. Don't start again from scratch.

## How many hours a day should you revise?

There's no magic number, and anyone who gives you one without asking about your week is guessing. As a starting point that most Year 11s can keep up, aim for one to two hours on a school night and three to four hours on a weekend day, in short sessions with breaks. In the Easter holidays most students can manage more, as long as the sessions stay short.

A-Level subjects are much bigger, so Year 12 and 13 students usually need more time for each subject than they did at GCSE, even though they take fewer of them.

If the hours are there but the marks aren't moving, look at what happens in the sessions before you add more. An hour of testing yourself does more than three hours of reading through notes.

## When should you start revising for GCSEs?

Most schools hold Year 11 mocks between November and January. A light timetable from the autumn means mocks become practice for the real thing rather than a shock. After February half term, step it up. By the Easter holidays you want the full plan running, with past papers done against the clock.

Starting early isn't really about putting in more hours. It's about spacing. A topic you come back to four or five times between October and May stays with you, and a topic crammed in the last fortnight mostly doesn't.

## For parents: helping without nagging

Let your child make the timetable themselves. A plan they built is one they're far more likely to follow than one handed to them.

Agree it once, then check in once a week, on a Sunday, rather than every evening. And ask "what did you test yourself on?" rather than "have you revised?" The first question has an answer you can talk about.

If one subject keeps dropping off the plan, it's usually the one that feels hardest, not the one your child cares least about. That's worth a conversation with the subject teacher.
