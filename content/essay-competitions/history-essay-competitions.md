By the spring of Year 12 most History students have done the Tudors, the Nazis and the Cold War, and some of them have found the thing they actually care about, which is usually none of those. The competitions on this page are where that goes. Two of them, Julia Wood and Robson, are judged by Oxford and Cambridge historians and close in late July, which suits a Year 12 timetable well.

## The Julia Wood History Essay Competition

St Hugh's College, Oxford runs the Julia Wood competition for students in Year 12 or 13 who have been in the sixth form no more than two years, including those on a gap year. The essay can be on any historical period and runs to between 2,000 and 4,000 words, which is the most generous limit on this page and a trap for anyone who mistakes length for depth. The prize is up to £500.

The 2026 deadline was 2pm on 22 July, and the college reported 1,039 entries, so this is a large field for a college prize. St Hugh's runs the Mary Renault Classical Reception competition alongside it, for essays on how the ancient world has been used since, with the same dates and limits.

## The Robson History Prize

Trinity College, Cambridge sets the Robson questions in March and closes the prize at 12 noon on the last working day of July, with no late entries. Year 12 students write no more than 3,000 words including footnotes. Trinity gives half of each prize to the student and half to their school as book tokens: £600 for first and £400 for second.

Robson questions are historians' questions. They tend to ask about causation, continuity and the use of evidence rather than about a period, and the college fellows who mark them are reading for an argument they could disagree with. A student who has read one serious historian on their chosen problem is at a large advantage.

## The other college prizes

Fitzwilliam College, Cambridge set History and Medieval World questions in its 2026 competitions, at 2,500 words with a deadline of 27 February and a first prize of £200. Newnham College runs a History Essay Prize for women in Year 12 from any school or country, with prizes of £400, £200 and £100 and an early June deadline. Magdalene College's Armstrong competition, for state-school students in Year 12 and 13, includes history among its twelve questions and closed on 26 April 2026. Oriel College, Oxford's Rex Nettleford Essay Prize asks Year 12 students to write on colonialism and its legacies, with two prizes of £250 and a February deadline. Dates and rules for all of these are on the [Oxford and Cambridge college prizes page](/study/essay-competitions/oxford-and-cambridge-college-essay-competitions/).

## John Locke History

The History category of the [John Locke essay competition](/study/essay-competitions/john-locke-essay-competition/) asks different questions from the college prizes. They're broader, more provocative and marked by a panel of senior academics in a field above 100,000. In 2026 they asked whether the arc of the moral universe really bends toward justice, what the world might look like if the Library of Alexandria hadn't burned, and whether Che deserves his iconic T-shirt. In 2025 one question put Bertrand Russell's line that Hitler was an outcome of Rousseau and Churchill of Locke to the test. The limit is 2,000 words, the deadline was 31 May in 2026, and the questions come out in February.

The Che question is a good example of how these work. The weak essay is a biography. The strong one decides what a T-shirt is for, and then asks whether Guevara's record earns it.

## The Concord Review

The Concord Review is an American quarterly that has published history research papers by secondary school students for decades. Papers run from 5,000 to well over 10,000 words with full endnotes and bibliography, the average published piece is more than 8,000 words, and submission requires an author membership, which is a fee. Publication is the prize. The review also awards the Emerson Prize each year to selected published authors.

It's the most demanding thing on this page by a distance, and it's the right target for a small number of students: the ones who've done an EPQ or something like it, have a genuine research question and a real set of sources, and want the work read by someone who takes it seriously. For everyone else, Julia Wood first.

## What historians reward

Every one of these prizes is judged by people who teach History at a university, and they read for the same things.

**An argument, not a narrative.** The story of what happened is the raw material. The essay is what the student thinks it means, stated in the first paragraph and held to the end. School History rewards the story. These competitions don't.

**A debate named.** Historians disagree, and the student who knows which historians disagree about their question, and picks a side, is doing university History. Thompson against the older labour historians on working-class agency. Beard against the narrative tradition on Roman citizenship. Olusoga on which archives have been read and which are still in the box.

**A source read properly.** One primary source, a parish register, a Hansard debate, a pamphlet on Early English Books Online, examined for who made it, who it leaves out and why it survived, is worth more than ten sources summarised.

**Somewhere the syllabus didn't go.** The judges have read the Third Reich essay four hundred times. Song China, the Haitian Revolution, the Mughal economy or English local history through its records is read by someone grateful for the change.

**Counterfactuals done with discipline.** John Locke's Library of Alexandria question invites a fantasy. The strong answer sets out what the library actually held, what was already copied elsewhere, and what the loss did and didn't change, before it speculates.

**The word limit respected.** Julia Wood's 4,000 words is a ceiling, not a target. Robson counts footnotes. A tutor's most useful job in July is cutting.
