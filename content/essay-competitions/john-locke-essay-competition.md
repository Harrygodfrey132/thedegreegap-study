Most parents first hear about it from a school email in February, or from another parent whose child was shortlisted last year. The questions have just come out. Somebody's daughter is entering Philosophy. Yours has a spare Sunday and a plan to "look at the questions". By May the plan hasn't moved, and the deadline is a fortnight away.

That's the John Locke essay competition for a lot of families. Enormous, prestigious, and easy to leave too late. This page sets out how it works and what the judges say they want, and then how we support a student through an entry they can honestly call their own.

## What the John Locke essay competition is

The John Locke Institute is an independent educational organisation based in Oxford. It runs residential courses in philosophy, politics, economics and history with academics from Oxford, Princeton and other universities, and its Global Essay Prize has grown from a few thousand entries into the largest essay competition open to school students anywhere. The Institute reported more than 60,000 entries from over 190 countries in 2025. Its 2026 shortlist letters put the field above 100,000.

Two things follow from that scale. A shortlisting is a genuine achievement that a school reference can name, and a commendation or a category prize is rarer still. And the judges are reading quickly. An essay that spends its first 400 words explaining what the question means has already lost them.

## Who can enter, and the ten categories

The prize is open to students anywhere in the world who are under 19 on the submission deadline. There's no lower age limit. Entrants under 15 are judged in a separate Junior division against their own age group, and in 2026 they answered the same questions as everyone else rather than a separate junior list, as in earlier years.

The 2026 competition ran ten categories: Philosophy, Politics, Economics, History, Psychology, Theology and Law, plus three new ones in International Relations, Public Policy, and Science and Technology. Each category publishes three questions. A student answers one question per essay, and can enter more than one category if they have the time. Most don't.

Every essay also needs an academic referee, usually a teacher, who isn't a relative and who knows the student's written work. The Institute contacts referees to confirm the essay is the student's own, so this isn't a box to leave until the last day.

## The 2026 dates, and why you should check them every year

For the 2026 prize the Institute published the questions on 2 February, closed registration on 31 March, opened submissions on 1 April and set the deadline at 31 May. Late entries were accepted for a fee: £25 for a further week, £75 for three weeks. Shortlisted students heard in early July, and the prizes were presented at an awards dinner in London on 3 October.

That timetable ran roughly a month earlier than 2025, when submissions closed on 30 June. So the shape of the year is stable but the dates aren't. If a student is planning around the competition, the official page is the only source worth trusting, and it's worth reading in January, before the questions appear.

## The prizes, and what they're worth

In 2026 each category offered a first prize of US$5,000, a second of US$2,000 and a third of US$1,000, with Junior and Senior entrants judged separately. The best essay overall wins the Grand Prize: a US$10,000 scholarship and an honorary Junior Fellowship of the Institute.

Parents should know what the money is. The prizes are scholarships towards the Institute's own summer schools and visiting-scholar programmes, not cash. Below the prizes sit three tiers of commendation, and below those the shortlist, which in 2026 took in roughly the top sixth of eligible entries. Entering is free. The awards conference and dinner in London are optional and ticketed, and a student can win without travelling.

## What the judges say they reward

The Institute is unusually direct about its criteria. Essays are judged on knowledge and understanding of the relevant material, competent use of evidence, quality of argument, originality, structure, writing style and persuasive force. The judges are senior academics drawn from universities including Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard, Princeton and Stanford, and the marking is anonymous.

The line that matters most is the Institute's own: the best essays are the ones capable of changing somebody's mind. Not the ones that cover the most ground. Not the ones with the longest bibliography. A 16-year-old who picks a side on whether trial by jury is obsolete, and holds it against the strongest objection, beats a 17-year-old who surveys the debate and concludes that it's complicated.

## The questions

Three per category, and they're written to provoke. A sample from 2026:

- Philosophy: Is it ever wrong to do the right thing for the wrong reasons?
- Economics: Should we fear a cashless society? And: Did Jeff Bezos get rich at the expense of his customers, his employees, neither or both?
- History: Does Che deserve his iconic T-shirt?
- Law: Is trial by jury obsolete?
- Politics: Is democracy in crisis?
- Psychology: Is mental illness over-diagnosed now, or just better recognised?
- Theology: If you achieve enlightenment, how will you know?
- Public Policy: Should vaccination be mandatory in a public health emergency?
- Science and Technology: Should we be polite to ChatGPT?

Earlier years were in the same spirit. In 2025, Economics asked what the UK government's plan to put VAT on school fees would do to social mobility, and Law asked whether Vladimir Putin is a war criminal. The questions read like dinner-table arguments. They're marked like undergraduate essays.

## Where entries fall down

The problems in a competition draft are the ones we see in A-Level essays every week, magnified by the word limit.

**The question that sounds clever.** Students pick the question with the most famous name in it, then discover they have nothing to say about Che Guevara that a Wikipedia editor hasn't. The right question is the one where the student already disagrees with somebody.

**Summary dressed as argument.** Two thousand words is short. An essay that spends 800 of them setting out both sides has 1,200 left to make its case, and usually makes it in the final paragraph, in a hurry.

**Evidence that decorates.** A quotation from Mill proves the student has read Mill. It proves nothing about the question unless the essay does something with it. Judges can tell a source used from a source cited.

**Citations that eat the limit.** In-text references count towards the 2,000 words. Endnotes and the bibliography don't, and footnotes aren't permitted at all. A student who learns this on the last night loses a paragraph.

**The last-fortnight draft.** The questions come out in February. An entry written in April and cut in May has had two rounds of honest feedback. One written in the final week has had none, and it reads that way.

## Originality, tutors and AI

Every essay must be entirely the work of one student, backed by a signed authorship declaration and a referee who can vouch for it. The Institute says it uses various means to detect plagiarism, contract cheating and AI use, that it may ask a student to discuss their entry with a member of its faculty, and that it tells the school if it finds a problem.

On AI the rules have moved. Earlier years banned it outright. The Institute's 2026 guidance allowed students to use large language models to develop their thinking and stress-test an argument, as research support and a thought partner rather than a writer, and warned that essays that read as heavily AI-influenced or unoriginal may be judged less favourably. Read the current year's wording before a student opens any tool. Our own rule is simpler. The student writes every sentence, and a tutor's job is to argue with them, not for them.

That's also the honest answer to what tutoring is for here. Help choosing a question the student can actually argue. Two or three things to read rather than twenty. A session on structure before a word is written. And drafts marked the way the judges will read them, by someone who studied the subject at university.
