There's a type of sixth former who argues about power. Not party politics, or not only. Why the council can close a library. Whether a referendum settles anything. Why one country's election went one way and its neighbour's the other. That student usually ends up applying for Politics, PPE, International Relations or History, and the question is where to put the arguing before then.

The competitions below are the answer. They fall into three kinds: the college prize set by a university department, the very large international prize, and the student-run competitions, one free and one paid, that sit somewhere in between.

## The R.A. Butler Prize

Trinity College, Cambridge runs the R.A. Butler Prize with the university's Department of Politics and International Studies, and for a UK Year 12 it's the best fit on this page. Students in their penultimate year of school, from any country, write up to 3,000 words including footnotes on one of a set of topics released in March. The deadline in 2026 was 12 noon on 31 July, and the college doesn't accept late entries. The prize drew 1,040 entries that year.

The first prize is £600 and the second £400, each split down the middle with the student's school, which takes its half in book tokens. What matters more is the commendation list: the department typically names around eight special commendations and forty commendations, so a good essay has a realistic chance of a line a school reference can use. The next topics are due in March 2027.

## John Locke: Politics, International Relations and Public Policy

The [John Locke essay competition](/study/essay-competitions/john-locke-essay-competition/) had one Politics category for years. In 2026 it added International Relations and Public Policy, so a student who thinks about power now has three sets of three questions to choose from, all at 2,000 words, all closing on the same day: 31 May in 2026, with the questions out in February.

The 2026 Politics questions asked whether the right to self-determination is absolute, whether the pandemic normalised authoritarianism, and whether democracy is in crisis. Public Policy asked what discount rate should apply to long-run environmental policy, which unintended consequence of policy was most devastating and why nobody predicted it, and whether vaccination should be mandatory in a public health emergency. International Relations asked, among other things, whether a coalition of countries or of billionaires should try a libertarian microstate. They read like provocations. They're marked like undergraduate essays, by a panel of senior academics, and the field is above 100,000.

## The LSE Undergraduate Political Review competition

The LSE Undergraduate Political Review is a student-run journal at the London School of Economics, and each autumn it runs a free essay competition for students in Year 12 and 13, from any country. The limit is 1,000 words, with citations and footnotes counted and only the title and bibliography excluded, which makes it a tight exercise in saying one thing. The prizes are publication on the review's blog, an invitation to its conference and a certificate.

It's smaller than the others and it's judged by undergraduates rather than academics. That's not a reason to skip it. For a student who has never written to a hard word limit, a thousand words on a political question, published if it's good, is a very useful autumn project. The competition is announced on the review's blog in September, and the deadline moves, so check there.

## The Harvard International Review contest

The Harvard International Review is a student publication at Harvard, and its Academic Writing Contest is the one competition on this page that charges to enter. Secondary students worldwide write 800 to 1,200 words on an international affairs topic, register and pay before submitting, and the strongest pieces receive gold, silver and bronze medals. The contest runs three cycles a year, so there's almost always a deadline within a few months.

Parents should weigh it honestly. A medal is a line on a form, and international students in particular value it. But it's a paid competition run by students, and a UK admissions tutor will give more weight to a free prize judged by a university department. If a student has the R.A. Butler and John Locke in hand, this is a reasonable third. It isn't a substitute for either.

## What political scientists reward

Every judge on this page, apart from the LSE undergraduates, teaches politics for a living, and they read in a particular way.

**A concept defined.** The questions turn on words: democracy, crisis, authoritarianism, self-determination. The essay that says what would count as a crisis, and why that definition rather than another, has done the first thing a political scientist does. The essay that assumes everyone agrees hasn't started.

**A comparison.** One case proves nothing. Two cases that differ in the thing the essay is about, and come out differently, prove something. A student writing on whether the pandemic normalised authoritarianism should be looking at two countries with different pandemic laws and asking what happened next.

**Evidence a reader could check.** Freedom House scores, the V-Dem democracy indices, turnout data from the Electoral Commission, a named statute, a named court judgment. Not an impression from the news.

**Theory used, not recited.** In international relations, realism and liberalism are tools for asking why states do things, not names to drop. One school of thought applied to the actual question beats a tour of all of them.

**The other side, stated properly.** These are contested questions, and the judges hold views. An essay that gives the strongest version of the opposing case and answers it reads as political science. One that ignores it reads as an op-ed.
