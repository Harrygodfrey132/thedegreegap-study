The college competitions are quieter than John Locke. No gala dinner, no six-figure field, and prizes measured in book tokens. But the person marking the essay is a fellow who teaches the subject at that college, and two years later may be reading the student's application. For a Year 12 with a subject in mind, that's the better trade.

Below is what ran in 2026, college by college, grouped by when the deadline falls. Every one of these changes its dates, and sometimes its questions, each year, so treat the dates as the usual pattern and check the college's own page before planning. Oxford keeps a [list of academic competitions for school students](https://www.ox.ac.uk/admissions/undergraduate/supercurricular-hub/academic-competitions-for-school-aged-students) that's worth cross-checking too.

## Spring deadlines: February to April

**St John's College, Oxford: Classics and Ancient History Essay Competition.** For Year 12 students at UK schools, and the student doesn't need to be studying a classical subject. One essay of up to 2,000 words on one of four questions covering literature, history, art and archaeology, and philosophy. The 2026 deadline was 22 February. Prizes are book tokens for the best essay in each category, with extra for the overall winner and for the best essay by a student who hasn't studied a classical subject. Every entrant gets a certificate, and entrants can apply for the college's Classics Subject Exploration Day in April.

**Oriel College, Oxford: Rex Nettleford Essay Prize and Lloyd Davies Philosophy Prize.** The Rex Nettleford prize asks Year 12 students to write on colonialism and its legacies, with two equal prizes of £250. In 2026 it closed on 23 February. The Lloyd Davies Philosophy Prize, which has run since 2006, sets topics for Year 12 and takes essays of up to 2,500 words by email. Check Oriel's page for this year's dates on both.

**Fitzwilliam College, Cambridge: essay competitions in eight subjects.** In 2026 Fitzwilliam set questions in Ancient World and Classics, Archaeology, Economics, English, History, Land Economy, the Medieval World and Slavonic Studies. Year 12 or equivalent, one competition and one entry per student, and the 2026 page welcomed overseas entrants. Most subjects allow 2,500 words, including footnotes but not the bibliography. Economics is much shorter. The 2026 deadline was 27 February, with a first prize of £200 per subject, book vouchers for runners-up and a prize-giving in May.

**Girton College, Cambridge: Humanities Writing Competition.** Different from the rest. Year 12 students write about one of five objects from the college's Lawrence Room museum, either as an essay or as a creative piece such as a monologue or a story, in up to 1,800 words. The 2026 deadline was 20 March, with a £500 prize fund and £400 of books from Cambridge University Press. Entries for the next round open in the autumn.

**Trinity College, Cambridge: Robert Walker Prize for Essays in Law.** A topical legal question set by Trinity's law fellows, up to 2,000 words including footnotes, open to students in their final or penultimate year of school, with separate UK and international divisions. In 2026 the deadline was 2 April, earlier than Trinity's other prizes. The college anticipated first and second prizes of £300 and £200 in each division, and invites the top ten in each to a prize ceremony.

**Peterhouse, Cambridge: Kelvin Sciences Essay Competition.** The one science essay prize among the Cambridge colleges. Year 12 students educated in the UK or Ireland, up to 2,000 words including footnotes, with a £750 prize pool. The 2026 deadline was 20 April. Peterhouse used to run history and English prizes as well, the Vellacott and the Thomas Campion, but those haven't run since before 2021, so don't let an old school handout send a student chasing them.

**Magdalene College, Cambridge: Armstrong Arts and Humanities Essay Competition.** For Year 12 and 13 students at UK state schools only. Twelve questions across the arts and humanities subjects Cambridge teaches, 2,000 words not counting footnotes and bibliography, and a stated rule of no AI-generated content. The 2026 deadline was 26 April, with prizes of £300, £200 and £100 and a visit to the college for the winners.

## Summer deadlines: June and July

**Newnham College, Cambridge: eight essay prizes.** Newnham ran prizes in 2026 in Biological Sciences, Engineering, History, Modern and Medieval Languages, Music, Philosophy and Physical Sciences, plus the Woolf Essay Prize, its best known. All are for women in Year 12 or the equivalent, from any school in any country, with prizes of £400, £200 and £100 in each. The Woolf Prize closed on 8 June 2026. Each prize publishes its own questions and word limit in an information document, so read that before choosing.

**Queens' College, Cambridge: Estelle Prize for English.** A set question, up to 2,500 words including footnotes, references and captions, open to Year 12 or equivalent. The essay can't be classroom or coursework material. In 2026 the deadline was 3 July, with a £500 prize and honourable mentions. Queens' is strict about the cover sheet and the length, and rejects late or over-length entries.

**St Hugh's College, Oxford: Julia Wood History Essay Competition and Mary Renault Classical Reception Essay Competition.** Both take Year 12 and Year 13 students who have been in the sixth form no more than two years, and both allow a generous 2,000 to 4,000 words. Julia Wood is for history on any period. Mary Renault is for essays on the influence of the classical world in any later period, and doesn't require Latin or Greek. Prizes of up to £500. The 2026 deadline for both was 22 July, and Julia Wood drew 1,039 entries that year, so this is one of the larger college fields.

**Trinity College, Cambridge: the end-of-July prizes.** Trinity runs the best-known set, all for Year 12 or Lower Sixth, all closing at 12 noon UK time on the last working day of July in 2026, and all with late entries impossible:

- Robson History Prize: up to 3,000 words including footnotes, first prize £600 and second £400.
- Gould Prize for Essays in English Literature: 1,500 to 2,500 words, £600 and £400, with high commendations and commendations.
- R.A. Butler Politics Prize, run with the Department of Politics and International Studies: up to 3,000 words including references, £600 and £400, and typically around eight special commendations and forty commendations. Open to students from any country.
- Languages and Cultures Essay Prize: up to 3,000 words, £600 and £400. Students based abroad are welcome.
- Linguistics Essay Prize: 2,000 to 2,500 words, references excluded, £600 and £300. No particular A-Level subjects required.

Trinity splits each cash prize equally between the student and their school, with the school's half as book tokens. It also lists a Philosophy Essay Prize, which didn't publish a 2026 round at the time of writing, so check the college's essay prizes page before a student plans on it.

**Lucy Cavendish College, Cambridge: Cottrell Essay Prize.** For Year 12 students at UK state schools thinking about Medicine or Veterinary Medicine, 1,000 to 1,500 words on a set question, with small cash prizes and unlimited highly commended places. The college hasn't confirmed a 2026 round on the page we could find, so treat this one as worth checking rather than counting on.

## What the college judges reward

Read the questions from any of these and one thing stands out: they're set by people who teach the subject, and they want to see a student think the way an undergraduate does. A Robson question is a historian's question. A Gould question expects close reading, not plot summary. The Kelvin wants a scientific argument, not a project write-up.

So the same problems that sink a John Locke entry sink these, in a smaller field where each one is more visible. Coverage in place of a claim. Sources cited to show they've been read rather than used to prove something. The introduction that restates the question for 300 words. And, more than anywhere else, the entry that goes over the word count, because most colleges say in plain terms that they won't read it.
