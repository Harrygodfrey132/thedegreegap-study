Economics is where the essay competition question comes up most often, and where the personal statement needs it most. A Year 12 applying for Economics at LSE or Warwick is competing against applicants who have all read Freakonomics. What separates them is whether they've done any economics themselves, and a competition essay is the cleanest evidence there is.

The good news is that the economics competitions cluster in the summer, when a Year 12 has time. The less good news is that they reward something A-Level Economics doesn't teach, which is a mechanism argued from evidence rather than a topic described from the news.

## The Marshall Society Essay Competition

The Marshall Society is the University of Cambridge's economics society, founded in 1927 and named after Alfred Marshall. Its annual essay competition is free and open to any student anywhere in the world who hasn't yet started university. The society sets six essay titles each year, the top tenth of entries are shortlisted and contacted by email, the winners receive prizes, and selected essays are published in the society's magazine, The Dismal Scientist.

In 2026 the deadline was 30 August at 11:59pm BST, and the society reported almost 1,500 entries. The word limit and prize details sit in a document linked from the society's page, so read that before planning the length. The August deadline is the useful thing: the questions are out well before the summer, and the whole of July and August are available for a student who has finished Year 12.

## The Institute of Economic Affairs prize

The IEA has run a sixth-form essay prize for years. The long-standing one is the Dorian Fisher Memorial Prize, named after the wife of the Institute's founder, with essays of around 1,200 words, a £500 first prize and an invitation to an IEA event for the top entries. The IEA's page for it shows older dates, so check what's running before a student writes to that brief.

In 2026 the Institute's live sixth-form competition was the Monetary Policy Essay Prize. Sixth-form and undergraduate categories, a maximum of 2,500 words with references counted and the bibliography not, a PDF submitted through the IEA's form, and a deadline of 31 August 2026. The sixth-form prizes were £1,500 for the winner and £750 for each of two runners-up, with semi-finals at the Vinson Centre at the University of Buckingham in October and a final at the IEA in London in December. Two things to know: the IEA is a free-market think tank and sets its questions accordingly, and its judges are happy to be argued with, as long as the argument is economics.

## RES Young Economist of the Year

The Royal Economic Society's competition is the best known name on this page and the one that has changed most. It launched in 2009 as an essay competition and for years ran with tutor2u. In 2026 the RES delivered it through Discover Economics, with KPMG as sponsor and support from FT Schools, and the entry was no longer an essay. Students in Years 10 to 13, individually or in groups of up to five, submitted a blog, a presentation, a podcast or a video on one of five topics, by 29 June. Five entries were shortlisted in September for an in-person final at KPMG in October, with a ten-minute presentation and questions.

That makes it a different kind of competition. It suits a Year 10 or 11 who wants to explain an economic idea clearly and a group who want to make something together. A sixth former who wants to write 2,500 words of argument should look at the Marshall Society or the IEA instead.

## John Locke Economics, and the college prizes

The Economics category of the [John Locke essay competition](/study/essay-competitions/john-locke-essay-competition/) is the hardest field here and closes earliest: 31 May in 2026, with three questions published in February. The 2026 questions asked whether we should fear a cashless society, what widespread personalised pricing would do, and whether Jeff Bezos got rich at the expense of his customers, his employees, neither or both. Those are economics questions dressed as provocations, and the students who do well answer the economics.

Fitzwilliam College, Cambridge also set an Economics question in its 2026 essay competitions, with a much shorter limit than its other subjects and a late February deadline. Details are on the [Oxford and Cambridge college prizes page](/study/essay-competitions/oxford-and-cambridge-college-essay-competitions/).

## What economists reward in 2,500 words

The judges for all of these are economists, and they read the way economists read.

**A claim with a mechanism.** "Cashless payments hurt the poor" is a position. "Cashless payments raise the fixed cost of participating in the economy for people without bank accounts, and here's what happened to the unbanked in Sweden" is an argument. The second is what wins.

**A model, used lightly.** One diagram, or one idea from the course such as consumer surplus, elasticity or moral hazard, applied to the actual question. Not a tour of the specification.

**Evidence that does work.** The Bank of England's own reports, an ONS series, a Competition and Markets Authority market study, a single well-chosen paper. Three sources used properly beat fifteen cited.

**The counterargument taken seriously.** The IEA in particular will have a house view on its question. A student who states the strongest version of the other side and answers it has done something most entries don't.

**Cutting.** Every one of these has a hard limit. The last week is for taking out the paragraph that explains what inflation is.
