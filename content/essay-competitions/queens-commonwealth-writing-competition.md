This is the one competition where a Year 9 and a Year 13 can enter the same field. It's also the one a school is most likely to have mentioned already, in an assembly or a form-time email, because it's been running since Queen Victoria was on the throne and the Royal Commonwealth Society still hands out the prizes at a palace.

Most families who ask us about it have a younger child in mind. A thousand words is a comfortable length for a 13-year-old with something to say, and a very short one for a sixth former who wants to say everything. Both need the same thing, which is one idea, held on to.

## What the competition is

The Royal Commonwealth Society has run this competition since 1883, which makes it the world's oldest international writing competition for schools. For most of its life it was the Commonwealth Essay Competition. Since 2013 it has carried The Queen's name, and for 2026 it was renamed the Queen's Commonwealth Writing Competition, with the old Junior and Senior categories merged into one.

It's free, it's open across 56 countries and territories, and it's judged on the writing rather than on a mark scheme. That last point matters for how a student should approach it.

## Who can enter, and what to write

Anyone aged 18 or under on the closing date who is a national or resident of a Commonwealth country or territory can enter. In 2026 the deadline was 30 April, at 11:59 BST, and there was one age category for everyone.

The entry is a piece of writing of up to 1,000 words on the year's theme. For 2026 the theme was Common Ground: Better Together! Earlier years have set themes in the same spirit, broad enough for a poem or a letter as much as an essay, so read the current rules for the forms accepted before a student settles on one.

## What the winners get

Each Commonwealth region produces a winner, and one entry is chosen as the overall winner. The regional winners are brought to London for a week of literary and cultural events, and the week ends with an award ceremony at a Royal Palace hosted by The Queen, who is the competition's patron. For a 15-year-old that is a fairly remarkable thing to have on a UCAS form three years later, and a better story at interview than most.

## How to write to a theme

Almost every competition on this site sets a question. This one sets a theme, and that's where most entries go wrong. A question forces a position. A theme invites a poster.

**Turn the theme into a claim first.** Common Ground: Better Together! is not asking the student to agree that togetherness is good. It's asking what they think about it. The first job, before any writing, is a single sentence that someone could disagree with. "The common ground in my family is an argument about who cooks, and the argument is what keeps us close" is a claim. "The Commonwealth shows that we are stronger together" is a slogan.

**One story, not a survey.** In 1,000 words there's room for one scene, told properly, and what it means. A student who tries to cover climate change, migration and the Commonwealth Games in the same piece covers none of them. The strongest entries we've seen are small: a grandmother, a bus route, a word that doesn't translate.

**Write like the writer, not like a textbook.** The judges are reading thousands of pieces. A voice that sounds like a real 14-year-old, with a real opinion, stands out from the first line. A voice borrowed from a GCSE model answer does not.

**Cut the last paragraph.** Most drafts end by restating the theme. If the piece has done its work, the reader already knows what it meant. End on the scene, or on the thing the student still can't decide.

## How we help

For this competition the tutor is usually an English tutor, and the job is closer to editing than to teaching. A first session to find the claim in what the student already wants to write about. A draft read honestly, with the slogans marked. A second draft cut to length. And the rules read together, so the student submits the right form, on time, with their own name on their own work.
