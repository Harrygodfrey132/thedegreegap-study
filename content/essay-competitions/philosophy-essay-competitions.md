Philosophy hardly exists as an A-Level, so the sixth former who asks "but why is that wrong?" usually has nowhere in the timetable to put the question. That's what these competitions are for. Every one of them is free, most take students who have never sat a philosophy lesson, and the judges are philosophers who want to see an argument built rather than a topic described.

## John Locke: Philosophy and Theology

The Philosophy category of the [John Locke essay competition](/study/essay-competitions/john-locke-essay-competition/) is the best-known philosophy prize open to school students, and Theology sits alongside it with the same rules: three questions, 2,000 words, under 19 on the deadline, and a 31 May closing date in 2026 with the questions out in February.

The 2026 Philosophy questions asked whether it is ever wrong to do the right thing for the wrong reasons, what consolations philosophy offers, and why incest is wrong. Theology asked whether religious experience is better explained by neuroscience or by theology, and, for anyone who achieves enlightenment, how they'd know. The Institute says the best essays are the ones capable of changing somebody's mind, and in philosophy that means a thesis, an objection and a reply, not a tour of the positions.

## The Think Essay Prize

The Royal Institute of Philosophy runs an annual essay prize for 15 to 18-year-olds, supported by its journal Think, which exists to make philosophy readable outside universities. The limit is 1,200 words, which is short for philosophy and deliberately so. The 2026 round drew over 1,500 entries, the Institute published a longlist and then a shortlist, and shortlisted entrants received a year's subscription to the journal, with further prizes for the longlist and the winner.

Two things to know. The longlist is public, so a good essay gets named even if it doesn't win, which is worth something in a reference. And the Institute also runs a separate Philosophy Essay Prize for its academic journal, with an 8,000-word limit and a £2,500 prize. That one is for academics. Make sure the student is reading the right page.

## The college prizes

Oriel College, Oxford has run the Lloyd Davies Philosophy Prize since 2006. It sets topics for Year 12 or Lower Sixth students, takes essays of up to 2,500 words by email, and awards prizes and commendations. Newnham College, Cambridge runs a Philosophy Essay Prize for women in Year 12 from any school or country, with prizes of £400, £200 and £100 and the questions and word limit published in an information document each spring. Both are judged by the college's own philosophers, which is the point: for a student thinking about Oxford or Cambridge, this is the closest they'll get to being read by the people who interview.

Trinity College, Cambridge lists a Philosophy Essay Prize among its competitions but hadn't published a 2026 round at the time of writing. Check the college's essay prizes page rather than planning on it. All the college prizes are on the [Oxford and Cambridge page](/study/essay-competitions/oxford-and-cambridge-college-essay-competitions/), with dates.

One to avoid chasing: the Uehiro Oxford Prize in Practical Ethics is open only to students already at a UK university. School students can't enter, however good the question looks.

## The shape of a philosophy essay the judges shortlist

Philosophers read for a structure, and it's the same one whether the judge sits at the Royal Institute of Philosophy, Oriel or the John Locke Institute. In order:

1. **The thesis, in the first paragraph.** Not a promise to explore the question. A claim: it is never wrong to do the right thing for the wrong reasons, because what makes an act right is what it does, not why it was done. The reader now knows what they're arguing with.
2. **The terms, defined.** Every question here turns on a word. Right, wrong, reason, consolation, experience. The essay that stops to say what it means by the word, and why that meaning rather than another, has done philosophy before it has argued anything.
3. **The strongest objection, given fairly.** Not a weak version set up to be knocked down. The best objection the student can find, stated so well that the reader briefly agrees with it. An essay with no objection in it is a speech.
4. **The reply.** This is the paragraph the whole essay exists for, and the one most drafts rush. It should be longer than the objection, and it should concede whatever the objection gets right.
5. **One text, read closely.** A single argument from Mill, Kant, Parfit or Nagel, quoted and taken apart, beats a paragraph naming all four. The judges know the names. They want to see whether the student can read one.
6. **An ending that argues.** The most common last paragraph in a school philosophy essay says the question is ultimately subjective. It is the one ending guaranteed to lose the judges. If the student's honest view is that the question has no answer, the essay has to argue for that, which is harder than it sounds.

Throughout, plain language. Philosophy has a vocabulary and sixth formers reach for it. The essay that says what it means in ordinary words, and uses the technical term only when nothing else will do, reads as confident. The other kind reads as borrowed.
