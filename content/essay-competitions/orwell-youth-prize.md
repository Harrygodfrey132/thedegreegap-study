Somewhere in most schools there's a Year 10 with opinions and nowhere to put them. The bus route that got cut. The uniform rule that makes no sense. The thing that happened to a cousin. They can write, when they're allowed to write about something that matters, and the Orwell Youth Prize is where that gets to happen.

It's a smaller competition than the international ones on this site, and a kinder one. It's free, it takes any form, and if a student enters early they get personal feedback on the piece before the final deadline. For a family looking for a first competition, it's the one we point to.

## What the prize is

The Orwell Youth Prize is run by the Orwell Foundation, the charity that also awards the Orwell Prizes for political writing, journalism and books. The youth prize exists to do for school students what the adult prizes do for writers: reward writing that makes political ideas clear, honest and readable.

That's Orwell's standard, and the Foundation means it. The winning pieces are rarely the ones with the biggest subject. They're the ones where a young writer looked at something in front of them and told the truth about it in plain words.

## Who can enter, and in what form

Anyone at secondary school or college in the UK, or studying a UK curriculum, can enter in one of three categories: Years 7 to 9, Years 10 to 11, and Years 12 to 13. The word limit is 1,000 words for the younger two categories and 1,500 for the sixth-form category.

The form is open. Essays and journalism are the obvious fit, but the prize accepts stories, poems, plays, blogs and even game concepts, as long as the piece responds to the year's theme. In 2026 the theme was Truth.

## The 2026 dates, and why the early one matters

The 2026 prize had two deadlines. Anyone who entered by 25 February received personal feedback on their piece and could revise it. The final deadline was 29 April. Around 40 pieces were shortlisted, with roughly two winners and three runners-up in each category.

The February date is the one to plan for. A free, honest read from someone who knows what the judges want, with two months to act on it, is worth more than the £100 prize. Most students never get a piece of writing read like that until university.

## The prizes

Winners receive £100, a certificate and the complete works of Orwell, for themselves and for their school. Runners-up receive a copy of Orwell's essays and a certificate. Shortlisted writers are named by the Foundation, which is the line a school reference can use.

## What Orwell would let through

The Foundation doesn't publish a mark scheme, but it doesn't need to. Orwell wrote one in 1946, in an essay called Politics and the English Language, and it still describes what the judges reward. His six rules, in short:

- Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech you're used to seeing in print.
- Never use a long word where a short one will do.
- If it's possible to cut a word out, cut it out.
- Never use the passive where you can use the active.
- Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
- Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

Put those next to a typical first draft and the problems show. The opening paragraph that says the issue is complex and multifaceted. The word "impactful". The passive sentence that hides who did the thing. The paragraph about "society" where a paragraph about the student's own street would have said more.

The other thing the winning entries share is a scene. Orwell's best essays start with something he saw: an elephant, a hanging, a bookshop. A student who starts with the empty bus stop, and only then gets to the council's budget, has learnt the trick. A student who starts with "In today's society" hasn't yet.

## Where entries fall down

**The issues essay.** A piece that could have been written by anyone, about a topic chosen because it seemed important, with nothing in it the student witnessed. The theme is a way in, not a topic list.

**Taking the theme literally.** In 2026 the theme was Truth. A piece about "what is truth?" in the abstract has a hard job. A piece about the one lie a family tells, and why, has a much easier one.

**Overwriting.** Sixth formers, in particular, reach for the long word. Orwell's rules exist because the plain sentence is harder to write and better to read.

**Missing the early deadline.** The feedback is the best thing about the prize, and it's only there for the students who enter in February.

## How we help

For the Orwell prize a tutor's job is mostly editing. A first session to find the scene inside the subject, and the claim inside the scene. A draft read against Orwell's rules, honestly. And, if the student entered early, a session working through the Foundation's feedback before the final version goes in. The student writes it, in their own words. That's the whole point of the prize.
