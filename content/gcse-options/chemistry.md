# Answers from Francis's email of 12 October 2026, lightly tidied.
faqs:
  - q: "Why is Chemistry so important?"
    a: |
      Chemistry helps us understand the world around us at the level of atoms and molecules. Everything we see, touch, eat and breathe involves chemistry.

      It plays a vital role in medicine, farming, food, energy, protecting the environment and developing new materials. From the medicines we take to the batteries in our phones, it shapes almost every part of daily life.

      I often tell my students that chemistry isn't simply a subject we study for exams. It's a science that helps us understand how the world works and how we can improve people's lives.
  - q: "How is Chemistry different from Biology and Physics?"
    a: |
      Chemistry is often called the *central science*, because it connects the other two.

      Biology focuses on living things and life processes. Physics looks at matter, energy, forces and the fundamental laws of nature. Chemistry concentrates on what substances are made of, how they behave and how they react: how atoms combine to form molecules, why reactions happen and how substances change.

      So biology explains how the body works, physics explains the principles behind energy and movement, and chemistry explains the molecular reactions that make life possible. The three sciences complement one another, with chemistry as the link between them.
  - q: "What's the biggest misconception about GCSE Chemistry?"
    a: |
      That it's simply about memorising equations, reactions and facts. In reality, chemistry is about *understanding principles and applying them logically*. Once students understand atomic structure, bonding, electron arrangement and energy changes, a lot of topics that looked complicated become much easier.

      Another misconception is that you have to be exceptionally gifted to do well. I disagree. With good teaching, patience, regular practice and a willingness to ask questions, students can make remarkable progress.
  - q: "Where do students lose the most marks?"
    a: |
      In my experience, in five areas:

      - **Calculations**, particularly moles, concentrations, reacting masses, titrations and percentage yield.
      - **Scientific terminology.** Students may understand a concept but not use the precise language the mark scheme needs.
      - **Application questions**, where students have to apply familiar principles to an unfamiliar situation.
      - **Practical chemistry**: questions on methods, observations, variables, uncertainties and evaluating an experiment.
      - **Exam technique.** Students sometimes misread command words such as *describe*, *explain*, *calculate*, *compare* and *evaluate*.

      I encourage students to show their working clearly, use the correct units, explain their reasoning and read each question carefully before answering.
  - q: "How would you suggest students revise for Chemistry?"
    a: |
      Actively, rather than just reading the textbook or copying out notes.

      Start with the fundamentals, particularly atomic structure, bonding, chemical equations and the mole. Then use a mix of short revision sessions, worked examples, flashcards, regular calculations and exam-style questions.

      Past papers are especially valuable, because they teach students how to apply what they know and show the level of detail examiners expect. Students should also keep a record of the questions they get wrong and go back to those topics regularly.

      Most importantly, I encourage students to ask *why* something happens, rather than simply memorising *what* happens.
  - q: "What can parents do at home to help?"
    a: |
      Parents can make an enormous difference, even with little or no science background. A quiet place to study, encouragement to revise regularly and an interest in what your child is learning all help.

      One particularly effective approach is to ask your child to explain a chemistry topic in simple language. If they can explain it clearly to someone else, that's often a good sign they understand it.

      Praise effort, improvement and persistence rather than focusing only on grades. Above all, students need reassurance that making mistakes is a normal and valuable part of learning.
