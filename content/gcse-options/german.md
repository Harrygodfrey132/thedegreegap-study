faqs:
  - q: "Why would you pick German for GCSE?"
    a: |
      Learning a language opens your mind to a different culture, history and geography, whilst improving your memory and problem-solving skills. German in particular opens up opportunities in an economic powerhouse, and it's the most widely spoken first language in the European Union.

      I chose it because some of my family live in northern Germany, and I was fascinated by the culture after a Year 10 German exchange in Stuttgart. I wanted to be able to hold conversations with locals and maybe live in Germany in the future (which I did, for a year!).
  - q: "Why might you not want to pick German?"
    a: |
      German is hard to learn in its own way. For example, the word for "the" has many different forms in German, and trying to remember which one to use was a challenge.

      I also think you need an interest in going to a German-speaking country, or some connection to one, otherwise the language will be hard to keep up. Spanish is a lot more accessible because millions more people speak it. With German, you have to look for the opportunities.
  - q: "What's the biggest misconception about GCSE German?"
    a: |
      That it sounds like an angry language! That idea only comes from pop culture. Listening to everyday conversations was actually really satisfying and comforting.
  - q: "What do students find hardest?"
    a: |
      Grammar! The word order is different, there are plenty of tenses to understand, and you have to know the conjugation of everything.
  - q: "How would you suggest students revise for German?"
    a: |
      It really is practice, but you can make it fun. I don't think traditional grammar tests and repeated exercises engage students. Fun quizzes, colourful tables and seeing how grammar is used in TV and books are a lot more engaging, and more realistic too.
  - q: "What can parents do at home to help?"
    a: |
      Pretend to have a conversation with your child as if you knew German. You could have a set script on your side, in English or in German, and let your child respond in German. Speaking is the real test of knowledge. It doesn't need your child to know every detail of the language, just to speak freely and build up confidence.

      Learning a language is also about frequency. You could buy your child a short book in German, recommend a cool song, or watch a funny video together. It all makes the language feel normal, and broadens the experience beyond lessons.
