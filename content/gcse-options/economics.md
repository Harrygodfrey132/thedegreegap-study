faqs:
  - q: "Why would you pick Economics for GCSE?"
    a: |
      Economics is a fantastic subject for students who are curious about the world around them and enjoy asking questions. Why do prices rise? Why are some countries wealthier than others? Why does the government change taxes or interest rates, and how do those decisions affect ordinary people?

      What makes it particularly rewarding is that students can immediately connect what they learn in the classroom to real events. It encourages them to think critically, consider different perspectives and form their own informed opinions.

      Having taught Economics and Business for many years, I've seen how it changes the way students engage with the news. It's also an excellent foundation for further study in Economics, Business, Politics and other analytical subjects.
  - q: "Why might you not want to pick Economics?"
    a: |
      It may not suit students who prefer subjects where success comes mainly from memorising information. There are important concepts and definitions to learn, but Economics asks students to apply that knowledge, interpret information and explain their reasoning.

      There's also a balance of numerical and written work. Students will meet calculations, graphs and data, but they also need to be comfortable building written explanations and arguments.

      I'd encourage students to choose Economics because they're genuinely interested in how the world works, rather than just because they think it might be useful for a career in finance or business.
  - q: "What's the biggest misconception about GCSE Economics?"
    a: |
      That it's simply about money, banking or the stock market. In reality, Economics is about choices: how we use limited resources to meet our wants and needs. It looks at the decisions made by individuals, businesses and governments, and what happens as a result.

      Another misconception is that Economics is all about finding the correct answer. Some of the most interesting economic questions don't have a single, straightforward solution. A government policy might help one group and disadvantage another, and students need to understand those trade-offs.
  - q: "What do students find hardest?"
    a: |
      Students can often recall definitions and explain the basic ideas, but struggle when asked to analyse how a change might affect a particular business, a household or the wider economy.

      Knowing that higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive is fairly straightforward. Explaining how that might affect spending, business investment, jobs and inflation takes a much deeper understanding. Students often need help building logical chains of reasoning, where each point clearly follows from the one before.

      Evaluation is the other big challenge. Students need to see that economic outcomes depend on circumstances, and that there are often competing arguments. That takes practice, but it's also one of the most valuable skills the subject teaches.
  - q: "How would you suggest students revise for Economics?"
    a: |
      With active recall, rather than rereading or highlighting notes. Students should test themselves regularly on key definitions, concepts and diagrams, explaining ideas in their own words without looking at the textbook.

      Once the foundations are secure, exam-question practice becomes essential. Students should learn to spot exactly what a question is asking, build clear chains of analysis and use relevant real-world examples.

      I also strongly recommend following economic news. Inflation, the cost of living, unemployment, taxation: linking classroom theory to what's happening now makes the subject more memorable and sharpens students' analysis.
  - q: "What can parents do at home to help?"
    a: |
      One of the simplest and most effective things is to talk about everyday economic issues. When you're shopping, ask why certain things have become more expensive. When the news is on, talk about why the government might raise taxes. Those conversations build the habit of thinking about cause and effect, and about the trade-offs behind decisions.

      You can also help by encouraging regular revision, asking your child to explain ideas out loud and helping them build confidence with exam questions.

      One thing I've noticed through tutoring is that students often understand more than they think. Sometimes the chance to explain an idea somewhere supportive makes a real difference to their confidence.
