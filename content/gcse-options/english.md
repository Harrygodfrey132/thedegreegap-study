faqs:
  - q: "Why is English so important?"
    a: |
      English is fundamental to a lot of other subjects, and it helps us communicate our thoughts and ideas in so many parts of life. It's an extremely versatile subject. I think it gives students a well-rounded range of skills that help in the classroom and in the world around them.
  - q: "What's the difference between English Language and English Literature?"
    a: |
      **Language** is the technical side of English. Students learn specific terminology, like personification, sibilance or imagery, and how it creates meaning. Words are so much more than what we see on the page, and studying language helps students understand the connotations of words and phrases, and how to use them to express themselves.

      The Language papers ask students to analyse extracts from texts they've never seen before, so a thorough knowledge of technical terms and structural features is key. They also give students the chance to show those skills in their own writing: stories or descriptions, and non-fiction such as an article, letter or speech.

      **Literature** is the study of written texts: poetry, plays and novels. These texts don't exist in isolation, so students also learn about the historical context and about each author's life, beliefs and intentions. It's like being able to time travel. Literature lets students think big, connecting themes and characters and tracing how they develop across a text. The Literature papers usually cover a Shakespeare play, an older text, a modern one and a collection of poems from different poets and periods.
  - q: "What's the biggest misconception about GCSE English?"
    a: |
      That English isn't relevant. With so much information at our fingertips, it's easy to go straight to the internet. But knowing about something and understanding it aren't the same thing. Our goal is to give students skills that last a lifetime: thinking critically, forming well-informed views and explaining them in the best way possible.
  - q: "Where do students lose the most marks?"
    a: |
      A common mistake is that students don't always know what ingredients their answers need. So I like to explain paragraph structures and patterns, or go through mark schemes together, so they can see how to present their ideas effectively.

      That usually helps with the next most common issue, which is confidence. Sometimes students just need a little support and reframing to see their own strengths. It makes a world of difference.
  - q: "How would you suggest students revise for English?"
    a: |
      Multi-modal revision, which just means doing lots of different things with the information they need to know. Making flashcards, doing practice questions, writing character pages, listening back to voice notes of their notes, rehearsing Literature quotes in silly voices. Anything that helps them process the information in different ways.
  - q: "What can parents do at home to help?"
    a: |
      As parents, we take on a whole range of roles, and getting your child through exams is no different. Your support might be helping them memorise quotes, reading over an article they've written, or letting their tutor know what they're working on at school. Those insights from parents really help shape what we do in lessons.

      Often, they just need to know you're there for them in this new chapter too.
