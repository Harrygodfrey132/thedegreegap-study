Short answer first. The revision that works is the revision that feels hard: testing yourself, coming back to topics over several weeks, and doing exam questions against the clock. Re-reading notes and highlighting feel productive and do very little. That holds in every subject.

What changes from subject to subject is what "testing yourself" looks like. A Maths paper, an English Literature essay and a French dictation reward different things. So rather than twenty tips, this post picks the two or three methods that matter most in each subject, matched to how the papers are marked.

Timing matters too. Most schools hold Year 11 mocks between November and January, and the summer 2027 exams are due to start on Monday 10 May 2027. Our [free revision timetable maker](/revision-timetable/) turns everything below into a week-by-week plan.

## Three rules for every subject

**Test, don't re-read.** Every method in this post is a way of pulling knowledge out of memory rather than looking at it again. A large review of study techniques by Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing and spaced practice as the two most useful methods, and rated re-reading and highlighting as low. The Education Endowment Foundation's review of the classroom evidence points the same way, with the caveat that how well it works depends on how it's done.

**Spread it out.** An hour on a topic spread over three weeks beats the same hour in one sitting. Coming back to a topic just as it's starting to fade is what makes it stick, and it's the whole point of a timetable: it decides in advance when each topic comes back.

**Practise the real thing, and mark it honestly.** Past papers, mark schemes and examiners' reports are free on the exam boards' websites, and they're the best revision resource there is. Mark harshly: if the wording isn't close to the mark scheme, the mark doesn't count. And check which exam board your child sits for each subject, because the papers differ more than most families expect.

## GCSE Maths

On AQA, Edexcel and OCR there are three papers of 1 hour 30 minutes, and one of them is non-calculator. In the 2027 exams, students get a formulae sheet, so the work goes into knowing which formula to use and when, rather than memorising it.

**Do a little every day, with the topics mixed up.** Twenty minutes of mixed questions most days does more than a two-hour session on one topic. Mixing matters because the exam never tells your child which method a question needs. In a well-known experiment, students who practised maths problems shuffled together scored far better on a test a week later than students who practised one type at a time, even though they did worse while practising. Corbett Maths' free "5-a-day" sheets are built for exactly this.

**Move from topic questions to full papers.** Start with past-paper questions sorted by topic (Maths Genie and Physics & Maths Tutor both group them, for free), then switch to whole papers against the clock as the exams get close. Do the non-calculator paper often. Fractions, percentages and written methods are where calculator habits cost the most.

**Learn from every wrong answer.** Our Maths tutor Emily's advice from [our summer webinar](/blog/how-to-maximise-every-gcse-mark/) still holds. Check the paper before opening the mark scheme, then write down why each wrong answer was wrong and what to do next time. Practise with your board's formulae sheet as well, so it's familiar on the day.

## GCSE English Language

On AQA there are two papers of 1 hour 45 minutes, each worth 80 marks. Question 5, the piece of writing at the end of each paper, is worth 40 of those 80.

**Practise the writing question more than anything else.** It's half the marks, and it's the easiest place to gain them. Practise it under time: a few minutes to plan, most of the time writing, and a few minutes to check. Keep a small bank of openings, sentence structures and ambitious words to reuse. Examiners are looking for control, variety and accuracy, not the next bestseller.

**Answer reading questions on extracts your child hasn't seen.** The reading questions always use unseen texts, so the only real practice is with new ones. Use past-paper extracts and answer one question at a time against the clock, staying inside the lines the question names. And say what the language or structure does in this extract, not in general: "short sentences create tension" earns little on its own.

## GCSE English Literature

Every board's exams are closed book, so your child can't take the texts in. AQA's two papers cover a Shakespeare play, a 19th-century novel, a modern text, the poetry anthology and unseen poetry.

**Learn a small set of short, flexible quotations.** Ten to fifteen per text is plenty if each one does several jobs: a theme, a character, a turning point. Short quotes are easier to remember and easier to weave into a sentence. Put the quote on one side of a flashcard and what it shows on the other, then test both ways round.

**Write plans, not just essays.** A five-minute essay plan on a likely theme or character practises the hardest part, choosing an argument and the evidence for it, in a fraction of the time. Do one full timed essay a week and plans in between.

**Use context only where it answers the question.** One or two sentences on why a writer made a choice earn more than a paragraph of history. For poetry, practise comparing two poems every time, because on AQA the anthology question asks for a comparison.

## GCSE Biology, Chemistry and Physics

Whether your child takes Combined Science or the three separate sciences, many questions describe a situation they haven't met and ask them to apply what they know. AQA's Combined Science has 21 required practicals, and at least 15% of the exam marks test what students learned from them.

**Turn the specification into questions.** The specification, free on the board's website, lists everything that can be asked. Go through it line by line and make a flashcard for each point, worded the way the mark scheme words it. Our Science tutor Callum's biggest warning was vague answers: knowing the idea but not using the exact scientific words costs marks.

**Revise the required practicals as a topic of their own.** For each one: the method, the variables, what the results show and what could go wrong. These questions come up on every paper, and they're very learnable.

**Practise the maths.** Graphs, percentages, rearranging equations and units carry a lot of marks. In 2027, students get an equations sheet in Physics and Combined Science, so practise picking the right equation and showing every step, because method marks survive a wrong final answer. Callum's rule of thumb from [the webinar](/blog/how-to-maximise-every-gcse-mark/): about three-quarters of the time on content and a quarter on past papers.

## French, Spanish and German

The new language GCSEs were first sat in 2026. They test a set vocabulary list (on AQA, 1,200 words at Foundation and 1,700 at Higher), a dictation in the listening paper and reading aloud in the speaking exam.

**Learn the board's vocabulary list, a little every day.** The list is published in the specification, so there are no surprises. Ten minutes of flashcards a day, testing both directions, beats an evening of cramming, and a flashcard app spaces the reviews for you.

**Listen and write down what you hear.** Dictation rewards knowing how sounds are spelt. Play short recordings, write them down, then check against the text. Reading aloud uses the same skill in reverse, so practise both.

**Prepare speaking answers you can adapt.** Have a few rehearsed answers for each topic that can be bent to fit different questions, each with an opinion and a reason. And say them out loud, not just in your head.

## GCSE History

History papers mix knowledge questions with longer answers that ask for a judgement.

**Build timelines and test them.** Knowing the order of events is what lets your child explain causes and consequences. A one-page timeline per topic, covered up and recalled from memory, makes a quick daily test.

**Practise the long answers in a fixed shape.** For "how far do you agree" questions, the top marks go to a clear judgement, argued both ways and backed by specific detail. Practise plans against past questions, then write one in full under time each week.

**Know your board's question types.** Each board asks different questions about sources and interpretations, and its mark schemes show exactly what the top level wants.

## GCSE Geography

Geography rewards named examples and confident skills.

**Put each case study on one page.** The place, the facts and figures, the causes, the effects and the responses, so it can be recalled as a whole. Then test it from a blank page.

**Practise the skills questions.** Graphs, maps, percentages and reading data come up on every paper, and they're easy marks to lose through rushing.

**On AQA, use the pre-release booklet well.** AQA releases a resources booklet 12 weeks before Paper 3. Your child can study and annotate it, but they get a clean copy in the exam, so the preparation has to be in their head rather than on the page.

## Every other subject

Religious Studies, Computer Science, Business and the rest follow the same three rules: test, space it out, and practise past questions with the mark scheme. Ask each teacher which question types cost the most marks in the mocks, and start there.

## How parents can help

Help your child make the plan, then step back. A plan they built themselves is one they're far more likely to follow. Check in once a week rather than every evening, and ask "what did you test yourself on?" rather than "have you revised?" The first question has an answer you can talk about.

When marked mock papers come home, ask to see them and [read the paper, not the number](/blog/do-mock-exams-matter/). If one subject keeps slipping however good the plan is, that's the moment to talk to the subject teacher.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many hours a day should my child revise for GCSEs?**
As a starting point in Year 11, one to two hours on a school night and three to four on a weekend day, in sessions of 30 to 45 minutes with breaks. Build up in the Easter holidays. The [revision timetable maker](/revision-timetable/) works this out from the time your child actually has.

**Is it better to revise one subject a day or several?**
Two or three a day. Switching between subjects feels harder, but it's better for remembering, and it stops the subjects your child likes least from always being left until last.

**Are revision guides and flashcard apps worth paying for?**
Some are useful, but the best resources are free: past papers, mark schemes and examiners' reports from the exam board, and the specification itself. A revision guide only earns its keep if your child tests themselves from it rather than reading it.

**Do the formulae sheets mean my child doesn't need to learn formulae?**
For the 2027 exams, students get a formulae sheet in Maths and an equations sheet in Physics and Combined Science. They still need to know which one to use and how to rearrange it, so practise with the sheet your board publishes.

**When should my child start revising for GCSEs?**
A light routine from the autumn of Year 11, stepped up after February half term. Starting early is less about extra hours and more about spacing: topics revisited over months stay put.
