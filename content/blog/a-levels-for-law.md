If your child is in Year 11 and thinking about law, here's the most important thing to know: **no UK law school requires a particular A-Level.** Not even A-Level Law. What matters is strong grades, subjects that build reading and argument, and, for the most selective universities, the LNAT.

This guide explains what top law schools actually ask for, which combinations work well, and how routes into the profession have changed. If you'd like to talk through your child's plans, [book a free 30-minute call](/book-a-call/).

## No subject is required, including A-Level Law

The leading law schools say this plainly:

- **Cambridge** says on its course page: "We don't ask for any specific subjects to apply to Law."
- **UCL** has no subject requirements for its LLB, but two of the three A-Levels must be on its university-wide preferred subjects list.
- **King's College London** has no required subjects, but doesn't accept General Studies, Critical Thinking, Thinking Skills or Global Perspectives as one of the three.
- **Durham** asks for top grades with no specific subjects.

So the choice of subjects is genuinely open. The question is which combination gives your child the best grades and the best preparation.

## What grades do law schools ask for?

At the most selective universities, the standard offer is A*AA. Across all UK law degrees, offers range from about BCC to A*AA, with ABB a common offer.

Many universities make contextual offers to eligible students, sometimes up to two grades lower than the standard offer.

## Is A-Level Law worth taking?

It's a perfectly good A-Level, and some students take it to find out whether they enjoy the subject. But it gives no advantage in applications, and the most selective universities don't prefer it. If your child takes it, it should be because they're interested, not because they think it's required.

## The best A-Levels for law

Law rewards close reading, clear argument and writing under pressure. Subjects that build those skills make good preparation:

- **History**, which develops source analysis and argument
- **English literature**, which builds close reading and essay writing
- **Politics**, which covers how laws are made
- **A modern or classical language**, which builds precision with words
- **Religious studies or philosophy**, which develops ethical reasoning

Maths and the sciences are also welcome. Law values analytical thinking, and a student with maths, history and chemistry has a strong, balanced profile. A good rule is to include at least one essay subject. If you're weighing up workload, our guide to [which A-Levels are hardest](/blog/hardest-a-levels/) shows what the 2026 results say.

## The LNAT

The Law National Aptitude Test (LNAT) is used by a small group of leading law schools, including Oxford, Cambridge, UCL, King's College London, LSE, Durham and Bristol. Check the LNAT website for the current list. It doesn't test legal knowledge. Instead, it has two parts:

- **Multiple-choice questions** on reading passages, testing comprehension and reasoning
- **An essay**, chosen from a set of topics, testing how well your child builds an argument

Registration usually opens in August and testing starts in September of Year 13. Applicants to Oxford and Cambridge sit the test earlier, to fit the October UCAS deadline. The summer after Year 12 is the natural time to prepare. For Oxford and Cambridge applicants, our [interview preparation guide](/blog/oxford-cambridge-interview-preparation-2026/) uses Oxford Law as its worked example, and our [UCAS deadline guide](/blog/ucas-deadline-2027/) covers the application itself.

## Do you need a law degree to become a lawyer?

Not any more. Since 2021, solicitors in England and Wales have qualified through the Solicitors Qualifying Examination (SQE). Candidates need a degree in any subject, or an equivalent qualification, plus both stages of the SQE and two years of qualifying work experience. Solicitor apprenticeships are another route, without a degree.

Barristers can take a law degree, or another degree followed by a conversion course. So a law degree is one good route, but it's not the only one. A student who loves history can study history and still become a lawyer.

## What to do in Year 11

1. Choose three subjects your child enjoys and can get top grades in, including at least one essay subject.
2. Check three or four target universities' rules, especially any subjects they exclude.
3. Read widely and follow the news. Interest in how society and the law work is what admissions tutors look for in the personal statement and interview.
4. Try debating, mock trials or Model United Nations if the school offers them. These build the skills law degrees use.
5. Plan ahead for the LNAT in the summer after Year 12, if they're aiming for a university that uses it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What A-Levels do you need to study law?

None in particular. UK law schools don't require specific A-Levels, but the most selective ask for A*AA and some exclude subjects such as General Studies or Critical Thinking.

### Do you need A-Level Law to study law at university?

No. Cambridge, for example, says it doesn't ask for any specific subjects. A-Level Law is fine to take if your child is interested, but it gives no advantage.

### What are the best A-Levels for law?

Subjects that build reading, argument and writing, such as history, English literature, politics, languages or religious studies. Maths and sciences are welcome too. Including at least one essay subject is a sensible rule.

### Which universities require the LNAT?

A small group of leading law schools, including Oxford, Cambridge, UCL, King's College London, LSE, Durham and Bristol. Check the LNAT website each year, because the list can change.

### Do you need a law degree to become a solicitor?

No. Since 2021, solicitors in England and Wales qualify through the SQE, which is open to graduates in any subject. They also need two years of qualifying work experience. Solicitor apprenticeships offer a route without a degree.

### What grades do you need for law?

Offers range from about BCC to A*AA. The most selective law schools typically ask for A*AA, with contextual offers available to eligible students.
