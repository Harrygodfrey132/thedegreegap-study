If your child is dyslexic and heading towards GCSEs, you've probably noticed the gap between what they understand and what ends up on the page. They can explain an idea out loud, then run out of time writing it down. That gap is exactly what GCSE papers punish, and it's also what the right support can close.

This guide covers how dyslexia affects GCSEs, the rules for extra time and other exam arrangements in 2027, what the evidence says actually helps, and how to choose a tutor. If you'd like to talk about your child, [book a free 30-minute call](/book-a-call/).

## How common dyslexia is, and why GCSEs make it harder

The British Dyslexia Association estimates that around 10% of people in the UK are dyslexic, with about 4% severely affected. That's roughly three children in an average class, and many of them never receive a formal diagnosis.

GCSEs bring the difficulties into sharp focus, for four reasons:

- **Reading load.** Long questions and source extracts in English, history and science take longer to read accurately, and that time comes out of writing time.
- **Timed writing.** Extended answers need ideas, structure and spelling all at once, under time pressure.
- **Spelling marks.** Several subjects, including English, history, geography and religious studies, award marks for spelling, punctuation and grammar.
- **Working memory.** Multi-step maths and science problems mean holding several pieces of information at once, which many dyslexic students find tiring.

None of this is about intelligence. Dyslexia occurs across the whole range of ability, and many dyslexic students have real strengths in reasoning, problem-solving and spoken explanation.

## Exam access arrangements: the rules for 2027

Access arrangements let students show what they know without their dyslexia getting in the way. The most common are:

- 25% extra time
- A reader or computer reader, which reads the questions aloud
- A word processor, often with the spell checker turned off
- A scribe, who writes down what the student dictates
- Supervised rest breaks or a smaller room

The rule that surprises most parents is that **a dyslexia diagnosis on its own doesn't secure extra time.** Under the Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ) rules, the school's own qualified assessor has to test your child from Year 9 onwards, using nationally standardised tests. For 25% extra time, the results need to show either:

- at least two below-average scores (84 or less), or
- one below-average score and one low-average score (85 to 89)

in different areas of speed of working, such as reading speed, writing speed or processing speed. The assessor records this on JCQ Form 8. Teachers also have to confirm that extra time is your child's normal way of working, so it should already be showing up in class tests and mocks.

The deadline matters. Schools must apply for most arrangements, including extra time and readers, by **21 March 2027**, and for modified papers such as large print by **31 January 2027**. Our [GCSE exam dates 2027 guide](/blog/gcse-exam-dates-2027/) has these alongside every exam date.

**What to do now:** email the school's SENCo. Ask whether your child has been assessed for access arrangements, what the results showed, and whether extra time or a word processor was used in the mocks. If they haven't been assessed, ask when that will happen.

## Do you need a private dyslexia assessment?

Not necessarily for GCSEs, because access arrangements depend on the school's own assessment. A private diagnostic assessment is still worth considering if you want to understand your child's full profile, or if the school's support isn't matching their needs.

Dyslexia isn't diagnosed on the NHS. Through the British Dyslexia Association, an assessment with a specialist teacher costs £690 and one with a psychologist £882, including VAT. Other assessors charge similar amounts. Ask whether the assessor holds an Assessment Practising Certificate or is a registered educational psychologist.

## What actually helps dyslexic students at GCSE

The evidence points clearly in one direction: structured, frequent, explicit teaching, closely linked to what the student is doing in class. The Education Endowment Foundation finds that one-to-one tuition adds about five months of progress on average, with the best results from short, regular sessions tied to normal lessons. Students who are behind benefit most.

In practice, these approaches work well for dyslexic GCSE students:

- **Shrink the reading load.** Use audio versions of set texts, text-to-speech for notes, and highlighters to mark command words in questions.
- **Teach answer structures.** A clear plan for a six-mark science answer or an English paragraph reduces how much has to be held in memory while writing.
- **Learn subject keywords, not general spelling.** A list of 30 words for each subject that carry marks is far more useful than general spelling practice.
- **Use active revision.** Flashcards, self-quizzing, diagrams and explaining aloud beat rereading notes, especially for students who read slowly. There's more in our guide to [how to revise for GCSEs](/blog/how-to-revise-for-gcses/).
- **Practise with the real arrangement.** If your child gets extra time or a reader, every timed practice paper should use it too.
- **Keep sessions short.** Thirty to forty-five focused minutes usually achieve more than a long session that ends in fatigue.

## Specialist dyslexia teacher or subject tutor?

They do different jobs, and many students benefit from knowing which one they need.

- **A specialist dyslexia teacher** (often with a Level 5 or Level 7 qualification) works on the underlying skills: reading accuracy and fluency, spelling and written expression. This is the right choice if those skills are well behind.
- **A subject tutor with dyslexia experience** teaches GCSE content and exam technique in a way that works for your child. This is usually the right choice at GCSE if the core skills are workable but grades are being held back in maths, English or science.

Whoever you choose, ask these questions first:

- **What have you done with dyslexic students before?** Look for specific examples, not a general reassurance.
- **How will you structure each session?** Good tutors recap, teach in small steps and check understanding often.
- **Do you know my child's exam board and their access arrangements?** Exam practice should match both.
- **Will you share notes with me, and with the school if needed?** Joined-up support works best.

## Look after confidence as well as grades

Many dyslexic teenagers have spent years feeling slower than their friends, and some decide they're just not clever. In a 2019 survey, the British Dyslexia Association found that 95% of parents don't feel they have the skills or knowledge to support their dyslexic child at home, so you're far from alone if you're unsure where to start.

Praise effort and strategy, not just results. Point out what your child does well. And if anxiety or low mood is getting in the way, talk to the school's pastoral team or your GP alongside any tutoring.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do dyslexic students get extra time in GCSEs?

Not automatically. The school's qualified assessor has to test your child and find below-average scores in at least two areas of speed of working, such as reading or writing speed. Extra time also has to be your child's normal way of working in class and mocks.

### When is the deadline for GCSE access arrangements in 2027?

Schools must apply for most arrangements, including 25% extra time and readers, by 21 March 2027. Modified papers, such as large print, have an earlier deadline of 31 January 2027.

### How do I get my child assessed for dyslexia?

Start with the school's SENCo, who may screen your child or assess them for exam arrangements. For a full diagnosis, you'll need a private assessment with a specialist teacher or educational psychologist, because the NHS doesn't diagnose dyslexia.

### How much does a dyslexia assessment cost?

Through the British Dyslexia Association, an assessment costs £690 with a specialist teacher or £882 with a psychologist, including VAT. Independent assessors charge similar amounts.

### How can I help my dyslexic child revise?

Keep sessions short and active: flashcards, self-quizzing, diagrams and explaining aloud. Reduce reading with audio and text-to-speech, and focus spelling practice on the keywords that carry marks in each subject.

### What kind of tutor is best for a dyslexic child?

It depends on the gap. A specialist dyslexia teacher helps most if reading and spelling are well behind. A subject tutor with dyslexia experience is usually better at GCSE, when the aim is to turn understanding into marks.
