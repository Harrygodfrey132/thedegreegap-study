Short answer first, because nobody gives you one.

Mock exams do not count. No exam board sees them, they are not moderated by anyone outside the school, and they never appear on a certificate. That is true, and it is also the least useful true thing anybody will tell you this term, because mocks go on to decide four separate things that matter a great deal.

I run a tutoring agency, so read this knowing that. But the most valuable part of this post is the bit about reading the paper, and it costs nothing.

## What a mock actually is

A mock is a paper set and marked inside the school. Sometimes it is a past paper the exam board has released, sometimes it is a school-written approximation, and the marking standard is the school's own. Two schools can sit the same paper in the same week and grade it differently, one deliberately harshly to make a point in November and one closer to the real boundaries.

That is worth knowing before you read anything into the number. A grade 5 in one school's November mock and a grade 5 in another's are not the same statement.

## The four things a mock decides anyway

**Predicted grades.** This is the big one, and it is the reason Year 13 mocks in particular carry real weight. [UCAS guidance for advisers](https://www.ucas.com/advisers/help-and-training/guides-resources-and-training/application-overview/predicted-grades-what-you-need-to-know-for-entry-this-year) describes predicted grades as a holistic judgement drawing on a student's whole performance, and in practice teachers weight recent formal assessments heavily because those are the most current evidence they have. The predicted grade then decides which university offers arrive at all, long before a real paper is sat.

**Tier entry at GCSE.** In Maths and the Sciences, the decision about whether a student sits foundation or higher usually gets made in the winter or early spring of Year 11, and the autumn mock is the main evidence in that conversation. Foundation caps at grade 5. That is a decision with a ceiling attached, taken off one paper in November.

**Sets and groups.** Schools rearrange teaching groups on mock evidence. Sometimes that is good for a student and sometimes it is not, but it is rarely a conversation the family gets much say in unless they ask.

**What your child concludes about themselves.** The one nobody measures. A Year 11 who gets a 3 back in November and decides they are bad at Maths will stop attempting questions, and unattempted questions cannot be diagnosed by anybody. The damage from the conclusion often outlasts the gap that caused it.

## Read the paper, not the number

This is the part I would want a parent to take away.

When the paper comes home, ignore the grade for ten minutes and sort the questions into three piles.

**Blank.** Nothing attempted. This almost always means a topic that was never properly taught, or was taught in a fortnight your child missed. Revision does not fix it, because there is nothing there to revise. Once it is named it usually rebuilds quickly, because nothing has to be unlearned first.

**Started, then stalled.** Working that goes two steps and stops. This is method confidence. The student knows the individual steps and cannot see the order to put them in. It needs practice with someone watching, not re-teaching.

**Finished, lost marks near the end.** Clean working that does not quite land. This is exam technique, and it is the fastest thing on this list to move, because the content is already in place.

Three different problems. On a school report they look identical, and they need three different terms of work. Half an hour at the kitchen table with a marked paper will tell you more about what your child needs than the grade at the top of it ever will.

## A bad mock in November is good news

Not the sentence anyone wants, but the arithmetic supports it.

Exams start in the second week of May. A result that arrives in late November leaves roughly twenty-five to thirty teaching weeks, which is enough time to find a missing topic and actually teach it. The same result arriving in March leaves eight or nine, which is only enough time to convert what a student already knows into marks.

Both are worth acting on. Only one of them can rebuild content. If the news is going to be bad, November is when you want it.

## And a good mock can be hiding something

Worth saying, because the relief is real and sometimes premature.

Autumn mocks are frequently sat on partial content. A Year 11 in November may have covered sixty or seventy per cent of the specification, and the paper will have been cut to fit. A grade 6 on two thirds of the course is not the same as a grade 6 on all of it, especially where the topics still to come are the ones that historically cost the most marks.

The question worth asking the school is simple: what proportion of the specification did this paper cover, and which of the harder topics were not on it. Most teachers will answer that straight away.

## What to do in the week after results

1. Read the paper before you react to the grade. Three piles, as above.
2. Ask the subject teacher one question: if you had an hour a week alone with my child, what would you work on. The answer is free, specific and usually right.
3. If it is Year 11 Maths or Science, ask directly whether this result changes the tier decision, and when that decision gets made.
4. If it is Year 13, ask what predicted grade this points to, because that number is being formed now whether or not anyone has said it out loud.
5. Decide on one subject. Not three.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do mock results go on my child's record anywhere?**
No. Mocks are internal, no exam board sees them and they do not appear on any certificate. They do influence predicted grades, tier entry and set placement, which is a different kind of permanence.

**My child's mock was much worse than their classwork. Which one is real?**
Usually both, describing different things. Classwork measures whether a student understands the material with support and without time pressure. A mock measures whether they can produce it alone, in silence, against a clock. A large gap between the two is an exam technique problem rather than a knowledge problem, and it is one of the more fixable ones.

**Are A-Level mocks more important than GCSE mocks?**
For a Year 13, yes, because of predicted grades and the UCAS timetable. A Year 13 mock in the autumn is the most recent formal evidence a teacher has when writing a prediction, and that prediction decides which offers arrive. GCSE mocks matter for different reasons, mostly tier entry and sets.

**Should we get a tutor straight after a bad mock?**
Not straight away. Read the paper first, then talk to the subject teacher, then decide. Some of what comes back from those two steps is fixable at home for nothing. If what is left is a genuine missing topic or a technique gap that nobody has time to work on in a class of thirty, that is the point where an hour a week starts earning its keep.

**How much can realistically change between November and May?**
It depends entirely on which of the three piles the marks are being lost in, which is why the sorting exercise matters more than any general answer. What I can say honestly is that we see more change from students who found out in November than from students who found out in March, and the difference is time rather than talent.
