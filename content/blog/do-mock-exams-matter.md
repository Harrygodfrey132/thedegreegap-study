Short answer first, because nobody gives you one.

Mock exams don't count. No exam board sees them, nobody outside the school checks the marking, and they never appear on a certificate. All true. It's also the least useful true thing anyone will tell you this term, because mocks go on to decide four separate things that matter a great deal.

I run a tutoring agency, and you should know that going in. But the most useful part of this post is the bit about reading the paper, and that costs nothing.

## What a mock actually is

A mock is a paper set and marked inside your child's school. Sometimes it's a past paper the exam board has released. Sometimes it's the school's own version of one. Either way, the marking standard is the school's. Two schools can use the same paper in the same week and mark it differently. One marks harshly on purpose, to make a point in November. The other sticks closer to the real grade boundaries.

That's worth knowing before you read anything into the number. A grade 5 in one school's November mock and a grade 5 in another's aren't saying the same thing.

## The four things a mock decides anyway

**Predicted grades.** This is the big one, and it's why Year 13 mocks carry so much weight. [UCAS guidance for advisers](https://www.ucas.com/advisers/help-and-training/guides-resources-and-training/application-overview/predicted-grades-what-you-need-to-know-for-entry-this-year) describes a predicted grade as an overall judgement, drawing on everything a student has shown. In practice, teachers lean hard on recent tests sat in exam conditions, because those are the most up-to-date evidence they've got. And the predicted grade then decides which university offers arrive at all, long before your child sits a real paper.

**Tier entry at GCSE.** In Maths and the Sciences, the decision about whether your child sits the foundation paper or the higher one usually gets made in the winter or early spring of Year 11. The autumn mock is the main evidence in that conversation. Foundation caps at grade 5. So it's a decision with a ceiling attached, made on the back of one paper in November.

**Sets and groups.** Schools move children between teaching groups based on mock results. Sometimes that's good for your child and sometimes it isn't. But it's rarely a conversation families get much say in, unless they ask.

**What your child concludes about themselves.** The one nobody measures. A Year 11 who gets a 3 back in November and decides they're bad at Maths will stop attempting questions. And if there's nothing on the page, nobody can see where it's going wrong. The damage from that conclusion often outlasts the gap that caused it.

## Read the paper, not the number

This is the bit I'd most want you to take away.

When the paper comes home, ignore the grade for ten minutes and sort the questions into three piles.

**Blank.** Nothing attempted. This nearly always means a topic that was never properly taught, or one taught in a fortnight your child missed. Revision won't fix it, because there's nothing there to revise yet. Once someone spots it, it usually goes in quickly, because nothing has to be unlearned first.

**Started, then stalled.** Working that goes two steps and stops. This is about confidence with the method. Your child knows the separate steps but can't see what order they go in. That needs practice with someone watching, not teaching it all again from scratch.

**Finished, lost marks near the end.** Clean working that doesn't quite land. This is exam technique, and it's the quickest thing on this list to move, because the content's already there.

Three different problems. On a school report they look identical, and each one needs a different kind of work. Half an hour at the kitchen table with a marked paper will tell you more about what your child needs than the grade at the top of it ever will.

## A bad mock in November is good news

Not what anyone wants to hear. But the arithmetic backs it up.

The summer exams start in the second week of May. A result that arrives in late November leaves roughly twenty teaching weeks once the holidays are taken out, and that's enough time to find a missing topic and actually teach it. The same result in March leaves eight or nine. That's only enough time to turn what your child already knows into marks.

Both are worth acting on. Only one leaves time to teach something new. If the news is going to be bad, November's when you want it.

## And a good mock can be hiding something

Worth saying, because the relief is real, and sometimes it comes too soon.

Autumn mocks are often sat before the whole course has been taught. A Year 11 in November may have covered sixty or seventy per cent of it, and the paper will have been cut to fit. A grade 6 on two thirds of the course isn't the same as a grade 6 on all of it. That's even more true when the topics still to come are the ones that tend to cost the most marks.

So the question to ask the school is a simple one: how much of the course did this paper cover, and which of the harder topics weren't on it? Most teachers will answer that straight away.

## What to do in the week after results

1. Read the paper before you react to the grade. Three piles, as above.
2. Ask the subject teacher one question: if you had an hour a week alone with my child, what would you work on? The answer's free, specific and usually right.
3. If it's Year 11 Maths or Science, ask straight out whether this result changes the tier decision, and when that decision gets made.
4. If it's Year 13, ask what predicted grade this points to. That number's being formed now, whether or not anyone's said it out loud.
5. Pick one subject to start with. Not three.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do mock results go on my child's record anywhere?**
No. Mocks stay inside the school, no exam board sees them and they don't appear on any certificate. They do shape predicted grades, tier entry and which set your child is in, though, and those can stick for a lot longer than the mock itself.

**My child's mock was much worse than their classwork. Which one is real?**
Usually both. They're just measuring different things. Classwork shows whether your child understands the material with help to hand and no time pressure. A mock shows whether they can produce it alone, in silence, against a clock. A big gap between the two is an exam technique problem rather than a knowledge problem, and it's one of the more fixable ones.

**Are A-Level mocks more important than GCSE mocks?**
For a Year 13, yes, because of predicted grades and the UCAS timetable. A Year 13 mock in the autumn is the most recent hard evidence a teacher has when they write a prediction, and that prediction decides which offers arrive. GCSE mocks matter for other reasons, mostly tier entry and sets.

**Should we get a tutor straight after a bad mock?**
Not straight away. Read the paper first, talk to the subject teacher, then decide. Some of what comes out of those two steps you can fix at home for nothing. If what's left is a topic that's genuinely missing, or a technique gap nobody has time to work on in a class of thirty, that's when an hour a week starts earning its keep.

**How much can realistically change between November and May?**
It depends on which of the three piles most of the lost marks are in, and that's why sorting the paper matters more than any general answer. What we do see is more change in children who found out in November than in those who found out in March. The difference is time, not talent.
