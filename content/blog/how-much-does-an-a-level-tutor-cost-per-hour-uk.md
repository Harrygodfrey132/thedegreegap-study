Most parents come to A-Level tutoring having already paid for GCSE tutoring, and the first thing they notice is that the number went up. Sometimes by a little, sometimes by half again. The question we get asked on consultation calls, almost word for word, is *why does A-Level cost more when it is the same hour of the same tutor's time?*

It is a fair question and it has a real answer. This piece walks through the 2026 A-Level market, where the money actually goes, and how to read a quote.

## The short answer

Across the main UK platforms, agencies and independent tutors in 2026, A-Level rates cluster like this:

| Tier | Typical hourly rate | What it usually means |
|---|---|---|
| New tutor, strong degree, no track record | £30 to £40 | Recent graduate, often tutoring alongside a first job or postgraduate study. |
| Vetted graduate tutor | £40 to £55 | The mainstream tier. Subject specialist, usually a strong degree in the subject itself. |
| Qualified teacher (QTS, PGCE) | £50 to £70 | Currently or recently teaching the specification at sixth form. |
| Experienced tutor with a track record | £60 to £90 | Years of A-Level results behind them, often examiner experience. |
| London and admissions specialists | £90 to £120+ | Oxbridge route, medicine, or a specialist subject with very few tutors. |

The practical working range for most families is **£40 to £60 per hour**. [Tutorful's published benchmark for A-Level sits at £41.88](https://tutorcruncher.com/blog/average-tutoring-rates-uk), which is a reasonable floor for the vetted-graduate tier. Anything under £30 usually means a tutor early in their career or an unvetted marketplace listing. Anything over £90 usually means a specialist niche or a central London premium agency.

## Why A-Level costs more than GCSE

Our [GCSE cost research](/blog/how-much-does-a-gcse-tutor-cost-per-hour-uk/) put the national GCSE average at £35 to £45. A-Level sits roughly £10 to £20 above that. Four things drive the gap.

**The supply of tutors is much smaller.** Almost any strong graduate can teach GCSE Maths well. A-Level Maths, with pure, statistics and mechanics, plus the jump into A2 content, needs someone who genuinely holds the material. Narrow that to Further Maths, or A-Level Physics, or Latin, and the pool of people who can teach it properly is small. Small supply, higher price. That is most of the gap right there.

**The prep time is longer.** A GCSE session can often be run from a mark scheme and a past paper. An A-Level session frequently cannot, because the questions are synoptic and the tutor has to plan a route through several linked topics. Tutors price that unpaid prep into the hour.

**The stakes change the work.** At GCSE the job is usually to secure a pass or lift a grade band. At A-Level the grade is attached to a specific offer at a specific institution, so the tutor is working to a target rather than to a general improvement. That is a different kind of engagement and it tends to be priced accordingly.

**Specification depth.** AQA, Edexcel, OCR A and OCR B are meaningfully different at A-Level in a way they are not at GCSE. A tutor who knows your child's exact specification is worth paying for. One who is a term behind on a spec change is not.

## Where your subject sits

Subject is the single biggest swing factor after tutor experience. Broadly, in 2026:

- **Maths, Chemistry, Physics, Further Maths, Economics** sit at the top of the standard band, often £50 to £80. The content is technical, the tutor pool is smaller, and demand is high because these subjects sit behind competitive degree routes.
- **Biology, Psychology, History, Geography, English Literature** sit closer to the middle, roughly £40 to £60. Good supply of subject-specialist tutors and a large body of past-paper material.
- **Sociology, Business, Media Studies, Religious Studies** often sit at or slightly below the standard rate, roughly £35 to £50, because supply is comfortable.
- **Modern languages** vary widely. French and Spanish sit near the standard rate. Less commonly taught languages run higher because there are simply fewer A-Level-level tutors available.
- **Admissions-linked work** (medicine, Oxbridge interview prep, admissions tests) is priced as its own thing and routinely runs £90 and up.

## London and the regions

The London premium is the clearest regional pattern in the data. [Reported London rates run £45 to £120 per hour](https://www.hrbeducation.co.uk/blog/how-much-does-private-tutoring-cost-in-london), against a national working range of £40 to £60. Central London premium agencies push higher again.

Online tutoring flattens most of this. If you live in Norwich or Wigan and want a tutor who has taught the OCR A Chemistry specification for six years, online means you can have one without paying a London rate for the privilege of them driving to you. That is the single biggest practical change in the market over the last few years, and it works in favour of families outside the south east.

## What you are actually buying at each price point

This is the part that matters more than the number.

**At £35 to £45**, you are usually buying a recent graduate who did well in the subject themselves. That can be excellent value, especially for a student who understands the content but is losing marks on exam technique, because a strong recent graduate remembers exactly how they cracked the same papers. It is weaker value if your child has real gaps in the foundations, because teaching someone from a standing start is a skill that takes time to build.

**At £50 to £70**, you are usually buying a qualified teacher or an experienced specialist tutor. The difference shows up most when a student has lost confidence or has a genuine hole in the content. Pacing, sequencing and knowing when to slow down are teaching skills, and they are what the extra £15 an hour buys.

**At £80 and up**, you are buying either scarcity or examiner-level mark-scheme fluency. Sometimes that is exactly right, for example a Further Maths student who needs someone genuinely fluent in the harder modules. Often it is overkill for a student whose actual problem is that they have not done enough past papers under timed conditions.

The mistake we see most often is not parents underpaying. It is parents paying £80 an hour for a problem that £50 an hour would have solved, because the higher number felt safer.

## Hidden costs to check before you book

- **Platform or agency margin.** Most agencies and platforms retain a margin on the rate you pay. We do too. What matters is not whether a margin exists, it is whether the total you pay is clear and agreed before the first session.
- **Trial session terms.** Some tutors charge full rate for session one. Others do a shorter diagnostic first.
- **Cancellation windows.** Twenty four hours is standard. Anything stricter is worth knowing about in advance, especially with a Year 13 timetable full of mocks and open days.
- **Minimum commitments.** Subscription platforms often require a multi-month block. That is fine if the match works and expensive if it does not.
- **Session length.** A-Level sessions are frequently 90 minutes rather than 60. Compare the total weekly cost, not the headline hourly rate.

## How many hours you will actually need

Most A-Level students we work with run one hour a week per subject through term time, rising to two in the run-up to mocks and the summer papers. Over two terms that is roughly 24 to 30 sessions.

At £50 an hour, that is £1,200 to £1,500 per subject across the year. It is a real amount of money and worth being honest about. It is also worth being honest that a student who needs one subject shored up does not need three tutors, and a good agency will tell you that on the first call rather than after you have booked.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is A-Level Maths more expensive than A-Level Sociology?**
Supply. There are far more people who can teach Sociology to A-Level standard than there are people fluent in pure, statistics and mechanics at A2. The subject is not more important, the tutor pool is smaller.

**Is a qualified teacher worth the extra £15 an hour?**
It depends entirely on the problem. If your child has gaps in the underlying content, or has lost confidence and needs rebuilding, teaching experience earns the premium. If they understand the material and are dropping marks on technique and timing, a strong recent graduate is often just as effective for less.

**Should I pay more in Year 13 than Year 12?**
Rates do not usually change by year group, but demand does. Year 13 tutors get booked up early in the autumn term, and the good ones in competitive subjects are often full by October.

**Is online cheaper than in person for A-Level?**
Yes, typically 10% to 20% less for the same tutor, because travel time is not built into the rate. At A-Level the bigger argument for online is not cost, it is that you can access a specialist in the exact specification rather than whoever lives nearby.

**What about group A-Level tutoring?**
It exists and costs less per student, and it works reasonably well for revision blocks. It works poorly for the thing most A-Level students actually need, which is someone looking closely at their own written answers.
