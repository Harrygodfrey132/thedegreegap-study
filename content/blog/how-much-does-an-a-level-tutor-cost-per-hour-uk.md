Lots of parents come to us for A-Level help after paying for GCSE tutoring. The first thing they spot is that the price has gone up. Sometimes by a little. Sometimes by half again. And we hear the same question on consultation calls, almost word for word: *why does A-Level cost more when it's the same hour of the same tutor's time?*

It's a fair question, and there's a proper answer. Below I'll walk you through what A-Level tutors charge in 2026 and where the money actually goes. Then how to read a quote when one lands in your inbox.

## The short answer

Look across the main UK platforms, agencies and independent tutors in 2026, and A-Level rates fall into roughly these bands:

| Tier | Typical hourly rate | What it usually means |
|---|---|---|
| New tutor, strong degree, no track record | £30 to £40 | Recent graduate, often tutoring alongside a first job or postgraduate study. |
| Vetted graduate tutor | £40 to £55 | The mainstream tier. Subject specialist, usually a strong degree in the subject itself. |
| Qualified teacher (QTS, PGCE) | £50 to £70 | Currently or recently teaching the specification at sixth form. |
| Experienced tutor with a track record | £60 to £90 | Years of A-Level results behind them, often examiner experience. |
| London and admissions specialists | £90 to £120+ | Oxbridge route, medicine, or a specialist subject with very few tutors. |

For most families, the realistic range is **£40 to £60 per hour**. [Tutorful's published benchmark for A-Level sits at £41.88](https://tutorcruncher.com/blog/average-tutoring-rates-uk), which is a sensible floor for a vetted graduate tutor. If you're quoted under £30, it's usually someone early in their career, or a marketplace listing nobody has checked. Anything over £90 usually means something niche, like Oxbridge or medicine prep, or a premium agency in central London.

## Why A-Level costs more than GCSE

Our [GCSE cost research](/blog/how-much-does-a-gcse-tutor-cost-per-hour-uk/) put the national GCSE average at £35 to £45. A-Level sits roughly £10 to £20 above that. Four things explain the gap.

**The supply of tutors is much smaller.** Almost any strong graduate can teach GCSE Maths well. A-Level Maths is another matter. It covers pure, statistics and mechanics, then gets harder again in the second year. So your child needs a tutor who really knows the material. Narrow it to Further Maths, or A-Level Physics, or Latin, and there aren't many people who can teach it properly. Fewer tutors, higher price. That's most of the gap right there.

**The prep time is longer.** A tutor can often run a GCSE lesson from a past paper and its mark scheme. An A-Level lesson often needs more than that. One question can pull several topics together, and the tutor has to plan a route through them before your child logs on. That prep is unpaid, so tutors build it into the hourly rate.

**The stakes change the work.** At GCSE the job is usually to get a pass or lift a grade band. At A-Level the grade is tied to the offer your child is aiming for, so the tutor's working towards a set target. It's a different kind of job, and it tends to be priced that way.

**Specification depth.** The specification is the exact course your child's school follows. At A-Level, the AQA, Edexcel, OCR A and OCR B courses differ from each other far more than they do at GCSE. So a tutor who knows your child's course inside out is worth paying for. One who's a term behind on a change to it isn't.

## Where your subject sits

After the tutor's experience, the subject itself moves the price more than anything else. Broadly, in 2026:

- **Maths, Chemistry, Physics, Further Maths, Economics** sit at the top of the standard band, often £50 to £80. The content is technical and there are fewer tutors to go round. And demand is high, because competitive degree courses ask for these subjects.
- **Biology, Psychology, History, Geography, English Literature** sit closer to the middle, roughly £40 to £60. There are plenty of tutors who know these subjects well, and lots of past papers to practise on.
- **Sociology, Business, Media Studies, Religious Studies** often sit at or a little below the standard rate, roughly £35 to £50. There's no shortage of tutors.
- **Modern languages** vary a lot. French and Spanish sit near the standard rate. Less common languages cost more, simply because fewer tutors can teach them to A-Level standard.
- **Admissions-linked work** (medicine, Oxbridge interview prep, admissions tests) is priced as its own thing, and it routinely runs to £90 and up.

## London and the regions

London costs more. It's the clearest regional pattern in the data. [Reported London rates run £45 to £120 per hour](https://www.hrbeducation.co.uk/blog/how-much-does-private-tutoring-cost-in-london), against a national working range of £40 to £60. Premium agencies in central London go higher again.

Online tutoring irons out most of that. Say you live in Norwich or Wigan and you'd like a tutor who's taught the OCR A Chemistry course for six years. Online, your child can have one, and you won't pay a London rate for the privilege of them driving to your door. It's the biggest practical change in the market over the last few years, and it works in favour of families outside the south east.

## What you are actually buying at each price point

This bit matters more than the number.

**At £35 to £45**, you're usually paying for a recent graduate who did well in the subject themselves. That can be excellent value if your child understands the content but keeps dropping marks on exam technique. A strong recent graduate remembers exactly how they cracked those same papers. It's weaker value if there are real gaps in the basics, because teaching someone from a standing start is a skill that takes time to learn.

**At £50 to £70**, you're usually paying for a qualified teacher or a tutor with years of experience. You'll notice the difference most if your child has lost confidence or has a genuine hole in the content. Knowing how fast to go, what order to teach things in and when to slow down are teaching skills. They're what the extra £15 an hour buys.

**At £80 and up**, you're paying for one of two things: a tutor who's genuinely hard to find, or someone who knows the mark scheme the way an examiner does. Sometimes that's exactly right, say if your child's taking Further Maths and needs a tutor who's fluent in the harder topics. But it's often overkill if your child's real problem is that they haven't done enough past papers against the clock.

The mistake we see most often isn't parents paying too little. It's parents paying £80 an hour for a problem that £50 an hour would've solved, because the bigger number felt safer.

## Hidden costs to check before you book

- **Platform or agency margin.** Most agencies and platforms keep a margin on the rate you pay. We do too. What matters is that you know the full amount you'll pay, and have agreed it, before the first lesson.
- **Trial session terms.** Some tutors charge their normal rate from lesson one. Others start with a shorter session to find out where your child is.
- **Cancellation windows.** Twenty-four hours' notice is standard. If it's stricter than that, you'll want to know before you start, especially once a Year 13 calendar fills up with mocks and open days.
- **Minimum commitments.** Subscription platforms often want you to commit to a block of several months. That's fine if the match works, and expensive if it doesn't.
- **Session length.** A-Level lessons are often 90 minutes rather than 60. So compare what you'll pay each week, not just the hourly rate on the website.

## How many hours you will actually need

Most families we work with book one hour a week per subject in term time. That goes up to two in the run-up to mocks and the summer exams. Over two terms, that's roughly 24 to 30 lessons.

At £50 an hour, that's £1,200 to £1,500 per subject across the year. It's a real amount of money, and it's better to know that before you start. And if your child only needs one subject shoring up, they don't need three tutors. A good agency will tell you that on the first call, not after you've booked.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is A-Level Maths more expensive than A-Level Sociology?**
Supply. Far more people can teach Sociology to A-Level standard than can handle second-year pure, statistics and mechanics with real confidence. Maths isn't more important. There are just fewer tutors who can do it.

**Is a qualified teacher worth the extra £15 an hour?**
It depends on what's going wrong. If your child has gaps in the underlying content, or has lost confidence and needs building back up, teaching experience is worth the extra. If they understand the material but are dropping marks on technique and timing, a strong recent graduate often does just as well for less.

**Should I pay more in Year 13 than Year 12?**
You shouldn't have to. Rates don't usually change by year group, but demand does. Tutors for Year 13 get booked up early in the autumn term, and the good ones in competitive subjects are often full by October.

**Is online cheaper than in person for A-Level?**
Yes, usually 10% to 20% less for the same tutor, because there's no travel time built into the rate. But at A-Level the bigger win from going online is choice. Your child can work with a tutor who knows their exact course, rather than whoever happens to live nearby.

**What about group A-Level tutoring?**
It's cheaper per student, and it works reasonably well for blocks of revision. Where it falls down is the thing most A-Level students actually need: someone looking closely at their own written answers.
