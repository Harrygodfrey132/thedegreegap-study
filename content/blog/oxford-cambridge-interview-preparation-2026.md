If your child has just been shortlisted, congratulations: that alone puts them in a minority. The interview is where preparation makes the most visible difference, and where the wrong kind of preparation does the most damage. This guide explains what happens in December 2026, what tutors are actually assessing, and how to use the two or three weeks you have.

## The 2026 interview timeline

**Oxford.** Shortlisted applicants for 2027 entry are interviewed online in December 2026:

- Most courses interview between Monday 7 and Friday 18 December, with a few in the week of 30 November.
- Colleges email shortlisting decisions between mid-November and early December, giving at least seven days' notice.
- Oxford says interviews can't be rearranged.
- Decisions are released through UCAS on **12 January 2027**.

**Cambridge.** The main interview period is also 7 to 18 December 2026. Unlike Oxford, Cambridge leaves the format to each college, so some interviews are in person and some online. Most applicants have one or two interviews of 20 to 45 minutes. Strong applicants who aren't offered a place by their college can go into the "winter pool" for other colleges to consider in January.

## How competitive is it, really?

Oxford's Annual Admissions Statistical Report (June 2026) gives the 2025 figures: 23,329 applications, 3,891 offers and 3,302 students admitted. On its interviews page, Oxford says that in a typical year it shortlists around 10,000 candidates and holds around 20,000 online interviews, so most interviewees have two.

The arithmetic is worth doing. About 10,000 interviewees and 3,891 offers means roughly one in three to one in two-and-a-half interviewees gets an offer. The interview genuinely decides things.

Subject matters a lot. Oxford's Law Faculty report for 2025-26 shows 2,115 applicants, 630 interviewed and 252 offers. That's about 30% of applicants interviewed, and about 40% of those interviewed receiving an offer. Each Law candidate typically had two interviews of around 25 minutes at their first college.

Cambridge received 22,513 applications in 2025, made 4,893 offers and accepted 3,669 students. Cambridge doesn't publish an official interview rate. Independent estimates put it at roughly 65% to 80% of applicants, well above Oxford's, so at Cambridge the interview tends to carry even more of the decision.

## What Oxford and Cambridge tutors are actually assessing

Oxford describes its interviews as "academic conversations about your chosen subject, similar to a short tutorial and designed to explore how you think and engage with new ideas". Its advice to candidates is direct: "Don't worry if you don't know an answer immediately - see if you can work your way through it aloud. This lets tutors see how you think and means that your final answer is only part of what they find out."

In practice, tutors are asking themselves one question: would I enjoy teaching this student for three years? That breaks down into:

- **Thinking out loud.** Can they reason through an unfamiliar problem, text or source, and show the steps?
- **Taking a hint.** When the tutor nudges them, do they use it, or carry on down the same dead end?
- **Changing their mind.** Can they say "I hadn't thought of that, so let me reconsider" without falling apart?
- **Real engagement beyond the syllabus.** Can they talk in depth about something they've read or done, especially if it's in their personal statement?

Oxford's Law Faculty puts it plainly: tutors "are not testing for existing knowledge of law but want to test your on-the-spot thinking and ability to formulate arguments". The same is true in almost every subject.

## The mistakes we see most often

From working with applicants for Oxbridge, Medicine and other competitive courses, these are the patterns that cost students most.

**1. Memorised answers.** Students who learn a polished paragraph about "why this subject" sound rehearsed, and tutors move straight past it to an unseen problem. The prepared answer buys nothing.

**2. Silence while thinking.** Online, silence feels longer. A student who goes quiet for 40 seconds and then gives a correct answer has shown less than one who reasons aloud and gets halfway.

**3. Defending a wrong answer.** Tutors often push back on a correct answer to see what happens, and on a wrong one to see whether the student spots it. Digging in looks like an inability to learn.

**4. A personal statement they can't discuss.** If the statement mentions a book, a project or an idea, expect detailed questions about it. Students who listed a book they only skimmed get found out in the first five minutes.

**5. Reading more instead of practising conversation.** Reading is useful, but most students are under-practised at the actual skill being tested: talking through hard problems with an expert who interrupts.

## A two-week preparation plan

Most families get the invitation with two to three weeks to go. This is how we'd use that time.

**Days 1 to 3: get the practicalities right.** Agree with the school where the online interview will happen: a quiet room, a reliable connection, a laptop with a camera, and a backup plan. Watch Oxford's demonstration interview for the subject. Re-read the personal statement and any written work submitted, and list every claim a tutor could ask about.

**Days 4 to 7: practise thinking aloud, every day.** Spend 20 to 30 minutes a day working through unseen problems out loud with someone who will interrupt: a teacher, a tutor or a confident parent. For sciences and maths, that means unfamiliar problems built on A-Level content. For humanities and social sciences, it means close reading of an unseen text or source and building an argument on the spot.

**Days 8 to 11: two full mock interviews.** Ideally with two different interviewers who know the subject but not the student. Thirty minutes each, on video, with feedback straight afterwards. The aim is to make the format feel normal, not to predict the questions.

**Days 12 to 14: go deeper, then rest.** Pick one or two areas from the personal statement and go one level deeper than the statement does. Oxford sometimes sends pre-reading, usually by email the day before; do it, then stop. A tired candidate thinks more slowly.

## What parents can do (and what to avoid)

The most useful thing a parent can do is the logistics: the room, the tech, the timetable, the food. The next most useful is to be a calm practice partner. Ask "how would you work that out?" rather than "what's the answer?".

What to avoid: drilling lists of "famous Oxford interview questions". The questions are tailored to each candidate and designed to be unfamiliar. A student who has memorised answers to 200 questions is preparing for the wrong test.

## When a mock interview with a tutor is worth it

Not every student needs paid help. If your child's school runs mock interviews with subject specialists, use them. Outside support earns its cost when the school can't offer a subject expert, when the student freezes under questioning, or when they've only practised with friendly teachers who never push back.

A good Oxbridge interview tutor should:

- be a subject specialist who has been through the process themselves
- interview your child on unseen material rather than coach set answers
- give specific, written feedback after each session

Be wary of anyone who promises "the questions they'll ask".

## Frequently asked questions

### When are Oxford interviews in 2026?

Most Oxford interviews for 2027 entry take place online between 7 and 18 December 2026, with a small number in the week of 30 November. Exact dates vary by course and are published on each course page.

### Are Oxford interviews online in 2026?

Yes. Oxford holds all undergraduate interviews online. Cambridge decides the format college by college, so check your invitation.

### What percentage of Oxford applicants get an interview?

Oxford says it interviews around 10,000 of more than 23,000 applicants, so a little over 40% overall. It varies widely by subject: Law interviewed about 30% of applicants in 2025-26.

### Can you prepare for an Oxford or Cambridge interview?

Yes, but by practising reasoning aloud on unseen problems, not by memorising answers. Mock interviews with a subject specialist who will challenge your child are the most effective preparation.

### When will we hear back?

Oxford releases decisions to shortlisted applicants on 12 January 2027 via UCAS. Cambridge decisions also arrive in January, after the winter pool.

### What should my child wear for an online Oxbridge interview?

Something comfortable and tidy. Tutors aren't assessing clothes. A quiet, well-lit space and a stable connection matter far more.
