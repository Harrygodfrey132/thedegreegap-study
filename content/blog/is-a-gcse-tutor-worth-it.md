I run a tutoring agency, so you can reasonably assume my answer to this question. What I want to do instead is give you the version I give parents on consultation calls, which includes the cases where we tell people not to book.

An hour a week for two terms is roughly 24 to 30 sessions. At the standard 2026 rate that is somewhere around £900 to £1,400 per subject. That is a holiday. It deserves a straight answer rather than a sales pitch.

## What the independent evidence actually says

The most useful independent source is the Education Endowment Foundation, which reviews the research on school interventions and publishes what works. On one-to-one tuition, [their toolkit finds an average of around five additional months of progress](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/one-to-one-tuition), drawn from 123 studies.

Two caveats matter, and I would rather give them to you than have you find them yourself.

The first is that the effect is **smaller at secondary than primary**: around four months of additional progress at secondary level, against six at primary. GCSE students sit in the weaker half of that finding.

The second is that the EEF explicitly notes that many of the studies were not independently evaluated, and that evaluations run by organisations connected to the approach, including commercial providers, tend to report larger impacts. Read that as a warning about tutoring companies quoting their own numbers at you. Including, reasonably, this one.

So the honest summary of the evidence is: one-to-one tuition works, the effect at secondary is real but moderate, and you should be sceptical of anyone promising more than that.

It is also worth knowing how common this has become. The [Sutton Trust's Private Tutoring 2026 report](https://www.suttontrust.com/our-research/private-tutoring-2026/) found that 29% of secondary students in England and Wales have had private tutoring, rising to 45% in London. Year 11 is the peak year at 25%, up from 10% in Year 10. Whatever you decide, you are not making an unusual choice.

## The four things you are actually paying for

When tutoring works, in our experience it is because of these four things rather than anything mystical.

**1. Someone finds the actual gap.** This is the big one. A student who is "bad at Maths" is almost never bad at Maths. They are usually missing three or four specific things, often from two years earlier, and every topic built on top of those things wobbles. School cannot easily diagnose that with thirty students in a room. One person looking at one recent paper can do it in forty minutes.

**2. Exam technique, taught against the real mark scheme.** A very large share of lost GCSE marks are not knowledge failures. They are marks left on the table by answering a six-mark question with a three-mark answer, or misreading a command word, or running out of time on paper two every single time. This is learnable and it is fast to fix.

**3. Someone who is not you.** Plenty of parents can teach their own child GCSE Maths. Almost none can do it without it turning into an argument by week three. A tutor is a neutral adult in the room, and that changes the dynamic more than parents expect.

**4. Confidence, which sounds soft and is not.** A student who believes the subject is closed to them stops trying, and then the grade confirms the belief. Breaking that loop is often the whole job. One of the reviews we have had put it better than I can, from Omo, a parent of a GCSE student: "My son, who is in Year 10, really struggled with English and was completely disengaged from the subject. After six months of working with Malvina, he has improved from a grade 5 to a 6/7. He now looks forward to his lessons and has learned strategies that help him approach English in a way that resonates with him."

## When it is worth it

In our experience, tutoring pays for itself most clearly in these situations:

- **There is a specific, identifiable gap.** Your child was fine until a particular point, then something broke. This is the single best case for tutoring and the fastest to fix.
- **A grade boundary is genuinely in play.** Moving from a 3 to a 4 in Maths or English changes what happens next, because it is the difference between resitting post-16 and not. Moving a 5 to a 6, or a 6 to a 7, opens up subject choices. The value is concentrated at these boundaries.
- **They have lost confidence rather than ability.** Very common, very fixable, and school does not have the time to do it.
- **They are strong but scattergun.** Bright students who revise by rereading notes and never do a timed paper leave a lot of marks behind. Structure fixes that quickly.
- **A subject is taught by a teacher they have not clicked with.** It happens. It is nobody's fault. A different explanation of the same idea can be all it takes.

## When it is not worth it, and we say so

We turn work away for these reasons regularly, and I would rather you hear them now than after you have spent £1,200.

**The student does not want to be there.** This is the big one. Tutoring a teenager who has been signed up against their will does not work. It burns money and it damages their relationship with the subject further. If your child is flatly refusing, the first conversation is with them, not with an agency.

**You are booking four subjects at once in a panic.** A student doing four extra hours a week on top of school and homework will be exhausted by December. Almost always the right move is one or two subjects where the gap is real, done properly, for longer.

**There are eight weeks left and no foundation.** Tutoring in the final run-in is very good at converting knowledge into marks. It is poor at building two years of missing content from scratch. We say this to parents in March and April fairly often.

**Something else is going on.** If attendance has collapsed, or there is something happening at home, or there is an undiagnosed need, a weekly Maths tutor is not the intervention. Sometimes the honest answer on a consultation call is that we are not the right call to be on.

**School support has not been tried.** Free is better than £40 an hour. Intervention sessions, subject clinics and a conversation with the head of department cost nothing and sometimes solve it.

## How to tell whether it is working

Book with an exit route and check at the six-week mark. What you are looking for:

- Can your child tell you what they worked on and why? Vague answers mean the sessions have no plan.
- Has the tutor looked at an actual recent paper, or are they teaching from the top of the syllabus forwards?
- Is homework being set and marked?
- Has anything changed in class, in a school assessment, or in their willingness to talk about the subject?

If the answer to most of those is no after six weeks, the match is wrong. That is a normal thing to happen and it is worth saying so early. A good agency will move you to a different tutor rather than let it drift.

## The honest cost-benefit

The commercial argument, stated plainly: around £1,000 to £1,400 a year, per subject, buys you a moderate but real improvement in progress, concentrated most usefully around grade boundaries that decide what your child can do next.

Whether that is worth it depends on how close to a boundary they are and how much the next step matters to them. For a student sitting at a 3 who needs a 4 in English to avoid resitting it alongside their A-Levels, the maths is straightforward. For a student comfortably sitting at a 6 who wants a 7 in a subject they will drop at sixteen, it is much more of a judgement call, and the honest answer is sometimes no.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long before we see a difference?**
Exam technique changes can show up in four to six weeks. Rebuilding missing foundations takes a term or more. If someone promises you a grade jump in a month, be careful.

**Is one hour a week enough?**
For most students, yes, in term time, for one subject. The gains come from consistency rather than volume. Two hours a week in the run-up to mocks and exams is reasonable.

**Does it have to be a qualified teacher?**
No. It depends on the problem. Rebuilding foundations and pacing suit a teacher. Exam technique and recent-experience insight often suit a strong recent graduate who sat the same papers.

**What if my child does not get on with the tutor?**
Say so quickly. The match matters more than the credentials, and a fortnight of awkwardness helps nobody. Any decent agency will swap the tutor without a fuss.

**Is group tutoring a cheaper alternative?**
It is cheaper and it works for revision blocks. It does not do the diagnostic work, which is the part that produces most of the value.
