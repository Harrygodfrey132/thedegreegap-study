I run a tutoring agency, so you can probably guess my answer to this one. What I'd rather do is give you the version I give parents on our consultation calls. That includes the times we tell people not to book.

An hour a week for two terms is roughly 24 to 30 lessons. At the standard 2026 rate, that's somewhere around £900 to £1,400 per subject. That's a holiday. So it deserves a straight answer, not a sales pitch.

## What the independent evidence actually says

The most useful independent source is the Education Endowment Foundation. It looks at the research on the extra help schools can offer, and publishes what works. On one-to-one tuition, [their toolkit finds an average of around five additional months of progress](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/one-to-one-tuition), drawn from 123 studies.

There are two catches. I'd rather you heard them from me than found them yourself.

The first is that the effect is **smaller at secondary than primary**: around four months of extra progress at secondary, against six at primary. That puts a child doing GCSEs in the weaker half of that finding.

The second is that the EEF points out many of the studies weren't independently evaluated. And the ones run by organisations connected to the approach, including commercial providers, tend to report bigger impacts. Read that as a warning about tutoring companies quoting their own numbers at you. Including, reasonably, this one.

So here's the evidence in a sentence. One-to-one tuition works, the effect at secondary is real but moderate, and you should be sceptical of anyone promising more than that.

It's also worth knowing how common this has become. The [Sutton Trust's Private Tutoring 2026 report](https://www.suttontrust.com/our-research/private-tutoring-2026/) found that 29% of secondary students in England and Wales have had private tutoring, rising to 45% in London. Year 11 is the peak year at 25%, up from 10% in Year 10. Whatever you decide, you won't be the odd one out.

## The four things you are actually paying for

When tutoring works, in our experience it's down to these four things. Nothing mystical.

**1. Someone finds the actual gap.** This is the big one. A child who's "bad at Maths" is almost never bad at Maths. They're usually missing three or four specific things, often from two years earlier, and every topic built on top of them wobbles. With thirty in a class, a teacher can't easily spot that. One person looking at one recent paper can find it in forty minutes.

**2. Exam technique, taught against the real mark scheme.** A big share of the marks lost at GCSE aren't about what your child knows. They're left on the table. Often it's a three-mark answer to a six-mark question, or a misread command word (the instruction, like "describe" or "explain"). Or running out of time on paper two, every single time. It's learnable, and it's quick to fix.

**3. Someone who isn't you.** Plenty of parents could teach their own child GCSE Maths. Hardly any can do it without it turning into a row by week three. A tutor is a neutral adult in the room, and that changes the mood at home more than most parents expect.

**4. Confidence, which sounds soft and isn't.** A child who believes the subject is closed to them stops trying, and then the grade confirms the belief. Breaking that loop is often the whole job. One of our reviews puts it better than I can. It's from Omo, a parent of a GCSE student: "My son, who is in Year 10, really struggled with English and was completely disengaged from the subject. After six months of working with Malvina, he has improved from a grade 5 to a 6/7. He now looks forward to his lessons and has learned strategies that help him approach English in a way that resonates with him."

## When it is worth it

From what we've seen, tutoring earns its money most clearly in these situations:

- **There's a gap you can put your finger on.** Your child was fine until a certain point, then something broke. It's the single best case for tutoring, and the quickest to fix.
- **A grade boundary is genuinely in play.** Going from a 3 to a 4 in Maths or English changes what happens next. It's the difference between your child resitting it at sixth form or college and not. Moving a 5 to a 6, or a 6 to a 7, opens up subject choices. That's where most of the value sits: at those boundaries.
- **They've lost confidence, not ability.** Very common. Very fixable, too, and a busy school rarely has the time for it.
- **They're strong but scattergun.** Lots of bright teenagers revise by rereading their notes and never sit a timed paper. They leave a lot of marks behind, and a bit of structure sorts that quickly.
- **They haven't clicked with the teacher for one subject.** It happens. Nobody's fault. Sometimes a different explanation of the same idea is all it takes.

## When it is not worth it, and we say so

We turn work away for these reasons regularly, and I'd rather you heard them now than after you've spent £1,200.

**Your child really doesn't want to be there.** This one matters most. A bit of grumbling at the start is normal, and it often fades once they've met the tutor. But tutoring a teenager who's been signed up against their will doesn't work. It burns money, and it pushes them further away from the subject. If your child is flatly refusing, the first conversation is with them, not with an agency.

**You're booking four subjects at once in a panic.** Four extra hours a week on top of school and homework, and your child will be worn out by December. Almost always, the better move is one or two subjects where the gap is real, done properly, for longer.

**There are eight weeks left and no foundation.** In the final run-in, tutoring is very good at turning what your child already knows into marks. It's poor at building two years of missing content from scratch. We say this to parents in March and April fairly often.

**Something else is going on.** Maybe attendance has collapsed, or something's happening at home, or there's a need nobody has diagnosed yet. If so, a weekly Maths tutor isn't the answer. Sometimes the honest thing to say on a consultation call is that we're not the right call to be on.

**School support hasn't been tried.** Free beats £40 an hour. Catch-up sessions, subject clinics and a chat with the head of department cost nothing, and sometimes they solve it.

## How to tell whether it is working

Make sure you can stop at any point, and take stock at six weeks. Here's what to look for:

- Can your child tell you what they worked on, and why? Vague answers mean the lessons have no plan.
- Has the tutor looked at a real recent paper, or are they starting at the beginning of the course and working forwards?
- Is the tutor setting homework, and marking it?
- Has anything changed in class, in a school test, or in how willing your child is to talk about the subject?

If most of those are still a no after six weeks, the match is wrong. That's a normal thing to happen, and it's worth saying so early. A good agency will move you to a different tutor rather than let it drift.

## The honest cost-benefit

Here's the money side, put plainly. Around £1,000 to £1,400 a year, per subject, buys a moderate but real improvement in progress. It's most useful around the grade boundaries that decide what your child can do next.

Whether that's worth it depends on how close to a boundary your child is, and how much the next step matters to them. Say they're sitting at a 3 and need a 4 in English, so they don't have to resit it alongside their A-Levels. Then the sums are easy. If they're comfortably at a 6 and want a 7 in a subject they'll drop at sixteen, it's much more of a judgement call. And sometimes the honest answer is no.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long before we see a difference?**
Better exam technique can show up within four to six weeks. Rebuilding missing foundations takes a term or more. If anyone promises you a grade jump in a month, be wary.

**Is one hour a week enough?**
For one subject in term time, usually yes. The gains come from sticking with it every week, not from piling on hours. Two hours a week in the run-up to mocks and exams is reasonable.

**Does it have to be a qualified teacher?**
No, it depends on what's going wrong. Rebuilding foundations and getting the pace right suit a teacher. Exam technique, and an inside view of what the papers ask for, often suit a strong recent graduate who sat the same papers.

**What if my child doesn't get on with the tutor?**
Say so, and say it early. The match matters more than the credentials, and a fortnight of awkwardness helps nobody. Any decent agency will swap the tutor without a fuss.

**Is group tutoring a cheaper alternative?**
Yes, it's cheaper, and it works well for revision blocks. But it doesn't do the diagnostic work, finding your child's gap, and that's where most of the value comes from.
