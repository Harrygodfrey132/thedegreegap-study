The A-Level answer isn't the same as the GCSE one. And the difference is the thing most families miss.

At GCSE, starting earlier mostly buys you more time before the exams. That's a good enough reason on its own. At A-Level there's something else going on underneath, and it matters more. The work your child does in Year 12 becomes their predicted grade, and that prediction decides which universities they can realistically apply to. It's written down and sent off months before anyone sits a real A-Level paper.

So the short answer is the autumn of Year 12. Not because the exams are close. They aren't. It's because that's when the number that opens doors is quietly being put together.

## The dates that shape the decision

If your child is going into **Year 13** this September:

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| Autumn 2026 | Predicted grades finalised, largely from Year 12 performance |
| 15 October 2026, 6pm | UCAS deadline for Oxford and Cambridge, and most medicine, dentistry and veterinary courses |
| 13 January 2027, 6pm | Main UCAS deadline for 2027 entry |
| 10 May 2027 | Summer 2027 exams begin (provisional) |

If your child is going into **Year 12** this September, the exams are nearly two years away, and it's tempting to file the whole thing under "next year". But this is the year that produces the prediction they'll apply with in autumn 2027. The gain on offer is at its biggest right now, and right now you can't see it at all. That's why the autumn of Year 12 is the most underused window at A-Level, in the same way Year 10 is at GCSE.

Entry requirements vary a lot from course to course and university to university. Check the ones your child's interested in, rather than going on a general impression. The point here isn't which grades they'll need. It's that the prediction is made from Year 12 work, and Year 12 work is happening now.

## The Year 11 to Year 12 jump

It's the biggest single step up in UK education, and it catches almost everyone out.

Students right across the ability range leave Year 11 having done well on a GCSE course. Then the first Year 12 assessment comes back looking nothing like it. A child who was comfortable at GCSE can feel completely lost by October of Year 12. That doesn't mean something's gone wrong with your child. It means the course has changed shape.

What actually changed:

- **The volume roughly doubles**, and your child can't hold a whole subject in their head the night before any more.
- **Questions stop telling you what to do.** GCSE questions tend to point you to the method. A-Level questions often don't. A child who learned to spot question types, rather than choose a method, hits a wall.
- **Marks come from argument, not recall.** In essay subjects especially, the effort that got a strong GCSE answer gets a middling A-Level one. That's because the assessment objectives (the skills the mark scheme rewards) are after something different.
- **Nobody chases them.** The support that surrounded them in Year 11 thins out a lot.

The families who cope best treat the first bad Year 12 assessment as information, not a verdict. The ones who struggle wait to see whether it sorts itself out. And they find out in June that it didn't.

## The signals worth acting on

- **They can follow the lesson but can't start the question on their own.** It's the most reliable single sign at A-Level, and you won't spot it from a mark alone.
- **They're memorising worked examples rather than methods.** Which works, right up until the numbers change.
- **Their notes are copied, not processed.** Beautiful notes and low marks usually mean the thinking isn't happening.
- **Essay marks are stuck in one band despite real effort.** That's almost always structure and the assessment objectives, not knowledge. It's also one of the quicker things to fix.
- **Coursework or non-exam assessment is drifting.** Non-exam assessment is the exam boards' name for most coursework. The deadlines creep, and once the window closes those marks are hard to win back.
- **They've started talking about dropping the subject.** Sometimes dropping it is right. But it should be a decision, not a retreat from something that could have been fixed.
- **They went from four subjects to three and the three they kept didn't improve.** Then the problem wasn't workload.

## The case for waiting, which is real

I'm not going to tell you every Year 12 needs a tutor in September.

**Give the first term a proper look.** The jump is brutal, and a lot of students find their feet on their own by Christmas. Half a term of the new course is a fair look before you decide.

**Don't tutor all three subjects.** Booking three A-Level tutors in September is expensive, exhausting and usually unnecessary. Pick the subject that's blocking the plan, and do that one properly.

**Use the free things first, because at A-Level they're really good.** Exam boards publish their specifications (the official outline of the course your child's school follows), mark schemes and examiner reports for free. They're far more detailed than most families realise. Subject clinics and a direct chat with the head of department cost nothing either.

**If they don't want it, sort that out first.** A sixteen- or seventeen-year-old who hasn't agreed to tutoring won't get much from it. That conversation comes before the booking.

## What each point in the two years is good for

**September and October of Year 12.** The best value in the whole course. The foundations are still going in and more tutors are free than at any other point. An hour a week is also easy to keep up before the workload builds.

**November and December of Year 12.** The first real assessments land. A tutor working from an actual marked paper learns more in one lesson than from a term's worth of reported grades.

**January to March of Year 12.** The content's stacking up now. Gaps stop being one-offs and start spilling over into the next topic.

**The summer term of Year 12.** Worth flagging on its own. At a lot of schools, the end-of-Year-12 exams are the biggest single thing the predicted grade is based on. It varies, so ask your child's school directly what they base predictions on. If it's those exams, that's the deadline that really matters. And it's a year earlier than most parents think.

**September of Year 13.** Roughly the last point where work can still shift the prediction before it's sent. After that, the application is about presenting what's already there.

**January to March of Year 13.** The focus moves to technique, timing and past papers. Done well, that can be worth several marks a paper.

**April and May of Year 13.** Last-minute, yes, but not pointless. Timed papers and drilling against the mark scheme turn what your child knows into marks. It won't build anything new. We wrote about the late windows in more detail in [how far before the exams is too late](/blog/how-far-before-exams-is-too-late-to-get-a-tutor/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Year 12 too early to bother?**
No, it's the opposite. Year 12 is where the prediction comes from, and the prediction is what goes to universities. Tutoring in Year 13 works on grades. Tutoring in Year 12 works on grades and on options.

**They got strong GCSEs. Do they really need help?**
Sometimes not. But strong GCSEs don't tell you much about how your child will cope with the A-Level jump, because the two courses ask for different things. Judge the A-Level work on its own terms, not on what came before.

**They're aiming for a pass rather than top grades. Is tutoring still worth it?**
Yes, and it's where a lot of our work sits. Getting your child from the edge of a pass to a safe one changes what they can do next just as much as any other jump. And the fixes are often technique rather than content.

**How many subjects should we start with?**
One. Occasionally two. The most progress comes from a commitment your child actually keeps up, week in, week out, right through to May.

**When do A-Level tutors get booked up?**
Through the autumn term, mostly. Fewer people can teach Further Maths or A-Level Chemistry well than can teach the GCSE versions, so the pool is smaller. You start to notice it in who's free by about October.

**What if we start and it isn't working?**
Take stock after six weeks. By then there should be a plan, built from an early lesson spent finding the gaps. You should also see marked work coming back, and some change in how your child tackles a question. If those are missing, change the tutor rather than giving up on the idea.
