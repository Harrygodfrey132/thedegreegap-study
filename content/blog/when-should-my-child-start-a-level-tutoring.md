The A-Level answer is not the GCSE answer, and the difference is the thing most families miss.

At GCSE, starting earlier mostly buys you more time before the exams. That is a good enough reason on its own. At A-Level something else is going on underneath, and it matters more: the work your child does in Year 12 becomes the predicted grade, and the predicted grade decides which universities they can realistically apply to. That gets written down and sent off months before anyone sits a real A-Level paper.

So the short answer is the autumn of Year 12. Not because the exams are close, they are not, but because that is when the number that opens doors is quietly being formed.

## The dates that shape the decision

For a student going into **Year 13** this September:

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| Autumn 2026 | Predicted grades finalised, largely from Year 12 performance |
| 15 October 2026, 6pm | UCAS deadline for Oxford and Cambridge, and most medicine, dentistry and veterinary courses |
| 13 January 2027, 6pm | Main UCAS deadline for 2027 entry |
| 10 May 2027 | Summer 2027 exams begin (provisional) |

For a student going into **Year 12** this September, the exams are nearly two years away and it is tempting to file the whole thing under "next year". But this is the year that produces the prediction they will apply with in autumn 2027. The leverage is at its highest right now, and it is completely invisible right now. That combination is why the autumn of Year 12 is the most underused window at A-Level, in the same way Year 10 is at GCSE.

Entry requirements vary a great deal between courses and providers, so check the specific ones your child is interested in rather than working from a general impression. The point here is not which grades are needed. It is that the prediction is made from Year 12 work, and Year 12 work is happening now.

## The Year 11 to Year 12 jump

This is the single biggest step in UK education and it surprises almost everyone.

Students across the whole ability range come out of Year 11 having done well by the standards of a GCSE course, then get a first Year 12 assessment back that looks nothing like it. A student who was comfortable at GCSE can find themselves genuinely lost in October of Year 12. That is not a sign that something has gone wrong with them. It is a sign that the course changed shape.

What actually changed:

- **The volume roughly doubles**, and it stops being possible to hold a subject in your head the night before.
- **Questions stop telling you what to do.** GCSE questions tend to signpost the method. A-Level questions frequently do not, and a student who learned to recognise question types rather than choose methods hits a wall.
- **Marks come from argument, not recall.** In essay subjects especially, the same effort that produced a strong GCSE answer produces a middling A-Level one, because the assessment objectives reward something different.
- **Nobody chases them.** The support structure that surrounded Year 11 thins out considerably.

The families who handle this well are the ones who treat the first bad Year 12 assessment as information rather than a verdict. The ones who struggle are the ones who wait to see whether it sorts itself out, and find out in June that it did not.

## The signals worth acting on

- **They can follow the lesson but cannot start the question on their own.** This is the most reliable single indicator at A-Level, and it is invisible from a mark alone.
- **They are memorising worked examples rather than methods.** It works until the numbers change.
- **Their notes are copied, not processed.** Beautiful notes and low marks usually mean the thinking is not happening.
- **Essay marks are stuck in one band despite real effort.** That is almost always structure and assessment objectives, not knowledge, and it is one of the faster things to fix.
- **Coursework or non-exam assessment is drifting.** Deadlines here creep, and the marks are hard to recover once the window closes.
- **They have started talking about dropping the subject.** Sometimes dropping is right. But it should be a decision, not a retreat from something that could be fixed.
- **They dropped from four subjects to three and the remaining three did not improve.** The problem was not workload.

## The case for waiting, which is real

I am not going to argue that every Year 12 needs a tutor in September.

**Give the first term a genuine look.** The jump is brutal and a lot of students recalibrate on their own by Christmas. Half a term of the new course is a fair look before deciding.

**Do not tutor all three subjects.** Booking three A-Level tutors in September is expensive, exhausting and usually unnecessary. Pick the subject that is blocking the plan, and do that one properly.

**Use the free things first, because at A-Level they are genuinely good.** Exam board specifications, mark schemes and examiner reports are published free and are far more detailed than most families realise. Subject clinics and a direct conversation with the head of department cost nothing.

**If they do not want it, sort that out first.** A sixteen or seventeen year old who has not agreed to the tutoring will not get much from it. That conversation comes before the booking.

## What each point in the two years is good for

**September and October of Year 12.** The best value in the whole course. Foundations are still being laid, the tutor pool is at its widest, and one hour a week is easy to sustain before the workload builds.

**November and December of Year 12.** First real assessments land. A tutor working from an actual marked paper learns more in one session than from a term of reported grades.

**January to March of Year 12.** Content is stacking now. Gaps stop being isolated and start compounding into the next topic.

**The summer term of Year 12.** Worth flagging on its own. At a lot of schools the end-of-Year-12 exams are the largest single input into the predicted grade. Practice varies, so ask your child's school directly what they base predictions on. If the answer is those exams, that is the deadline that actually matters, and it is a year earlier than most parents assume.

**September of Year 13.** Roughly the last point at which work can still influence the prediction before it is submitted. After this the application is about presenting what already exists.

**January to March of Year 13.** The goal changes to technique, timing and past papers. Done well this is worth several marks a paper.

**April and May of Year 13.** Genuinely last-minute and still not pointless. Timed papers and mark-scheme drilling convert knowledge into marks. It will not build anything new. We wrote about the late windows in more detail in [how far before the exams is too late](/blog/how-far-before-exams-is-too-late-to-get-a-tutor/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Year 12 too early to bother?**
It is the opposite. Year 12 is where the prediction comes from, and the prediction is what gets sent to universities. Year 13 tutoring works on grades. Year 12 tutoring works on grades and options.

**They got strong GCSEs. Do they really need help?**
Sometimes not. But a strong GCSE result is a weak predictor of the A-Level jump, because the two courses ask for different things. Judge the A-Level work on its own terms rather than on what came before.

**They are aiming for a pass rather than top grades. Is tutoring still worth it?**
Yes, and the honest answer is that this is where a lot of our work sits. Moving a student from the edge of a pass into a secure one changes what they can do next just as much as any other jump, and the fixes are often technique rather than content.

**How many subjects should we start with?**
One. Occasionally two. The students who make the most progress are the ones with a commitment they actually sustain through to May.

**When do A-Level tutors get booked up?**
Subject specialists at A-Level are a smaller pool than at GCSE and tend to fill through the autumn term. Fewer people can teach Further Maths or A-Level Chemistry well than can teach the GCSE equivalents, and it shows in availability by about October.

**What if we start and it is not working?**
Review it at six weeks. You are looking for a plan, a diagnostic, marked work coming back, and some visible change in how your child approaches a question. If those are missing, change tutor rather than abandoning the idea.
