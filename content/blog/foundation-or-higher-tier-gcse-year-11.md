Most parents first see the word "tier" on a report in about February, written next to a decision that's already been made.

It's better to meet it now. Tier just means which version of the paper your child sits, foundation or higher. Year 11 starts in a fortnight, and the decision gets made somewhere between the autumn mocks and the entry deadline in February. It matters long after the exam, too, because **foundation tier maths cannot award a grade above 5**, and a grade 5 isn't enough for A-Level maths almost anywhere.

## Which subjects are tiered

Only some. In England, the tiered GCSEs are:

- **Maths**
- **Combined Science**, and the three separate sciences
- **Modern foreign languages**, such as French, German and Spanish

Most other subjects are single tier, including English Language, English Literature, history, geography, RS and computer science. Everyone sits the same paper, with every grade from 9 to 1 open to them. So if English is the subject your child finds hardest, tiering isn't part of the problem.

## What each tier can award

| Subject | Foundation | Higher |
|---|---|---|
| Maths | Grades 5 to 1 | Grades 9 to 4, with an allowed grade 3 |
| Combined Science | 5-5 to 1-1 | 9-9 to 4-4, with an allowed 4-3 |
| Separate sciences | Grades 5 to 1 | Grades 9 to 4, with an allowed grade 3 |
| Languages | Grades 5 to 1 | Grades 9 to 4, with an allowed grade 3 |

Two things in that table matter most.

**Foundation stops at 5.** If your child sits foundation and gets every single question right, they get a grade 5. There's no way to get a 6, 7 or 8 from a foundation paper.

**Higher has a floor, and it isn't a soft one.** The allowed grade 3 is a narrow safety net for a child who just misses the 4. Fall well below that and there's no 2 or 1 to land on. It's a **U** (ungraded), and that's a very different result from a 3.

So that's the whole decision. Foundation caps the ceiling. Higher removes the floor.

## When it actually gets decided

There's no single date for the whole country, which is part of why parents miss it.

| Roughly when | What happens |
|---|---|
| November and December | Year 11 mocks, usually sat at a provisional tier |
| January and February | Departments finalise entries, informed by those mocks |
| Around 21 February | The awarding bodies' entry deadline for the summer series |
| After that | Tier can often still be amended, board and deadline dependent |

The department will use the mock as its evidence, so it matters more than you'd expect from a practice paper. And **the entry isn't always final**. Schools can usually change a child's tier after the February deadline, sometimes quite late, though the cut-off depends on the exam board and there may be a fee. If the decision looks wrong in March, it's worth asking. Don't assume the door's shut.

## The trade-off, honestly

Roughly half of sixteen-year-olds taking GCSE maths are entered for foundation. That's a normal outcome, not a failure, and for a lot of children it's simply the right call.

**Foundation is right when a 4 or 5 is the realistic ceiling.** The paper is built for that range, so the questions are pitched where your child is. If they'd spend a third of a higher paper staring at questions they can't start, they'll do better, and feel better, on a paper they can actually have a go at. A secure 5 from foundation beats a shaky 4 from higher. And it beats a U by a mile.

**Higher is right when a 6 or above is genuinely in reach**, or when what comes next needs it. The risk is real. So is the cost of not taking it.

**The question that settles it isn't "which tier can they cope with".** It's this: *what does the next step need?* If your child's heading for a BTEC, an apprenticeship or A-Levels in essay subjects, they need a 4 or 5, and foundation gets them there with less risk. If they want A-Level maths, physics, chemistry or economics, most sixth forms will ask for a 6 or 7 in maths. Foundation rules that out before they've even sat the paper.

That's the version of this conversation parents don't usually get. And it's the only one that matters if your child has any interest in a science or maths A-Level.

## What to ask the school, and when

The time to ask is between the mocks and February. Here's what I'd ask:

**Which tier is my child currently on track for, in each tiered subject?** Go subject by subject. Maths and science can end up on different tiers. If your child takes the three separate sciences, each one has its own tier, so they could be on higher in one and foundation in another.

**What would they need to show in the mock to be entered for higher?** This turns a verdict into a target. Departments are usually specific if you ask them straight.

**When is the decision made, and can it be revisited?** Ask for the department's own dates, not just the exam board's deadline.

**Is my child on Combined or Triple Science?** Triple means the three separate sciences. Combined puts all three into one subject. It's a different question from tiering and gets decided earlier, but it can also affect A-Level options. Worth checking while you're asking.

**For languages: does the tier apply across all the papers?** In the current language GCSEs, your child is normally entered at one tier for listening, speaking, reading and writing, so it's one decision rather than four.

## What we would actually tell you

**A U is worse than a 3, and higher tier can produce one.** If your child is a long way below the grade 4 boundary in maths, a school pushing for foundation is protecting them. It isn't writing them off.

**But don't just accept foundation if your child wants A-Level sciences.** That's when the decision needs challenging, and it needs challenging in the autumn, while there's still time to change the evidence.

**The autumn is when this is winnable.** If your child is two grades short of higher tier in September, that can move by February. In April it can't. There's no time left to change the evidence, and the entry may well be fixed by then.

**Don't let the tier become the story.** We've been on calls with teenagers who'd decided they were "a foundation person". But the tier is just a paper, picked for one exam, on the evidence of one mock. It isn't a description of your child.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can my child be moved to a different tier after the entry deadline?**
Usually, yes. Exam boards accept changes for a while after the February deadline. The cut-off varies by board, and there's sometimes a fee. The school's exams officer can tell you the exact date.

**Is English tiered?**
No. English Language and English Literature are single tier, so every grade from 9 to 1 is open to your child.

**What's the highest grade my child can get on foundation?**
A grade 5. That's a strong pass, roughly a high C or low B on the old scale. Nothing above it is available.

**If they take higher and it goes badly, what do they get?**
A grade 4, or the allowed grade 3 if they only just miss it. Any lower and it's a U, because there are no grades 2 or 1 on the higher paper.

**Does a foundation grade 5 look different to a higher tier grade 5 on the certificate?**
No. The certificate shows the grade, not the tier. Sixth forms do sometimes ask which tier your child sat, especially for maths, because it tells them something the grade doesn't.

**My child is stronger in one science than the others. Which tier for Combined Science?**
It has to be one tier for all three, because Combined Science is entered as a single subject. That's exactly why it can be a hard call for a child whose sciences are uneven, and why it's worth asking how they did on each paper in the mock.
