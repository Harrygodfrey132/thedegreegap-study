Most parents meet the word "tier" for the first time on a report card in about February, written next to a decision that has already been made.

It is worth meeting it now instead, because your child is starting Year 11 in a fortnight, the decision gets taken somewhere between the autumn mocks and the entry deadline in February, and it has a consequence that lasts longer than the exam: **foundation tier maths cannot award a grade above 5**, and a grade 5 is not enough for A-Level maths almost anywhere.

## Which subjects are tiered

Only some are. In England, tiered GCSEs are:

- **Maths**
- **Combined Science**, and the three separate sciences
- **Modern foreign languages** — French, German and Spanish

Everything else — English Language, English Literature, history, geography, RS, computer science — is single tier. Every student sits the same paper and the full 9 to 1 range is available to all of them. If your child's difficult subject is English, tiering is not part of the problem.

## What each tier can award

| Subject | Foundation | Higher |
|---|---|---|
| Maths | Grades 5 to 1 | Grades 9 to 4, with an allowed grade 3 |
| Combined Science | 5-5 to 1-1 | 9-9 to 4-4, with an allowed 4-3 |
| Separate sciences | Grades 5 to 1 | Grades 9 to 4, with an allowed grade 3 |
| Languages | Grades 5 to 1 | Grades 9 to 4, with an allowed grade 3 |

Two things in that table do the work.

**Foundation stops at 5.** A student who sits foundation and answers every question correctly gets a grade 5. There is no route from a foundation paper to a 6, 7 or 8.

**Higher has a floor, and it is not a soft one.** The allowed grade 3 is a narrow safety net for a student who just misses the 4. A student who falls well below it does not get a 2 or a 1. They get a **U**, and the difference between a 3 and a U on a certificate is not nothing.

That asymmetry is the whole decision. Foundation caps the ceiling. Higher removes the floor.

## When it actually gets decided

There is no single national moment, which is part of why parents miss it.

| Roughly when | What happens |
|---|---|
| November and December | Year 11 mocks, usually sat at a provisional tier |
| January and February | Departments finalise entries, informed by those mocks |
| Around 21 February | The awarding bodies' entry deadline for the summer series |
| After that | Tier can often still be amended, board and deadline dependent |

The mock is the evidence the department will use, so the mock matters more than its status as a practice paper suggests. And **the entry is not always final**: schools can usually change a student's tier after the February deadline, sometimes quite late, though the cut-off varies by board and there may be a fee. If the decision looks wrong in March, it is worth asking rather than assuming it is closed.

## The trade-off, honestly

Roughly half of sixteen-year-olds taking GCSE maths are entered for foundation tier — in 2023 it was around 46% of awards at that age. It is a normal outcome, not a failure, and for a lot of students it is straightforwardly the right one.

**Foundation is right when a 4 or 5 is the realistic ceiling.** The paper is built for that range, the questions are accessible, and a student who would spend a third of a higher paper staring at questions they cannot start will do better, and feel better, on a paper they can actually attempt. A secure 5 from foundation beats a fragile 4 from higher, and it beats a U by a distance.

**Higher is right when a 6 or above is genuinely in reach**, or when what comes next requires it. The risk is real, and so is the cost of not taking it.

**The question that resolves it is not "which tier can they cope with".** It is: *what does the next step need?* A student heading for a BTEC, an apprenticeship or A-Levels in essay subjects needs a 4 or 5, and foundation delivers that with less risk. A student who wants A-Level maths, physics, chemistry or economics needs a 6 or 7 in maths at most sixth forms, and foundation makes that impossible before they have sat the paper.

That is the version of this conversation that parents do not usually get, and it is the only version that matters if your child has any interest in a science or maths A-Level.

## What to ask the school, and when

The useful window is between the mocks and February. Ask these:

**Which tier is my child currently on track for, in each tiered subject?** Ask per subject. It is common for a student to be higher in one science and foundation in another, and for maths to differ from both.

**What would they need to show in the mock to be entered for higher?** This turns a verdict into a target, and departments are usually specific when asked directly.

**When is the decision made, and can it be revisited?** Get the department's own timeline rather than the board deadline.

**Is my child on Combined or Triple Science?** A separate question from tiering, decided earlier, and one that also constrains A-Level options. Worth confirming at the same time.

**For languages: does the tier apply across all the papers?** In reformed language GCSEs students are normally entered at one tier across the skills, so it is a single decision rather than four.

## What we would actually tell you

**A U is worse than a 3, and higher tier can produce one.** If your child is a long way below the grade 4 boundary in maths, the school pushing for foundation is protecting them, not writing them off.

**But do not accept foundation for a child who wants A-Level sciences.** That is the case where the decision needs challenging, and it needs challenging in the autumn, while there is still time to change the evidence.

**The autumn is when this is winnable.** A student two grades below higher tier in September can move by February. A student in the same position in April cannot, because the entry has gone in and the paper is fixed.

**Do not let the tier become the story.** We have sat on calls with students who decided they were a foundation person. The tier is a paper, chosen for one exam, on the evidence of one mock. It is not a description of them.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can my child be moved to a different tier after the entry deadline?**
Usually yes. Boards accept amendments for a period after the February deadline, with the cut-off varying by board and sometimes a fee. Ask the exams officer for the specific date.

**Is English tiered?**
No. English Language and English Literature are single tier, and the full 9 to 1 range is open to every student.

**What is the highest grade from foundation tier?**
A grade 5, which is a strong pass and equivalent to a high C or low B on the old scale. Nothing above it is available.

**If they take higher and it goes badly, what do they get?**
A grade 4, or the allowed grade 3 if they narrowly miss. Below that it is a U — there are no grades 2 or 1 on the higher paper.

**Does a foundation grade 5 look different to a higher tier grade 5 on the certificate?**
No. The certificate shows the grade, not the tier. Sixth forms sometimes ask, particularly for maths, because the tier tells them something the grade does not.

**Which tier for Combined Science if the sciences are uneven?**
Combined Science is entered as one subject at one tier, so it is a single decision across all three. That is exactly why an uneven student can end up debating it, and why the mock evidence per paper is worth asking about.
