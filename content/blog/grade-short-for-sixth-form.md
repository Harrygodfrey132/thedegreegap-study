Got asked this three times in the last fortnight, so here it is properly.

The short answer first. If it's September and your child is a grade short, you've got about thirty teaching weeks before the exams, and that's the best position anyone in this situation can be in. If it's August and the results are already in, the route is almost never closed. It just changes shape. Both are worth taking seriously. But neither is the emergency it feels like at 10pm on the day you find out.

I run a tutoring agency, so bear that in mind as you read. Most of what follows costs nothing, though.

## First, find out what the requirement actually says

More families get this wrong than you'd think, and it's the cheapest mistake to fix.

Every sixth form and college publishes its own entry requirements, and they're usually set subject by subject. They change from one year to the next, too. So if you're going on what an older sibling needed in 2024, or what a friend in the WhatsApp group remembers, you could easily be working from a number that's no longer the number.

And they really do differ. Take three examples, all published online: [Durham Johnston](https://www.durhamjohnston.org.uk/6thform/admissions), [Sale Grammar School](https://www.salegrammar.co.uk/160/sixth-form-entry-requirements-and-an-overview-of-the-application-process) and the [University of Birmingham School](https://uobschool.org.uk/our-sixth-form/entry-requirements/). All three set out their criteria openly, and all three do it differently, with a different overall bar and different conditions for each subject on top. There's no single national standard. That's exactly why the only page worth reading is the one for the place your child actually wants to go.

So your first job this week is to open that page and write down two things. The overall requirement across all their GCSEs, and the grades each of their A-Level choices asks for. They're often different numbers, and it's the second one that catches people out.

## "A grade short" is three different problems

People talk about them as if they're one thing. They aren't, and each one needs a different response.

**Short in a subject they want to carry on with.** When a sixth form asks for a 6 in Chemistry before your child can take A-Level Chemistry, it's really asking whether they'll cope with the step up. This is the most fixable version. It's one subject, and you know exactly what you're aiming for.

**Short in English or Maths.** These two sit underneath almost everything, even courses that have nothing to do with either subject. More on this below, because the rules aren't what most people assume.

**Short on the overall threshold.** Some sixth forms look at an average, or a points total, across every GCSE your child sits. Your child can meet every single subject requirement and still miss this one, usually because of two or three subjects nobody was worried about.

Work out which of the three you've got before you decide anything, because the answer to "what do we do" is different for each.

## The English and Maths one works differently

This is the bit that gets misreported all the time, so I'll be precise about it.

If your child finishes Year 11 without at least a grade 4 in GCSE English or Maths, the [Department for Education's condition of funding for 2026 to 2027](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/16-to-19-funding-maths-and-english-condition-of-funding/2026-to-2027-academic-year-16-to-19-funding-maths-and-english-condition-of-funding) says their sixth form or college has to keep teaching them that subject. That means at least 100 planned teaching hours in the year for each subject involved.

Read that twice, because the version most people have heard is wrong. The rule puts a duty on the college to teach your child. It doesn't shut them out. And it's written around the teaching, not around sitting one particular exam, so whether and when your child sits a resit is something you'd talk through with the college. It isn't decided for them automatically.

What that means in practice: a grade 3 in Maths usually isn't a closed door. It's a subject your child carries into Year 12 alongside everything else. That's more work, but it's normal, and it's a very well-trodden path.

## What a year actually buys you

If you're reading this in September of Year 11, here's the simple sum. Exams start in the second week of May. Take out the holidays and that's somewhere around thirty teaching weeks. Enough time to do the thing that actually moves a grade: find out what's missing, and rebuild it.

I won't tell you how many grades tutoring adds, because anyone who promises you a grade is selling something. What I will say is this. The kind of gap decides how long it takes, more than the size of it does. If your child understands the work but loses marks towards the end of questions, that's exam technique, and it's the quickest thing there is to move. If there's blank space on whole types of question, that's a topic that never properly went in. That takes a term, because it has to be built before it can be practised.

On a school report, those two look exactly the same. But you can tell them apart in about an hour, if someone reads the working on a marked paper instead of the score at the top. Do that before you spend anything.

## If you are reading this in August, having just missed

Different situation, shorter list, and more options than it feels like on the day.

**Talk to the sixth form or college before you assume anything.** Places move in the days after results. Other students change their minds, and sixth forms often have more room to bend than they let on. If your child missed by one mark in one subject and has a strong case everywhere else, that's a conversation worth having before you give up on the place.

**Ask the school about a review of marking.** If a grade looks wrong, especially where the mock and the coursework said something different, the school can ask the exam board to look at the marking again for you. There are deadlines, and a grade can go down as well as up. So ask the school what they honestly think before you request one.

**Look at the November resits for English and Maths.** English Language and Maths are the only GCSEs with a resit in the autumn. It's a really useful route if your child was close. It's much less useful if they were a long way off, because there are only a few weeks to get ready.

**Ask what else the sixth form or college runs.** If your child misses the grades for A-Levels, they can often do a Level 3 vocational course at the same place instead. It's the same level as A-Levels. A proper qualification, not a consolation prize.

## What to do this week

1. Open this year's entry requirements page for the sixth form or college. Not last year's, and not a friend's memory of it.
2. Write down the overall requirement, and the grade needed for each A-Level your child wants to take.
3. Work out which of the three problems above you've actually got.
4. Find a recent marked paper in the subject your child's short in, and look at the working, not the grade. Blank space and attempts that stall halfway mean different things.
5. Then decide whether your child needs help, and in which one subject. Almost nobody needs it in three.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a sixth form change its entry requirements between when we look and when we apply?**
Yes, and they do. Sixth forms and colleges look at their criteria again before each new intake, so what's published for one September isn't a guarantee for the next. Check the page again in the spring of Year 11, rather than relying on the version you read in September.

**My child is short in a subject they don't even want to take at A-Level. Does it matter?**
It can, if the sixth form sets an overall requirement as well as subject ones. That's why it's worth reading both parts. If there's only a subject requirement, and it isn't a subject your child's carrying on with, it usually matters much less.

**Is it too late to start tutoring in the spring of Year 11?**
No, but what you're paying for changes. From about February, the realistic job is turning what your child already knows into marks, rather than teaching them content they missed. That's worth doing, and it works. It's just a narrower job than the same hour would have been in October.

**How many subjects should we get help with?**
One or two. Almost never three. The students who make the most progress are the ones who keep it up for the whole year. Three subjects of weekly tutoring usually turns into none by February.

**Should we tell the school we've got a tutor?**
Yes, and most teachers are glad to hear it. Ask the subject teacher what they'd work on if they had an hour a week alone with your child. That answer costs nothing, it's specific, and it's usually right.
