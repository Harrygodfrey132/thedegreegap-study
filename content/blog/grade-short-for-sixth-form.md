Got asked this three times in the last fortnight, so here it is properly.

The honest answer first: if it is September and your child is a grade short, you have about thirty teaching weeks before the exams, which is the most useful position anyone in this situation can be in. If it is August and the results are already in, the route is almost never closed, it changes shape. Both are worth taking seriously and neither is the emergency it feels like at 10pm on the day you find out.

I run a tutoring agency, so read this knowing that. But most of what follows costs nothing.

## First, find out what the requirement actually says

More families get this wrong than you would think, and it is the cheapest mistake to fix.

Sixth form and college entry requirements are published, they are set per provider, and within a provider they are usually set per subject. They also move between years. A parent working from what an older sibling needed in 2024, or from what a friend in the WhatsApp group remembers, is often working from a number that is no longer the number.

The variation between providers is real. To take three published examples: [Durham Johnston](https://www.durhamjohnston.org.uk/6thform/admissions), [Sale Grammar School](https://www.salegrammar.co.uk/160/sixth-form-entry-requirements-and-an-overview-of-the-application-process) and the [University of Birmingham School](https://uobschool.org.uk/our-sixth-form/entry-requirements/) all publish their criteria openly and all express them differently, with different overall thresholds and different subject-level conditions on top. There is no single national standard, which is exactly why the only page worth reading is the one belonging to the place your child actually wants to go.

So the first job this week is to open that page, and write down two things: the overall threshold, and the specific subject requirement for each A-Level your child wants to take. Those are frequently different numbers, and the second one is the one that catches people.

## "A grade short" is three different problems

They get talked about as one thing and they need different responses.

**Short in a subject they want to carry on with.** A provider asking for a 6 in Chemistry to take A-Level Chemistry is making a judgement about whether the student can survive the step up. This is the most fixable version, because it is one subject and the target is precise.

**Short in English or Maths.** These sit underneath almost everything, including routes that have nothing to do with either subject. More on this below, because the rules here are different from what most people assume.

**Short on the overall threshold.** Some providers set an average or a points total across the whole set of GCSEs. A student can clear every individual subject requirement and still miss this one, usually because of two or three subjects nobody was worried about.

Work out which of the three you are dealing with before deciding anything, because the answer to "what do we do" is different in each case.

## The English and Maths one works differently

This is the part that gets misreported constantly, so it is worth being precise.

Where a student finishes Year 11 without a grade 4 or above in GCSE English or Maths, the [Department for Education's condition of funding for 2026 to 2027](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/16-to-19-funding-maths-and-english-condition-of-funding/2026-to-2027-academic-year-16-to-19-funding-maths-and-english-condition-of-funding) requires their post-16 provider to keep teaching them the subject, with a minimum of 100 planned teaching hours in the year for each subject involved.

Read that carefully, because the common version of it is wrong. The rule is an obligation on the college to teach, not a rule that shuts a student out. It also frames the requirement around teaching rather than around sitting one specific exam series, so whether and when your child enters a particular resit is a separate conversation with the provider rather than something automatically decided for them.

What that means practically: a grade 3 in Maths is not usually a closed door. It is a subject your child carries into Year 12 alongside everything else, which is more work but is a normal and very well-trodden path.

## What a year actually buys you

If you are reading this in September of Year 11, here is the honest arithmetic. Exams start in the second week of May. That is somewhere around thirty teaching weeks once you take out the holidays, and it is enough time to do the thing that actually moves a grade, which is find out what is missing and rebuild it.

I will not put a number on what tutoring delivers, because anyone who promises you a grade is selling something. What I will say is that the shape of the problem decides the timeline more than the size of the gap does. A student who understands the material and loses marks near the end of questions has an exam technique problem, and that is the fastest thing there is to move. A student with blank space on whole question types has a topic that was never properly taught, and that takes a term because something has to be built before it can be practised.

Those two look identical on a school report. They are visible in about an hour if someone reads the working on a marked paper rather than the score at the top. Do that before you spend anything.

## If you are reading this in August, having just missed

Different situation, shorter list, and more options than it feels like on the day.

**Talk to the provider before you assume anything.** Places move in the days after results, students change their minds, and providers frequently have discretion they do not advertise. A student who missed by one mark in one subject and has a strong case elsewhere is a conversation worth having rather than a form worth abandoning.

**Ask the school about a review of marking.** If a grade looks wrong, especially on a subject where the mock and the coursework said something different, your school can request a review from the exam board on your behalf. There are deadlines and there is a possibility that a grade moves down as well as up, so ask the school for their honest read before requesting one.

**Look at the November series for English and Maths.** Those two subjects have an autumn resit sitting, which no other GCSE subject does. It is a genuinely useful route for a student who was close, and much less useful for a student who was a long way off, because there are only a few weeks to prepare.

**Ask what else the provider runs.** A student who misses an A-Level threshold is often eligible for a Level 3 vocational route at the same institution, which is a real qualification and not a consolation prize.

## What to do this week

1. Open the provider's current entry requirements page. Not last year's, not a friend's memory.
2. Write down the overall threshold and the subject-level requirement for each A-Level your child wants.
3. Work out which of the three problems above you actually have.
4. Find a recent marked paper in the subject you are short in and look at the working rather than the grade. Blank space and stalled attempts mean different things.
5. Then decide whether you need help, and in which single subject. Almost nobody needs it in three.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a sixth form change its entry requirements between when we look and when we apply?**
Yes, and they do. Providers review criteria between admissions cycles, so a requirement published for one September intake is not a guarantee for the next. Check the page again in the spring of Year 11 rather than relying on the version you read in September.

**My child is short in a subject they do not even want to take at A-Level. Does it matter?**
It can, if the provider sets an overall threshold as well as subject requirements. That is why it is worth reading both parts of the criteria. Where only a subject requirement applies and it is not a subject your child is carrying on with, it usually matters much less.

**Is it too late to start tutoring in the spring of Year 11?**
No, but what you are buying changes. From about February onward, the realistic job is converting what your child already knows into marks rather than teaching them content they missed. That is worth doing and it works. It is simply a narrower job than the same hour would have been in October.

**How many subjects should we get help with?**
One or two, almost never three. The students who make the most progress are the ones with a commitment they actually keep for the whole year. Three subjects of weekly tutoring usually becomes none by February.

**Should we tell the school we have a tutor?**
Yes, and most teachers are glad to hear it. It is worth asking the subject teacher what they would work on if they had an hour a week alone with your child. That answer is free, it is specific, and it is usually right.
