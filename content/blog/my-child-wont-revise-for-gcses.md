It's November of Year 11, the mocks are coming or have just happened, and your teenager has done almost nothing. Every conversation about revision ends in a slammed door. You're not imagining it, you're not alone, and it's fixable. But the fix is rarely the one parents reach for first.

This guide explains why teenagers avoid revision, what the research says parents should and shouldn't do, and a plan for the months before May. If you'd like to talk about your child specifically, [book a free 30-minute call with our team](/book-a-call/). We speak to parents in exactly this situation every week.

## First, the timeline you're working with

The summer 2027 GCSE exams start on **Monday 10 May 2027** and finish on Friday 18 June, with results on Thursday 19 August. For most students the first big paper is maths Paper 1, on Friday 14 May.

From mid-November that's about 25 weeks, minus Christmas, February half term and Easter. That's enough time to change a grade in several subjects. It isn't enough time to spend another three months arguing.

## Why teenagers don't revise (it's rarely laziness)

When we talk to students who "won't revise", the reason is almost never that they don't care. It's usually one of these.

**1. They don't know how.** Most students have never been taught to revise. They reread their notes, get bored, decide they're "bad at revising" and stop. Rereading feels like work but achieves little.

**2. They don't know where to start.** Eleven subjects, each with dozens of topics. Faced with all of it, many teenagers freeze and do nothing.

**3. They're protecting themselves.** If you don't try, a bad grade doesn't mean you're not clever. For a student who is anxious about failing, not revising keeps that fear at a distance. This is especially common after a poor mock.

**4. A real gap in one subject is dragging everything down.** A student who is lost in maths or chemistry often stops revising everything, because the hard subject makes the whole thing feel hopeless.

**5. The phone wins.** Not because they're weak, but because phones are designed to win against anything that feels hard and slow.

The right response depends on which of these is going on, so the first job is to find out.

## What the evidence says parents should (and shouldn't) do

The Education Endowment Foundation (EEF), which reviews research for schools in England, finds that parental engagement has a positive average impact of several months' extra progress. Most of that evidence comes from younger children, though, and the evidence for secondary school is thinner.

The most useful finding for parents of teenagers is about *how* to help. The EEF's guidance on working with parents says parents should support children to do their work "rather than get directly involved in the actual assignments". It also says parental support can build the self-discipline needed to reach goals: goal-setting, planning and perseverance. In other words, at GCSE you're most useful as a coach and organiser, not as a teacher.

That matches what we see. Parents who try to re-teach maths usually use a different method from the school and add confusion. Parents who help their child plan the week, protect the time and notice the effort tend to get much further.

## The conversation that works better than nagging

"Have you revised?" invites either "yes" or a row. Try these instead:

- "What's the subject you're most worried about?"
- "If you could only fix one thing before the mocks, what would it be?"
- "Show me what you did today, in five minutes."

Then listen. You're trying to work out which of the five reasons is in play:

- A student who says "I don't know where to start" needs a plan.
- A student who says "what's the point, I'll fail maths anyway" needs a quick win in maths.
- A student who says nothing at all may be more anxious than they're letting on.

## A plan for the next 25 weeks

**Step 1: Use the mocks as a map, not a verdict.** A mock never goes to an exam board; its value is in what it shows. Sit down with the results and the papers and sort subjects into three groups: on track, close, and in trouble. Ask the school specifically about tier for maths and science, because tier decisions are often based on a mock. Our [GCSE mocks guide](/gcse-mocks/) goes through this step by step.

**Step 2: Start with one subject, not eleven.** Pick the subject where the gap is biggest or the stakes are highest, usually maths or English for sixth form entry. One small, visible improvement does more for motivation than a perfect timetable.

**Step 3: Make sessions short and active.** Thirty minutes, phone in another room, then stop. Each session should end with the student *doing* something: answering questions, testing themselves with flashcards, or doing an exam question and checking it against the mark scheme. Our [free revision timetable](/revision-timetable/) can help structure this.

**Step 4: Build up gradually.** In November and December, three or four short sessions a week is a realistic start. By February, most Year 11s can manage an hour or more a day. By Easter, full past papers under timed conditions.

**Step 5: Keep your role clear.** You handle the environment: food, sleep, a quiet space, the timetable on the fridge, and noticing effort. They handle the content.

**Step 6: Know when to step back.** If every revision conversation turns into a fight, hand the content side to someone else (a teacher, a revision club or a tutor) and protect the relationship. A teenager who feels supported revises more than one who feels watched.

## Anxiety, avoidance or something more?

Some students who won't revise are dealing with genuine anxiety, low mood or an undiagnosed learning difficulty. Signs worth taking seriously include poor sleep, withdrawal from friends, physical symptoms before school, or a sudden drop across every subject. If you see these, talk to the school's pastoral team or your GP first. Tutoring can build confidence in a subject, but it's no substitute for support with wellbeing.

## When a tutor helps (and when it doesn't)

Sutton Trust polling published in 2026 found that 29% of secondary pupils in England and Wales have had private tutoring at some point, and that Year 11 is the most likely single year for it, at 25%. That's because Year 11 is when the gap between "I'll do it later" and the exam date becomes real.

A tutor tends to help most when:

- the block is one or two subjects, and that subject is dragging motivation down everywhere else
- the student needs someone outside the family to set and check work, so home stops being a battleground
- the student doesn't know how to revise a particular subject and needs to be shown, using real exam questions

A tutor won't help much if the student is refusing all work for reasons that are really about wellbeing, or if the plan is to book three subjects and hope. Start with one subject, give it six to eight weeks, and judge it on whether your child's confidence and past-paper scores are moving.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why won't my teenager revise for their GCSEs?

Usually because they don't know how, don't know where to start, are anxious about failing, or are stuck in one subject that makes everything feel hopeless. Laziness is far less common than it looks.

### Should I make my child revise?

You can't force learning, and constant pressure tends to backfire. You'll get further by agreeing a small, specific plan together, protecting the time and environment, and noticing effort rather than checking up.

### Is it too late to start revising in Year 11?

No. From November there are about 25 weeks before the first exam on 10 May 2027. That's enough time to move grades if revision starts small and builds steadily.

### How much revision should a Year 11 do?

Start with three or four short sessions a week in the autumn and build to around an hour a day by February, rising before the exams. Consistency matters more than marathon sessions.

### Should I help with the actual work?

The evidence suggests parents help most by supporting planning, routine and motivation rather than doing the work alongside their child. Leave the subject content to teachers or a tutor.

### When does GCSE tutoring make the most difference?

When it targets one or two specific subjects and starts early enough to fix gaps before moving on to exam practice. Our guide on [how far before the exams is too late to get a tutor](/blog/how-far-before-exams-is-too-late-to-get-a-tutor/) covers this in detail.
