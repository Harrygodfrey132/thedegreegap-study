If your child has just missed a grade 4 in GCSE maths, or is waiting for November results and already suspects the worst, here is the honest picture. They are in a very large group. The odds on the next attempt are worse than most families assume. And the students who break the cycle almost always change how they prepare, rather than just trying again.

This guide covers the 2026/27 rules, the real pass rates, the summer 2027 dates and costs, and the plan we use with resit students. If you'd rather talk it through, you can [book a free 30-minute call with our team](/book-a-call/) and we'll tell you honestly whether a tutor is the right next step.

## First, the numbers nobody puts on the college leaflet

In summer 2026, 219,135 students aged 17 and over in England resat GCSE maths. Only 15.3% got a grade 4 or above, down from 17.1% the year before, according to JCQ results reported by FE Week. English Language resits did slightly better, at 19.8%.

The age breakdown matters more than the headline:

- **17-year-olds** (first resit after Year 11): 16.4% reached a grade 4
- **18-year-olds:** 10.4%
- **19-year-olds:** 10.3%
- **20 and over:** 32.5%

Two things stand out. First, the chances fall with every extra year a student spends in the resit loop. A 17-year-old has a better shot than an 18-year-old, so the best time to fix this is this academic year, not next. If a missing 4 is also standing between your child and their sixth form place, our guide for when [your child is a grade short for sixth form](/blog/grade-short-for-sixth-form/) covers that conversation. Second, adults aged 20 and over pass at roughly double the rate. In our experience that's because adults who resit have chosen to, usually for a clear reason, and they prepare differently. So motivation and method matter as much as ability.

The smaller November series also tends to get more students through than summer. FE Week's analysis of the November 2025 series put the maths pass rate at 23.2%, and at 26.1% for 17-year-olds. If your child sat in November 2026, results come out on **Wednesday 13 January 2027**.

## Does my child have to resit GCSE maths?

No law forces a student to resit. The requirement is a condition attached to the funding a sixth form or college receives for every 16 to 19 year old. The Department for Education's 2026 to 2027 guidance on the maths and English condition of funding sets it out:

| Prior grade in maths | What a full-time student aged 16 to 18 must do in 2026/27 |
|---|---|
| Grade 3 | Study towards GCSE maths |
| Grade 2 or below | Study GCSE maths or Functional Skills Level 2 |
| Grade 4 or above | No requirement |

Full-time students must be offered at least 100 hours of maths teaching across the year, and the DfE encourages an extra 35 hours on top. In practice that's roughly three to four hours a week of maths at college.

Two details catch families out. First, the rule is about continuing to study, not about entering a particular exam series, so the college decides when your child sits the exam. Second, a grade 3 is the more restricted position: the DfE treats a 3 as close enough to a 4 that the GCSE is the required route.

You may also have read that resits are being scrapped. They aren't, at least not yet. The Curriculum and Assessment Review's final report (November 2025) recommended new one-year Level 1 "stepped" qualifications in maths and English for the lowest attainers, and the government has committed to them. But no first teaching date has been set, so the current rules apply for the whole of 2026/27.

## Why students keep failing GCSE maths (it's usually not ability)

When a student has failed twice, families assume the problem is maths itself. When we look at the actual papers, it rarely is. These are the common patterns.

**1. They underestimate the Foundation pass mark.** Most resit students sit Foundation tier, where the top grade is a 5. That's fine, because a 4 is the target. But a grade 4 on Foundation is not a low bar. On Pearson Edexcel's Foundation tier, a 4 needed 151 out of 240 in June 2026 (about 63%) and 143 out of 240 in November 2025, according to grade boundaries reported by Third Space Learning. Your child needs to bank almost two thirds of the marks.

**2. They're losing easy marks, not hard ones.** The same analysis found that 57% of the marks on the June 2026 Foundation papers came from standard procedural questions. Resit students often spend revision time on topics they find interesting or hard, then keep dropping marks on fractions, percentages, ratio and reading scales. Those marks decide a 3 or a 4.

**3. Nobody has looked at the paper-by-paper marks.** Every result comes with a mark for each paper. If a student did well on the two calculator papers and collapsed on the non-calculator one, the fix is arithmetic fluency, not more topics. Ask the college or school for these marks. They're the most useful diagnostic you'll get.

**4. The college class reteaches what they've already failed.** A resit class of 20 students, all with different gaps, usually starts the course again from the beginning. A student who is three marks short and one who is forty marks short need completely different plans.

**5. They've stopped believing it's possible.** After two attempts, many students sit the paper expecting to fail and give up on questions halfway through. Confidence affects exam performance, and it comes back through small wins, not pep talks.

## The summer 2027 dates you need

The summer 2027 timetable is final. For AQA and Pearson Edexcel GCSE maths:

| Paper | Date | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 (non-calculator) | Friday 14 May 2027, morning | 1h 30m |
| Paper 2 (calculator) | Thursday 27 May 2027, morning | 1h 30m |
| Paper 3 (calculator) | Monday 14 June 2027, morning | 1h 30m |
| Contingency day | Wednesday 23 June 2027 | Keep free |
| Results day | Thursday 19 August 2027 | |

OCR uses the same three dates but puts its non-calculator paper second, on 27 May. Check which board your child's college uses.

From mid-January, when November results arrive, to 14 May is about 17 weeks. From now, mid-November, it's about 26 weeks. Both are enough for a student who is close. Neither is enough if nothing changes.

## Resitting GCSE maths privately: when and how

If your child has left education, is home-schooled, or the college won't enter them for summer, they can sit the exam as a **private candidate** at an approved exam centre. The basics:

- **Deadline:** JCQ's entry deadline for exam boards is 21 February 2027, but centres set their own, earlier cut-offs. Published examples include 8 January 2027 (St Albans Independent College), 1 February 2027 (several London centres) and 15 February 2027 (Private Exams Centre). Treat January as your real deadline.
- **Cost:** the exam board's own fee is small (AQA's summer 2027 fee for GCSE maths is £52.85), but centres add their own charges. Published all-in fees range from about £250 to £545 per subject. Late entries cost more: Private Exams Centre lists £250 standard, £320 late and £395 very late for GCSE maths.
- **Access arrangements:** extra time or a reader must be requested when the entry is made, with evidence. Don't leave this to the last minute.
- **Over 19?** Adults without a grade 4 in maths can usually take a fully funded GCSE course, although an exam-only resit isn't covered.

## A 17-week resit plan that actually changes the result

This is the structure we use with [GCSE resit students](/subjects/gcse-resit-tutor/) who have one or two failed attempts behind them. It assumes one hour a week with a tutor on top of college classes. If you're wondering whether there's still time, see [how far before the exams is too late to get a tutor](/blog/how-far-before-exams-is-too-late-to-get-a-tutor/).

**Weeks 1 to 2: diagnose, don't teach.** Sit one full Foundation paper under timed conditions and mark it question by question. Sort every lost mark into three piles:

- "didn't know the method"
- "knew the method, made an error"
- "ran out of time or didn't attempt"

Most resit students are shocked by how big the second and third piles are.

**Weeks 3 to 8: secure the procedural marks.** Work through the most common Foundation topics until they're automatic: fractions, decimals and percentages, ratio and proportion, area and perimeter, angles, substitution, simple equations and reading charts. Short, frequent practice beats long sessions. Fifteen minutes a day on non-calculator arithmetic is the single highest-return habit we know.

**Weeks 9 to 13: add the reachable grade 4 and 5 topics.** Only now move on to topics such as simultaneous equations, Pythagoras, basic trigonometry and probability trees, chosen according to what the first paper showed.

**Weeks 14 to 17: exam conditions, every week.** One full paper a week, timed, marked and reviewed. Practise the order of attack: do every question you can first, then go back. Students who freeze on question 12 and never reach question 20 lose marks they actually knew.

**Throughout:** track the score on every past paper against the grade 4 boundary. Seeing 120, then 135, then 148 out of 240 is what rebuilds belief.

## What good resit tutoring looks like (and how to judge a tutor)

Sutton Trust polling published in 2026 found that 29% of secondary pupils in England and Wales have had private tutoring at some point, so you're not alone in considering it. Typical UK rates for GCSE tutoring are about £30 to £55 an hour, according to TutorCruncher's 2026 rate survey.

Whoever you choose, ask these questions on the first call:

- **Will you look at my child's paper-by-paper marks or a past paper before teaching anything?** If the answer is "we'll start from the beginning of the course", keep looking.
- **Do you know the board and tier?** A resit tutor should know the Foundation paper inside out, including the Edexcel or AQA question styles. Our [GCSE maths tutors](/subjects/gcse-maths-tutor/) are matched by board and tier.
- **How will we know it's working?** You want a past-paper score tracked against the grade boundary every few weeks, not a vague "they're doing well".
- **What do you do with a student who has given up?** The best resit tutors spend as much effort rebuilding confidence as teaching content.

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens if you fail GCSE maths?

If you're under 18 and in full-time education in England, your sixth form or college must keep you studying maths. With a grade 3 that means GCSE maths; with a 2 or below it can be GCSE or Functional Skills Level 2. Your other courses still go ahead.

### How many times can you resit GCSE maths?

There's no limit. You can resit in November or summer, at school, at college, or as a private candidate at any age.

### Can I resit GCSE maths privately in summer 2027?

Yes. Find an exam centre that accepts private candidates and enter before its deadline. Many close in January or early February 2027, ahead of JCQ's board deadline of 21 February 2027. Expect to pay about £250 to £545, including centre fees.

### When are the GCSE maths resits in 2027?

The summer papers are on 14 May, 27 May and 14 June 2027, with results on 19 August 2027. November 2026 resit results come out on 13 January 2027.

### Does a resit grade replace the original grade?

Both results stay on record, but colleges, universities and employers look at the highest grade. A 4 on a resit is what counts.

### Is Functional Skills Level 2 as good as GCSE maths?

It meets the funding rule for students with a grade 2 or below, and some employers and apprenticeships accept it. But some courses and universities specifically ask for GCSE, so check the requirement for your child's next step before choosing.
