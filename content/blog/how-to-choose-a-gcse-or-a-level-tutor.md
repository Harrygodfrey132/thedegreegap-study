Deciding your child needs a tutor takes about five minutes. Choosing one takes most families a fortnight of scrolling through profiles that all say roughly the same thing. That's because most tutor profiles are written to offend nobody, not to help you choose.

Here's what we'd look at, including the bits that don't flatter us.

## The safeguarding rule changed in January 2026

Start here, because most of the advice out there on this is now out of date.

**There is no legal requirement for a self-employed private tutor to hold a DBS check.** That hasn't changed, and it surprises a lot of parents.

What has changed is what you can ask for. Until this year, a self-employed tutor could only apply for a **basic** check. That shows unspent convictions and nothing else. An enhanced check goes further, and the barred list part tells you whether someone has been banned from working with children. But enhanced checks had to be requested by an organisation, so a tutor working for themselves just couldn't get one, however much they wanted to.

**Since 21 January 2026, self-employed workers can apply for Enhanced and Enhanced with Barred Lists checks themselves**, where the role qualifies. They apply through a DBS umbrella body, an organisation registered to process checks. It's a change in the law, and it's recent enough that plenty of tutoring sites still tell you it can't be done.

So the question to ask has changed too. Don't settle for "do you have a DBS?", because a yes might only mean a basic check. Ask this instead:

> "Do you have an enhanced DBS with barred list check, and can I see the certificate?"

Ask to see it, rather than just being told about it. The certificate shows a date and the level of check, and a tutor who has one won't mind at all.

## The three routes, and what each actually costs you

Families find a tutor in one of three ways. They differ less on quality than you'd think, and more on who carries the risk.

**Hiring directly**, through a personal recommendation or a local advert, is the cheapest per hour because there's nobody in the middle. But you do the vetting, the reference checks and the safeguarding yourself. If the match doesn't work, you're back to square one. If someone you trust has strongly recommended a tutor, this is often the right call, and we say so when families ring us.

**A marketplace** gives you a big searchable list and leaves the choosing to you. The list is the product. Vetting varies a lot from one platform to the next, so read what a site actually checks rather than assuming. And it's still down to you to judge how deep a tutor's subject knowledge goes.

**An agency** does the shortlisting and takes on the safeguarding, and charges for it. Here's the bit agencies tend to skate over: **we retain a margin on the hourly rate.** That's how we're paid. What it buys you is someone who's already interviewed the tutor and checked their references. If this one's wrong, they'll find you a different one on Monday.

None of the three is the right answer for everyone. It depends on whether you'd rather spend money or time, and on how confident you feel judging whether someone really knows their subject.

## What actually predicts a good tutor

Four things, in roughly this order.

**Exam board and specification, not just the subject.** Each exam board writes its own course for a subject, and your child's school picks one. That exact course is the specification. A tutor who teaches "GCSE Science" isn't the same as one who teaches AQA Combined Science. The second one knows which required practicals (the set experiments) come up, and how the six-mark questions are marked. It's the most common mismatch we see, and you can't spot it on a profile.

**Recent contact with the actual papers.** Mark schemes shift. A tutor who's marked, taught or sat the current course in the last couple of years knows where the marks are hiding. Someone brilliant who last looked at the syllabus in 2015 is working from a different exam.

**Whether they can explain, which is a separate skill from knowing.** The best mathematician in the room often isn't the best person to teach a Year 11 who's struggling. What you're paying for is someone who can find the exact point where your child's understanding broke, then explain it a second way, and a third. In our own interviews, this is where most applicants come unstuck. It's why only about three in every hundred who apply end up on the platform.

**The match with your particular child.** Your child might be quiet and a bit anxious. A brisk, demanding tutor can be excellent at the job and still be the wrong pairing for them. You can't judge this one from a profile at all, which is why the free introductory meeting matters more than the CV.

Notice what's not on that list. Once you're past a certain level, the name of the tutor's university is a weak guide. So is years of experience, beyond the first two or three.

## The questions worth asking before you book

Most parents ask about availability and price, and stop there. These are the questions that actually tell you something.

- **Which exam board, and have you taught this specification recently?** A vague answer here is the clearest early warning you'll get.
- **What would you do in the first session?** A good answer is about finding out what's going wrong. A weaker one starts at chapter one.
- **How do you know whether it's working?** You're looking for marked work and papers, not "they seem more confident."
- **Can I see an enhanced DBS certificate?**
- **What happens if my child doesn't get on with you?** Ask the tutor, and if you're going through an agency, ask the agency too. The two answers should match.
- **What do you expect between sessions?** An hour a week with nothing in between rarely moves a grade.

## What to watch in the first session

Nearly everyone offers a free introductory meeting. Use it to watch your child, not to interview the tutor.

**Does your child talk?** If they say three words in twenty minutes, that's telling you something. And it may be about the pairing rather than about them.

**Does the tutor ask more than they tell?** If the tutor spends most of the first session explaining, that's a bad sign. A useful first session is mostly the tutor working out what's wrong.

**Do they ask to see actual work?** A tutor who asks for a recent mock paper before deciding anything is doing the job properly. The mark on the front matters far less than which questions your child left blank.

**Then step back.** Sit in on the first one if you like. Most tutors are happy with that. After that, children usually open up more without a parent in the room, and you'll still see what was covered in the notes afterwards.

## How to tell in three sessions whether it is working

Don't wait a whole term. The signs show up well before that.

**By session three you should be able to name the problem.** Not "Maths", but "she loses marks on the multi-step problems at the back of the calculator paper." If nobody can tell you what's actually wrong yet, the tutor hasn't found it.

**There should be marked work.** Something your child has written, marked the way the exam board would mark it, with the reason for each mark explained. That's what separates tutoring from someone sitting beside them while they do their homework.

**Your child should be slightly less vague about the subject.** Not necessarily happier. Clearer.

What you shouldn't expect by session three is a better grade. In most cases that takes eight to twelve weekly sessions, and anyone promising it faster is selling you something.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a private tutor legally need a DBS check?**
No. There's no legal requirement for a self-employed private tutor to hold one. But since 21 January 2026, self-employed workers can apply for an Enhanced or Enhanced with Barred Lists check themselves, through a DBS umbrella body, where the role is eligible. So a tutor who wants one can now get one. Ask to see the certificate rather than taking it on trust.

**Is a more expensive tutor better?**
Not reliably. A tutor's rate tends to follow their experience and demand, not their results. And if your child needs patience more than depth, the most expensive tutor isn't automatically the right match. Judge them on how well they know the course, and on how the first session goes.

**Should I use a qualified teacher or a graduate?**
Both work, for different children. A qualified teacher brings classroom experience and a good sense of pace. A recent graduate who sat the current course often remembers the mark scheme more sharply, and your teenager may find them easier to talk to. The match matters more than the label.

**How do I know if my child needs one subject or three?**
Usually just one. When families get in touch wanting everything fixed at once, it's often worry talking rather than a clear picture of what's wrong. Three subjects at once is expensive, exhausting for your child and rarely necessary. Start with the subject that's furthest from where it needs to be.

**What if the first tutor isn't right?**
Say so early. Most families put up with a poor match for weeks out of politeness. That costs money, and it can put your child off the whole idea. A good agency will swap tutors without a fuss. If you hired directly it's an awkward conversation, but it's still worth having in week two rather than week ten.

**Can I sit in on sessions?**
Yes, and most tutors are glad to have you there for the first one. After that, most children engage more without an audience, and the notes after each lesson will keep you in the loop.
