Deciding your child needs a tutor takes about five minutes. Choosing one takes most families a fortnight of scrolling profiles that all say roughly the same thing, because most tutor profiles are written to be unobjectionable rather than useful.

Here is what we would look at, including the parts that do not flatter us.

## The safeguarding rule changed in January 2026

Start here, because most advice on this is now out of date.

**There is no legal requirement for a self-employed private tutor to hold a DBS check.** That has not changed and it surprises a lot of parents.

What has changed is what you can ask for. Until this year a self-employed tutor could only apply for a **basic** check, which shows unspent convictions and nothing else. Enhanced checks had to be requested by an organisation, so a tutor working for themselves simply could not get one, however much they wanted to.

**Since 21 January 2026, self-employed workers can apply for Enhanced and Enhanced with Barred Lists checks themselves**, through a DBS umbrella body, where the role qualifies. That is a legislative change and it is recent enough that plenty of tutoring sites still tell you it is impossible.

So the question to ask has changed. It is no longer "do you have a DBS?", which invites a yes that might mean a basic check. It is:

> "Do you have an enhanced DBS with barred list check, and can I see the certificate?"

Ask to see it rather than to be told about it. Certificates carry a date and a level, and a tutor who has one will not mind.

## The three routes, and what each actually costs you

There are three ways families reach a tutor. They differ less on quality than people assume and more on who carries the risk.

**Hiring directly**, through a personal recommendation or a local advert, is the cheapest per hour because there is nobody in the middle. You do the vetting, the reference checking and the safeguarding. If the match does not work, you start the search again from nothing. For a family with a strong recommendation from someone they trust, this is often the right call and we say so on calls.

**A marketplace** gives you a large searchable list and leaves the choosing to you. Volume is the product. Vetting varies a great deal between platforms, so read what a given site actually verifies rather than assuming, and the burden of judging subject depth stays with you.

**An agency** does the shortlisting and carries the safeguarding, and charges for it. Being straightforward about this, since it is the part agencies tend to skate over: **we retain a margin on the hourly rate.** That is how we are paid. What it buys is that somebody has already interviewed the tutor, checked the references, and will find you a different one on Monday if this one is wrong.

None of those three is the correct answer. The right one depends on whether you would rather spend money or spend time, and how confident you feel judging whether someone actually knows their subject.

## What actually predicts a good tutor

Four things, in roughly this order.

**Exam board and specification, not just the subject.** A tutor who teaches "GCSE Science" is not the same as one who teaches AQA Combined Science and knows which required practicals come up and how the six-mark questions are marked. This is the single most common mismatch we see, and it is invisible on a profile.

**Recent contact with the actual papers.** Mark schemes shift. A tutor who has marked, taught or sat the current specification within the last couple of years knows where the marks are hiding. Someone brilliant who last looked at the syllabus in 2015 is working from a different exam.

**Whether they can explain, which is a separate skill from knowing.** The best mathematician in the room is often not the best person to teach a struggling Year 11. What you are buying is the ability to find the exact point where the understanding broke, then explain it a second and third way. In our own interviews this is where most applicants come unstuck, and it is why roughly three in every hundred who apply end up on the platform.

**The match with your particular child.** A quiet, anxious student and a brisk, demanding tutor can both be excellent and still be a bad pairing. This is the one you cannot assess from a profile at all, which is why the free introductory meeting matters more than the CV.

Notice what is not on that list. University name is a weak predictor once you are past a certain point. So is years of experience, beyond the first two or three.

## The questions worth asking before you book

Most parents ask about availability and price, then stop. These are the ones that tell you something.

- **Which exam board, and have you taught this specification recently?** Vague answers here are the clearest early warning you will get.
- **What would you do in the first session?** A good answer involves finding out what is wrong. A weaker one involves starting at chapter one.
- **How do you know whether it is working?** You want marked work and papers, not "they seem more confident."
- **Can I see an enhanced DBS certificate?**
- **What happens if my child does not get on with you?** Ask the tutor and, if you are going through an agency, ask the agency. The answers should match.
- **What do you expect between sessions?** An hour a week with nothing in between rarely moves a grade.

## What to watch in the first session

Nearly everyone offers a free introductory meeting. Use it to watch your child rather than to interview the tutor.

**Does your child talk?** A student who says three words in twenty minutes is telling you something, and it may be about the pairing rather than about them.

**Does the tutor ask more than they tell?** First sessions that are mostly the tutor explaining are a bad sign. The useful ones are mostly diagnosis.

**Do they ask to see actual work?** A tutor who wants a recent mock paper before deciding anything is doing the job properly. The mark on the front matters far less than which questions were left blank.

**Then step back.** Sit in on the first one if you like, and most tutors are happy for that. After that, most students open up more without a parent in the room, and you will still see what was covered in the notes afterwards.

## How to tell in three sessions whether it is working

Do not wait a term. The signals arrive earlier than that.

**By session three you should be able to name the problem.** Not "Maths", but "she loses marks on the multi-step problems at the back of the calculator paper." If nobody can say what is actually wrong yet, the diagnosis has not happened.

**There should be marked work.** Something written, marked against the real scheme, with the reason for the mark explained. This is the thing that separates tutoring from supervised homework.

**Your child should be slightly less vague about the subject.** Not happier necessarily. Clearer.

What you should not expect by session three is a grade change. That takes eight to twelve weekly sessions in most cases, and anyone promising faster is selling you something.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a private tutor legally need a DBS check?**
No. There is no legal requirement for a self-employed private tutor to hold one. Since 21 January 2026, self-employed workers can apply for an Enhanced or Enhanced with Barred Lists check themselves through a DBS umbrella body where the role is eligible, so a tutor who wants one can now get one. Ask to see the certificate rather than taking it on trust.

**Is a more expensive tutor better?**
Not reliably. Rate tends to track experience and demand rather than results, and the most expensive tutor is not automatically the right match for a student who needs patience more than depth. Judge on specification knowledge and on how the first session goes.

**Should I use a qualified teacher or a graduate?**
Both work, for different students. A qualified teacher brings classroom experience and pacing. A recent graduate who sat the current specification often has sharper recall of the mark scheme and can be easier for a teenager to talk to. The match matters more than the category.

**How do I know if my child needs one subject or three?**
Usually one. Enquiries that arrive wanting everything fixed at once tend to come from panic rather than diagnosis, and three subjects at once is expensive, exhausting and rarely necessary. Start with the subject that is furthest from where it needs to be.

**What if the first tutor is not right?**
Say so early. Most families sit with a poor match for weeks out of politeness, which costs money and puts the student off the whole idea. A good agency will swap without a fuss. If you hired directly, it is an awkward conversation, and it is still worth having in week two rather than week ten.

**Can I sit in on sessions?**
Yes, and most tutors welcome it for the first one. Beyond that most students engage more without an audience, and post-session notes will keep you informed.
