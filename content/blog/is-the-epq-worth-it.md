The EPQ takes around 120 hours. That's a lot of time to carve out of Year 12 and Year 13, so it's fair to ask what your child gets back for it.

The honest answer is that it depends on where they apply. At some universities, a good EPQ grade is worth a full A-Level grade off the offer. At Oxford and Cambridge, it will never appear in the offer at all, although both say they value it. This guide sets out what the EPQ is, how universities treat it, what the research says, and how to decide. If you'd like to talk it through, [book a free 30-minute call](/book-a-call/).

## What the EPQ actually is

The Extended Project Qualification is a standalone level 3 qualification, worth half an A-Level, that students usually take alongside their A-Levels. They choose their own topic, have it approved by the school, and then plan, research and produce one of two things:

- **a written report of around 5,000 words**, often a mini-dissertation answering a research question, or
- **an artefact**, such as a piece of engineering, a computer program, an artwork or a performance, with a shorter written report.

Alongside the product, students keep a **production log** recording their planning, decisions and reflections, and they give a **presentation** to a non-specialist audience. AQA's total is 120 guided learning hours, and most of it is independent work.

The school marks the project and the exam board moderates it. A large share of the marks are for managing the project and reviewing it, not just for the essay itself. That's where many students lose marks they didn't know they could earn.

## What it's worth to universities

**UCAS points.** An EPQ carries up to 28 UCAS points: A* = 28, A = 24, B = 20, C = 16, D = 12, E = 8. That only matters for courses that make offers in points, and most selective universities make offers in grades.

**Alternative offers.** This is where the real value lies. Several universities make a second, lower offer to students taking the EPQ, typically one A-Level grade lower if the student gets a set grade in the EPQ. Southampton, Birmingham, Exeter and Bath have all used this approach, and some, such as Birmingham, now accept a B in the EPQ rather than an A.

Two things to watch. First, the rules vary by university and often by course, and many exclude medicine, dentistry or courses with fixed subject grades. Second, the reductions rarely stack: if your child already qualifies for a contextual offer, the EPQ may add nothing to it. Always check the specific course page for 2027 entry.

## What Oxford and Cambridge say

Both universities speak warmly about the EPQ, but neither will put it in an offer.

- **Oxford** says the EPQ will not be a condition of any offer, and encourages applicants to draw on their EPQ experience in the personal statement.
- **Cambridge** encourages applicants to take an EPQ because it builds independent research skills, but says completing it will not normally be a requirement of an offer.

For an Oxbridge applicant, then, the EPQ's value is as material. A well-chosen project gives your child something real to write about and to talk about at interview. Our [Oxford and Cambridge interview guide](/blog/oxford-cambridge-interview-preparation-2026/) explains why that depth matters. It won't lower the grades they need.

## How hard is it to get an A?

EPQ grades look generous compared with A-Levels: most AQA candidates reach a B or above. But fewer than half get an A or A*, and the EPQ is mostly taken by motivated students, so the figures aren't a like-for-like comparison with A-Levels. The practical point: an A in the EPQ is far from guaranteed, while a B is more within reach.

## Does it actually help at university?

The best evidence comes from Cambridge University Press & Assessment, which linked national school records to university records to compare EPQ and non-EPQ students with similar sixth form results. Students with an EPQ were more likely to go on to university, more likely to get a first or a 2:1, and slightly less likely to drop out.

The caveat matters. Students who choose to take an EPQ are likely to be more motivated and better supported than those who don't. The research controls for grades, not for motivation. So the EPQ is associated with better outcomes, but nobody can prove it causes them.

## Who should do it, and who shouldn't

It's likely to be worth it if your child:

- is applying to universities that make EPQ offers, where a good grade can take a grade off the offer
- wants to study an essay-based or research-heavy subject and can pick a topic linked to their degree
- is aiming for Oxford, Cambridge or another interview-based course and needs real depth to discuss
- is taking three A-Levels and has the capacity for one more serious commitment

It's probably not worth it if:

- **it would put their A-Level grades at risk.** A dropped A-Level grade costs far more than an EPQ gains
- they're applying mainly for medicine or dentistry at universities that ignore it
- they already qualify for a contextual offer that the EPQ wouldn't improve
- their school offers little supervision and they find long independent projects hard to manage without structure

## The timing trap

Most students finish the EPQ in Year 13 and get their grade on A-Level results day. So when universities make offers in the winter, they don't yet know the EPQ grade. That's why EPQ offers come in two parts: the standard offer, and a lower one that applies if you get the EPQ grade. Your child needs to hit both the reduced A-Level grades and the EPQ grade on the same day.

The EPQ also fits the new UCAS personal statement neatly. The statement is now structured around three questions, and the second asks how your qualifications and studies have helped you prepare for the course. A relevant EPQ is a ready-made answer, but only if the project is well under way by the autumn of Year 13. Our [UCAS deadline guide](/blog/ucas-deadline-2027/) covers the statement timeline.

## How to make the EPQ count

- **Link the topic to the degree.** A future law student might research a real legal reform; a medic might evaluate the evidence for a treatment. The project then pays off twice, in the offer and in the personal statement. Our guides to [A-Levels for medicine](/blog/a-levels-for-medicine/) and [A-Levels for law](/blog/a-levels-for-law/) cover those routes.
- **Treat the production log as half the job.** With so many marks for managing and reviewing the project, a thin log can cost a grade even when the essay is strong.
- **Set a narrow question.** "To what extent..." questions with a clear scope are easier to research and argue well in 5,000 words than broad topics.
- **Keep it their own work.** The project must be the student's own. Using AI tools or outside help without acknowledging it counts as malpractice under exam board rules.

## Not every student gets the chance

Research led by Oxford Brookes University found that only a minority of sixth form students take an EPQ, with uptake much higher in independent schools than in further education colleges, and higher in less deprived areas. If your child's school doesn't offer the EPQ, they're not alone, and Cambridge explicitly recognises that access is unequal.
