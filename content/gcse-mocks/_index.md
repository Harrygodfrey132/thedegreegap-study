Mocks are the closest thing to the real GCSEs your child will sit before the summer. They're set and marked by the school, nobody outside the school sees them, and they never appear on a certificate. But they still shape a few decisions that last well beyond the day the results come home.

This guide covers when mocks usually happen, how to read the result, and what to do next.

## When are GCSE mocks?

There's no national mocks timetable. Each school picks its own dates, so the school calendar is the place to check. But most follow the same pattern.

| Year group | When mocks usually happen | What they're for |
|---|---|---|
| Year 11 | Main mocks between November and January | Tier entry, sets, predicted grades, sixth form applications |
| Year 11 | Often a shorter second round in February or March | A last check before the real exams |
| Year 10 | End of the summer term, usually June or July | A first go at full papers, and early tier decisions |

So for this school year, a Year 11 will most likely sit mocks between November 2026 and January 2027. A Year 10 will sit theirs in summer 2027, then their Year 11 mocks the following winter.

### The real exams in 2027

| What | When |
|---|---|
| First GCSE exam | Monday 10 May 2027 |
| Last GCSE exam | Friday 18 June 2027 |
| Contingency day | Wednesday 23 June 2027 |
| GCSE results day | Thursday 19 August 2027 |

A November mock leaves roughly twenty teaching weeks before 10 May, once the holidays come out. A March mock leaves eight or nine. That's why an early mock, even a bad one, is useful. There's still time to teach something new.

## What a mock result actually tells you

The grade at the top of the paper is the school's best guess, made with grade boundaries from a past year. It's useful, but it's rough, for three reasons.

**Schools mark differently.** The same paper can come back a grade apart in two schools. Some mark harshly in November on purpose.

**Autumn papers are often cut down.** A Year 11 in November may have covered two thirds of the course. A grade 6 on two thirds of the course isn't the same as a grade 6 on all of it. It's worth asking the teacher which topics weren't on the paper.

**It measures exam conditions, not understanding.** A child who does well in class and badly in the mock usually has an exam technique problem rather than a knowledge one. That's good news. It's one of the quicker things to fix.

### What schools use mock results for

- **Tier entry in Maths and the Sciences.** Foundation papers top out at grade 5, so this is a decision with a ceiling attached, and it's usually made over the winter of Year 11.
- **Sets and groups.** Children often move up or down after mocks.
- **Predicted grades.** These matter for sixth form and college applications, and some sixth forms ask for a certain grade before your child can take a subject at A-Level.
- **What your child decides about themselves.** The one nobody measures. A Year 11 who gets a 3 in November and decides they're "just bad at Maths" can stop trying, and that does more damage than the mark itself.

## What to do in the week after

1. **Read the paper before reacting to the grade.** Sort the lost marks into three piles: questions left blank, questions started then abandoned, and questions finished but marked down. Each pile needs a different kind of help.
2. **Ask the subject teacher one question.** "If you had an hour a week with my child, what would you work on?" The answer is free and usually spot on.
3. **For Maths and the Sciences, ask about the tier.** Does this result change it, and when is the final decision made?
4. **Pick one subject to start with.** Not three. The one where the gap is biggest, or where the stakes are highest for next year.
5. **Keep the conversation calm.** Most teenagers already know it went badly. What helps is a plan, not a post-mortem.

We go into the three piles, and why a bad November mock is better news than it feels, in [our longer post on whether mock exams matter](/blog/do-mock-exams-matter/).

## Year 10 mocks: worth taking seriously?

Yes, but for a different reason. Year 10 mocks are often the first time your child sits a full paper under exam conditions, and the result is less important than what it shows about stamina and timing. It also tends to be the first evidence a school uses for tier conversations. A summer of gentle catching up on the topics that went missing makes Year 11 a lot calmer.
